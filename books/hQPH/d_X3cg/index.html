Fundamentals Are Where Systems Either Hold or Quietly Collapse

“The hardest systems do not fail because teams forgot components. They fail because teams never made the promises explicit.”

Before You Trust the Diagram

Part I taught you how to think before you draw: clarify the problem, understand the domain, map the customer journey, define capabilities, and structure architecture so responsibility does not disappear when the whiteboard is erased.

Part II is where that thinking meets pressure.

Dependencies slow down. Data arrives out of order. Queues grow. Caches lie at exactly the wrong moment. AI answers confidently, using outdated evidence. A retry that looked harmless in development multiplies the cost in production. A support team quietly becomes the real architecture because the system was never designed to recover honestly.

That is why this part is called System Design Fundamentals. Not because the material is basic. It is called fundamentals because these are the ideas that decide whether a system holds when reality stops being polite.

Senior engineers do not respect fundamentals because they are fashionable. They respect them because they have paid for ignoring them. Usually in production. Usually during a launch, a traffic spike, an audit, or a customer incident. Usually, when the diagram still looked reasonable, but the system no longer behaved in a way anyone could defend.

The Core Idea of Part II

Fundamentals are not implementation details. They are the mechanics that make your system promises believable under scale, latency, inconsistency, failure, and cost pressure.

Part II at a Glance

In Part II, you will learn how to

  • Treat scale as the pressure test that exposes hidden assumptions.
  • Design consistency as a trust contract, not merely a database choice.
  • Turn latency into an explicit budget across critical user and system paths.
  • Treat failure as system behavior, not an exception path.
  • Connect cost, sustainability, retries, caching, inference, and operational cleanup.
  • Explain trade-offs clearly in system design interviews and real architecture reviews.

Part II Promise

By the end of Part II, you will know how to turn architectural intent into operational guarantees. You will understand how scale exposes weak boundaries, how consistency protects trust, how latency reveals broken critical paths, how failure becomes system behavior, and how economics shapes every retry, cache, queue, model call, and recovery path. You will be able to explain not only how a system works, but what it guarantees when reality becomes messy.

Why These Fundamentals Are Not Basic

Most candidates treat fundamentals as topics to mention after drawing services: add a cache, use a queue, shard the database, add retries, make it eventually consistent, monitor it, scale it horizontally. Those are useful moves, but they are not architecture by themselves.

Architecture begins when you can explain what those moves protect and what they risk.

A cache protects latency but may weaken freshness. A queue protects availability but may create ordering ambiguity. Retries improve resilience but can create duplicate work and overload the dependency that is already failing. Eventual consistency helps scale, but can damage trust at a commitment point. AI assistance can reduce support load, but can also create fluent wrongness if the evidence, authority, and escalation boundaries are weak.

Fundamentals are where trade-offs stop being theoretical. They become customer experience, operational cost, support burden, and executive risk.

Interview Rule

Do not list a pattern until you can say which promise it protects and which new failure mode it introduces.

The DreamMart Thread Continues

DreamMart continues in Part II because e-commerce exposes architectural pressure quickly. The words on the screen look simple: in stock, delivery tomorrow, order confirmed, refund issued, address updated. Behind each phrase is a guarantee that must survive latency, stale data, partial failure, and human trust.

Customers do not care that your services are separated if checkout creates duplicate orders. They do not care that your refund workflow is asynchronous if nobody can explain why the refund was denied. They do not care that your AI assistant sounds confident if it promised something fulfillment cannot honor.

DreamMart teaches the central Part II lesson: discovery can be approximate, but commitment must be defensible. A search result can tolerate approximation. A recommendation can be probabilistic. A checkout confirmation, payment capture, shipment commitment, refund approval, or policy decision cannot casually be approximate. It must be correct enough, fresh enough, explainable enough, and recoverable enough for the promise being made.

The Mistake Readers Still Make After Part I

Even after learning to name capabilities and boundaries, most readers will still feel the old reflex: start drawing components. It feels productive. It fills the page. It gives the illusion that the design is moving.

But a diagram drawn before the guarantee is named often hides the hardest decision. Are you optimizing for speed or correctness? Freshness or availability? Automation or review? Customer convenience or auditability? Cost reduction or recoverability?

If you carry only one habit from Part I into Part II, make it this: delay the diagram until you can state the guarantees out loud.

Part II Rule

A queue, cache, database, model, or retry policy is not “good” in isolation. It is good only if it protects the right promise at the right moment without creating a worse failure somewhere else.

If You Read Part II Without Part I

You may be tempted to treat the chapters as a checklist: scale, consistency, latency, failure, cost. That will give you vocabulary but not judgment.

Part I gave you responsibility. Part II gives you the mechanics to defend it.

Without that connection, fundamentals become disconnected techniques. With that connection, they become architectural commitments. You stop saying, “Use a queue,” and start saying, “This workflow can be asynchronous because the customer-visible commitment does not require immediate finality, and we have idempotency, replay, and escalation for the cases where the queue falls behind.”

That is the difference between sounding experienced and designing responsibly.

Where Your Architecture Agreement Gets Tested

Part II is where teams discover whether they actually agreed on the architecture or merely agreed on a diagram.

What happens when the refund pipeline is correct but not explainable? What happens when the shipping ETA is timely but wrong? What happens when inventory is fresh in one system and stale in another? What happens when an AI assistant recommends an action that policy does not allow?

These questions reveal what the team truly optimized for: speed or auditability, freshness or consistency, customer trust or internal convenience, automation or controlled escalation.

Real architecture is not the diagram. It is the agreement behind the diagram: what the system guarantees, what it refuses to guarantee, and what it will do under pressure when those guarantees collide.

The Executive Question: Which One Saves the Most Money?

The uncomfortable answer is often the chapter that prevents silent failure and slow drift.

Outages are expensive because they are visible. Drift is expensive because it compounds quietly. Support load grows. Exceptions become normal. Manual overrides pile up. Every temporary workaround becomes a tax on the next release. The incident may last hours, but the operational compensation can last months.

If you fix only one thing, fix the conditions that force support, finance, operations, or compliance to carry guarantees that the architecture should have carried itself.

  • What can be stale, and what must be fresh?
  • Which system is the source of truth, and what happens when sources disagree?
  • Where do retries help, and where do they amplify damage?
  • Which failures are recoverable, and which must escalate or stop?
  • Where can AI assist safely, and where must it defer or refuse?
  • What evidence will exist when a customer, executive, auditor, or regulator asks, “Why did the system do that?”

  • Scale exposes assumptions that small systems hide.
  • Consistency is a trust contract, not only a storage decision.
  • Latency matters most at the moments where waiting changes trust.
  • Failure is behavior. Design the behavior before production designs it for you.
  • Cost is architecture multiplied by volume.
  • AI may assist, but evidence, policy, and ownership must decide what commits.
  • A system design is only as real as the guarantees it can defend.

6.1 Introduction

Most teams think they are designing an AI feature. What they are really designing is a new kind of behavior inside the system—behavior that can suggest, decide, act, defer, and escalate at machine speed. That shift changes what architecture means. In classic systems, architecture often takes the form of components, data stores, APIs, and integrations. In AI-enabled systems, architecture also becomes the shape of authority, commitment, time, evidence, and ownership.

Uncertainty is no longer a rare edge case. It is the operating condition. Evidence arrives late. Dependencies degrade quietly. Policies change. Models produce fluent answers that can hide doubt. Humans become the invisible layer of reconciliation when the system cannot defend its own actions. The risk is not only that the system goes down. The bigger risk is that the system stays up while shipping decisions that the organization cannot later explain.

Chapter 5 gave you ARCHVIEW as a way to make architecture visible. This chapter gives you the behavioral primitives that ARCHVIEW must expose. If ARCHVIEW is the structure for drawing accountable architecture, these primitives are the things you cannot leave invisible.

AI architecture is not about intelligence. It is about admissible behavior.

You are deciding what the system is allowed to make true, what it must refuse to do, and what evidence must exist whenever it commits an act.

Chapter 6 at a Glance

In this chapter, you will learn how to

  • Define the six behavioral primitives behind AI-enabled systems
  • Separate suggestion from action through authority boundaries
  • Identify the moment when a system stops advising and starts committing
  • Treat time as a policy surface across event time, ingestion time, and decision time
  • Design degradation paths that refuse, defer, or escalate instead of bluffing
  • Capture evidence so AI decisions can be replayed and defended
  • Define ownership and stop authority before production pressure exposes the gap

Chapter Promise

By the end of this chapter, you will know how to design AI-enabled behavior that can be defended under uncertainty. You will be able to state who can act, when the system is allowed to commit, which clock governs a decision, how the system degrades, what evidence must exist for replay, and who can stop or narrow automation when risk increases.

6.2 Authority: Who Is Allowed to Act, Not Just Call

Authority is the first primitive because it sets the blast radius of every other mistake. In AI-enabled systems, the most expensive failure rarely begins with a bad answer. It begins with a bad right. The common shortcut is familiar: give the agent broad permissions to make the demo work, then forget to unwind them. The assistant quietly becomes a superuser.

An agent is not simply a tool you call. It is a principle that can act. Once it can act, authority must be treated as architecture rather than configuration. The agent must operate inside the same authorization envelope as the role it represents. If it acts for a Sales Development Representative, it carries the Sales Development Representative role boundary. If it assists support, it carries support permissions. Anything broader is privilege escalation wearing a productivity badge.

Authority has three parts. Permissions answer what the agent can do. Licensing answers what capabilities it can invoke. Execution identity answers who did it in the audit trail. If you cannot name the acting principal, you cannot defend the action later.

In mature systems, authority is the boundary between suggestion and action. Suggestions are reversible: summarize, draft, recommend, explain. Actions commit state: create the order, approve the refund, send the email, change the status, and close the case. That boundary must be enforced with role scope, license scope, tool allowlists, and refusal lines.

As shown in Figure 6-2, you should separate what an AI system is allowed to suggest from what it is allowed to actually do. Suggestions and assistance belong in the outer ring because they are usually reversible. State-changing actions belong in the inner circle because they create real commitments. The authority gate controls when the system can move from advice to action by checking permissions, execution identity, required evidence, and refusal conditions. Making this boundary explicit helps you prevent an AI assistant from quietly becoming an autonomous actor without the controls needed to support that responsibility.

Figure 6-2

Authority Envelope-Suggest vs. Act. The outer zone contains reversible assistance. The inner zone contains state-changing actions. The authority envelope gates the movement from advice to action through permissions, license scope, execution identity, evidence, and refusal lines

6.3 Commitment: The Moment You Stop Suggesting and Start Owning

Commitment is the moment a system stops suggesting and starts owning. It is the line between usefulness and liability. A system can be uncertain and still be valuable until it crosses a commitment point. After that point, it is no longer producing output. It is producing consequences.

DreamMart makes this clear. At low volume, order states feel simple: created, confirmed, shipped. At scale, confirmed becomes a claim that can be false across boundaries. Inventory truth may lag across CRM, ERP, supplier, and warehouse flows. Without a reservation token or hold ID, in stock is not a defensible fact. It is a guess wearing a customer-facing label.

A strong design makes commitment explicit and boring. It defines which states are provisional, which states are committed, and which transitions require admissible evidence. Confirmed should map to a defensible condition: inventory reserved, payment authorized, partner acknowledged, policy version known, and exception approved when required.

When proof is missing, the system should not bluff. It should move to pending allocation, backorder, manual approval required, or another honest state. Commitment is not a label shown to the user. It is a contract the organization is prepared to defend.

As shown in Figure 6-3, you should treat AI outputs and system signals as provisional until you have enough evidence to make them a real commitment. A predicted inventory level, pending payment, or preliminary result may be useful, but it is not yet something you should allow the system to act on as fact. The move to committed truth should happen only after the required evidence—such as a reservation token, authorization, partner acknowledgment, policy version, or human approval—is present. If you skip that evidence gate, you risk turning an uncertain claim into a state-changing decision that is difficult to defend later.

Figure 6-3

Provisional Truth vs. Committed Truth. Provisional states are useful but uncertain. Committed states require proof such as reservation tokens, payment authorization, partner acknowledgments, policy versions, and human approval when needed

6.4 Time: Which Clock Wins, and Where That Rule Is Written

Time is not a detail. Time is a policy surface. Distributed systems rarely have one timestamp. They have competing clocks: event time, ingestion time, and decision time. If you do not explicitly declare which clock governs each capability, the system will implicitly choose whichever timestamp is easiest to implement or arrives first.

DreamMart is full of deadlines wearing friendly labels: returns, cancellations, fulfillment cutoffs, delivery attempts, and refund eligibility. Is the return window based on order date, ship date, delivery date, or first delivery attempt? If the answer is not written into the architecture, customer fairness becomes a support negotiation.

AI agents make time more important because automation removes human delay. Humans naturally add hesitation and context. Agents do not unless you design it. Once an agent can send emails, issue refunds, modify orders, or change status, decision time accelerates into minutes what once took hours.

The Sales Development Representative (SDR) agent example shows time as architecture. The rule is not simply to send emails. It is: do not send after 6 PM local time, batch at 1 PM, allow three to four hours for rep review, hold one hour for human intervention before sending, and push post-6 PM leads to the next day. Those are not preferences. They are time guardrails that make AI behavior respectful, predictable, and defensible.

As shown in Figure 6-4, you should make time an explicit part of the design instead of relying on whatever timestamp happens to be easiest to use. Separate when the event actually happened, when your system learned about it, and when the system made a decision. Then define which of those clocks governs each action. This matters because late-arriving data, stale information, or the wrong clock can cause a system to make a technically valid but operationally incorrect decision. A visible time policy helps you reason about freshness, review windows, deferrals, and postmortems with much more confidence.

Figure 6-4

Time Policy as a First-Class Design Artifact. The time policy separates event time, ingestion time, and decision time, then declares which clock governs each capability. It also shows human-aligned guardrails such as review windows, send windows, and next-day deferral

6.5 Degradation: The Refusal Lines That Keep You Honest

Degradation is not simply running slower. Degradation is what the system chooses to do when it cannot keep its promise. Many systems have only two modes: succeed or crash. Under pressure, that binary creates improvisation: retry storms, partial writes, hidden overrides, and manual workarounds that keep the uptime graph green while the system violates its own contract.

A mature AI system has refusal lines by domain, risk, and evidence. In DreamMart, a refund above a threshold for a new customer should not be fully automated. In healthcare, an AI may summarize but must not automatically prescribe treatment. In finance, if evidence is incomplete at the time of decision, the system should not bluff a clean approval or denial. It should move to pending review with missing evidence made explicit.

The rule is simple: when certainty drops, system power must drop with it. Degradation keeps commitments defensible, authority bounded, and evidence captured even as capability narrows. It is a controlled descent, not a free fall.

Figure 6-5 illustrates how you can reduce system capability in a controlled way when confidence, evidence, or system health begins to weaken. Instead of jumping directly from normal operation to complete failure, you can move through defined stages: full capability, degraded safe defaults, pending human review, and finally a hard stop. This gives you a practical way to preserve safety while keeping as much useful functionality as possible. The key is that authority remains bounded, evidence continues to be captured, and the system knows when it should stop acting autonomously.

Figure 6-5

Degradation Ladder. The system descends from Normal to Degraded, Safe Defaults to Pending Review, and to Hard Stop. Across every rung, commitment stays defensible, evidence stays captured, and authority stays bounded

6.6 Evidence and Replay: If Legal Asks Why, What Must Exist?

Evidence is the primitive that prevents governance theater. Most organizations can tell a story about what happened. Fewer can produce proof. In AI-enabled systems, that gap matters because fluent output can hide uncertainty while the system makes real commitments: emails sent, refunds issued, orders changed, cases closed.

The failure pattern is familiar. A customer escalates, or an auditor asks why an action occurred. The logs show API calls but not intent. The transcript shows text but not authority. The model output is preserved, but the policies that constrained it are gone. Teams argue about what the agent must have seen. That is not accountability. It is folklore.

The minimum evidence set at commitment includes a correlation ID, capability, decision mode, acting principal, on-behalf-of role, policy, and guardrail versions, evidence sources, tool calls, commit token, customer-visible outcome, and reversal link. Guardrails evolve in production, so versioning is non-negotiable. You cannot explain yesterday’s action using today’s rules.

Figure 6-6 gives you a practical way to make AI decisions replayable instead of opaque. The Decision Replay Card captures the minimum evidence you need to reconstruct a decision later: which tools were called, which evidence sources were used, what context influenced the outcome, and where human approval or reversal occurred. This is what turns a simple activity log into defensible evidence. If a decision is challenged later, you should be able to explain not only what the system did, but why it did it and what proof supported the action.

Figure 6-6

Decision Replay Card. The Decision Replay Card records the minimum evidence package needed to reconstruct an AI-involved decision: who acted, under which rules, using what evidence, through which tools, and with what proof that the commitment was valid

6.7 Ownership: Who Can Stop the System, and What Triggers That Stop

Ownership is the primitive that keeps architecture from drifting into politics. We own it, but it is not ownership. Ownership means someone can change behavior under pressure without waiting for a crisis meeting or cross-functional debate.

AI systems need a stop authority. Feedback trickles in: thumbs-down ratings, escalations, confusing answers, disputed refunds, customers saying the agent promised them. Everyone sees it, but no one can act. Product wants conversion. Legal wants protection. Support wants relief. Engineering wants time. If the only response is discussion, the system does not have ownership. It has a delay with the dashboard.

Stop conditions must be measurable: disputed actions above the threshold, escalation rate above the baseline, tool failures above the budget, cost per action above the cap, replay coverage below 100% for high-risk actions, or policy violations more than N times per day. The response must be scoped: pause, degrade, defer, throttle, or hard stop by capability, tenant, region, cohort, or tool path.

Figure 6-7 shows how you turn ownership from a governance concept into an operational control. You monitor signals such as incidents, disputes, tool failures, cost, and replay coverage; when a threshold is breached, a designated capability owner can pause, restrict, throttle, or stop the behavior. The important part is that re-enabling the capability is also explicit: you review the evidence, fix the root cause, and confirm that the defined criteria have been met. This gives you a practical way to ensure that someone has both the responsibility and the authority to stop unsafe or unreliable behavior before the blast radius grows.

Figure 6-7

6.8 A Design-Review Walkthrough: One Capability, All Six Primitives

If you want to know whether these primitives are real, do not debate them in the abstract. Pick one capability and make the answers concrete. The moment you do that, vague AI ambition becomes architecture.

Take the DreamMart order modification. It looks like a simple update: change the address, change the quantity, or swap an item. At scale, it touches fulfillment cutoff, fraud posture, settlement timing, warehouse labels, and carrier handoff. The friendly UI says Edit, but the system is entering a junction of commitments.

Start with authority: who is authorized to approve the modification, and does the agent operate only within that role? Then, commitment: what state must exist before the system can say “modified”? Then, at what time does the clock govern for the cutoff? Then degradation: what happens when the promise cannot be kept? Then, evidence: can the decision be replayed end-to-end? Finally, ownership: if disputes spike, who can pause automation today?

This walkthrough exposes the difference between what we built and how we can defend it. Teams often discover that they have implementation details but little declared truth: logs without replay, permissions without envelopes, states without commitments, and escalation paths without stop authority.

Table

This table gives a design walkthrough for DreamMart order modification using the six AI architecture primitives. It shows how each primitive turns a risky “AI can modify an order” idea into a defensible system design.

For Authority, the key question is: Who can approve the change? The design answer is that the agent acts only as a support representative, not as an admin. It has a strict tool allowlist: it can read CRM data, create a modification request, request an ERP hold, apply an action, and, when allowed, invalidate a WMS label. This prevents the AI from gaining hidden administrative power.

For Commitment, the question is: When can the system determine that the order has been modified? The answer is: only after the ERP acknowledgment token exists and a shipping label already exists is the label invalidation confirmed. This prevents the system from making a customer-visible promise before downstream systems have accepted the change.

For Time, the question is: Which clock governs the fulfillment cutoff? The design separates decision time from event time. Decision time is used for automation, while event time is preserved for fairness review and disputes. This matters because an order may cross a cutoff while systems are delayed or callbacks arrive late.

For Degradation, the question is: What happens if ERP or WMS is slow? The system does not pretend that the modification succeeded. It moves the request to pending review, defers customer-facing finality, and escalates if the shipment has already been released. This gives the system a safe way to handle uncertainty.

For Evidence, the question is: Can the decision be replayed? The answer is yes: the replay card includes the correlation ID, envelope version, policy version, tool calls, evidence snapshots, and commit token. This allows support, operations, or auditors to reconstruct why the system behaved as it did.

For Ownership, the question is: Who can stop the behavior? The capability owner can shift the agent from acting to assisting by tenant, region, or tool path when disputes, tool failures, or replay gaps exceed thresholds. This ensures that risky behavior can be narrowed or stopped before it creates wider harm.

Figure 6-8 brings the six AI architecture primitives together into a practical design review you can use before an AI-enabled capability goes live. For each capability, you should be able to state who has authority to act, what evidence makes a decision committed, which clock governs the action, how the system degrades when confidence or signals weaken, what replay evidence must be captured, and who has the authority to pause or stop the behavior. Using the review this way helps you move from abstract governance principles to concrete architecture decisions that can be tested, operated, and defended in production.

6.10 Worked Example: DreamMart Order Modification

Order modification looks simple until it hits scale. CRM may say one thing, ERP another, the warehouse may already have printed the label, and the carrier may be about to take custody. This single capability forces every primitive into the open.

Authority: the agent acts only as a Support Rep and uses a strict tool allowlist. Commitment: Accepted is provisional; Modified requires ERP acknowledgment and label invalidation if needed. Time: the fulfillment cutoff is a testable rule. Degradation: if ERP is slow or evidence is incomplete, the system shifts to pending review. Evidence: the replay card captures correlation ID, envelope version, policy version, tool calls, and commit token. Ownership: if disputes spike or replay coverage drops, the capability owner can switch from act to assist within a scoped blast radius.

Table

This table turns stop authority into concrete operating behavior. Each stop threshold is tied to a specific action. If replay coverage drops below 100% for high-risk modifications, state-changing automation stops, and the system falls back to summaries only. If ERP tool failures exceed the allowed budget, the capability degrades to pending review and suppresses final customer confirmation. If disputed modifications exceed the threshold, the system shifts from acting to assisting the affected tenant, region, or cohort. If the shipment has already been released or the carrier handoff is confirmed, the automated modification is refused, and the case escalates with a full evidence package. The purpose is not to stop AI broadly; it is to narrow authority precisely when guarantees, evidence, or downstream control can no longer be trusted.

6.11 How to Use the Primitives in an Interview

You do not need to announce a formal framework. Use the primitives to order your thinking and sound senior under pressure.

What to Say in the Interview

“For any AI-involved state-changing capability, I would define the six primitives explicitly. First, who is allowed to act and under which role envelope? Second, where does the system cross from suggestion into commitment? Third, which clock governs the decision? Fourth, how does the system degrade when evidence is incomplete? Fifth, what replay evidence is captured? Sixth, who can pause or narrow automation if thresholds fire? That keeps the design accountable, not just intelligent.”

6.12 Chapter Summary

  • AI architecture becomes dangerous when behavior is left implicit.
  • Authority defines who may act and prevents assistants from becoming superusers.
  • Commitment defines when output becomes a consequence that the business must defend.
  • Time defines which clock governs eligibility, cutoffs, and automation behavior.
  • Degradation describes how the system narrows its power as certainty drops.
  • Evidence and replay turn governance from storytelling into proof.
  • Ownership gives someone the authority to stop, narrow, or re-enable behavior under pressure.

6.13 Architect’s Reflection

If you want to know whether you are designing an AI system or just shipping a demo, ask the question that forces a refusal line: what is the most dangerous permission you granted temporarily, and is it still there?

Ask the question that forces a commitment definition: which single state flag, token, or label, if wrong, would become your worst trust breach?

Ask the question that forces time to become testable: when event time and decision time disagree, which clock wins, and where is that rule written?

Then ask the question that separates confident architecture from accountable architecture: if a customer, executive, or regulator asked six months from now, Why did the system allow this?, would you produce a decision replay or a debate?

These primitives are not an extra ceremony. They are the difference between building something that merely functions and building something you can still defend after reality has had its turn.

6.14 Transition to Chapter 7

The primitives in this chapter define admissible AI behavior. Chapter 7 moves into trust, safety, and LLM-SHIELD: the practical controls that keep prompts, tools, retrieval, escalation, and policy boundaries from becoming attack surfaces. If Chapter 6 answers what the system is allowed to do, Chapter 7 asks how to protect those boundaries when users, data, tools, and models behave unpredictably.

6.15 Interview Practice and Assignments

Use the following questions to test whether you can design AI-enabled behavior that remains accountable under uncertainty. A strong AI system design answer does not merely say “add an agent” or “call an LLM.” It defines who may act, when the system commits, which clock governs the decision, how the system degrades, what evidence is preserved, and who can stop or narrow automation when risk increases.

1. Six-Primitives Warm-Up Question

DreamMart wants to add an AI assistant that can help customers modify orders, answer refund questions, recommend products, and escalate issues to support.

Before designing the services or model calls, apply the six AI architecture primitives.

For each primitive, answer one design question:

  • Authority: Who is allowed to act, and under which role?
  • Commitment: When does the assistant’s output become a business consequence?
  • Time: Which clock governs eligibility, cutoff, or policy decisions?
  • Degradation: What happens when evidence is missing or confidence drops?
  • Evidence and Replay: What must be captured to defend the decision later?
  • Ownership: Who can pause, narrow, or stop the automation?

Explain why these primitives should be defined before implementation begins.

2. Authority Boundary Question

A team gives a customer-support AI agent broad permissions so it can quickly resolve cases, issue refunds, modify orders, send emails, and close tickets.

Analyze this design using the Authority primitive.

Your answer should include:

  • The difference between calling a tool and being allowed to act
  • The represented human role the agent should operate under
  • Which actions should be suggest-only
  • Which actions may be allowed under strict gates
  • Which actions should be forbidden
  • How tool allowlists, data scope, role scope, and execution identity should be enforced
  • What audit trail should show who or what acted

3. Commitment Question

DreamMart’s AI assistant tells a customer, “Your order has been modified,” after sending a request to the ERP system. Later, the warehouse rejects the change because the shipping label was already printed.

Analyze this failure using the Commitment primitive.

Your answer should address:

  • Why “modified” is a committed claim, not just a message
  • Which states should be provisional versus committed
  • What evidence is required before the system can say “modified”
  • What customer-visible state should be shown while confirmation is pending
  • How the system should handle ERP delay, WMS delay, or carrier handoff
  • What replay record should prove whether the commitment was valid

4. Time Policy Question

DreamMart allows customers to cancel or modify an order before fulfillment cutoff. The order event was created at 4:55 PM, ingested by the AI workflow at 5:03 PM, and evaluated by the decision engine at 5:07 PM. The warehouse cutoff is 5:00 PM.

Which clock should govern the decision?

Your answer should include:

  • The difference between event time, ingestion time, and decision time
  • Which clock should govern automation
  • Which clock should be preserved for fairness review
  • Where the time policy should be written
  • How the system should behave when clocks disagree
  • How this decision should appear in logs, replay records, and customer messaging

5. Degradation and Refusal-Line Question

A refund AI agent normally approves low-risk refunds automatically. During a production incident, policy retrieval becomes stale, customer history is incomplete, and the payment gateway is responding slowly.

Design the degradation behavior.

Your answer should include:

  • Which actions should continue normally
  • Which actions should move from automated to assistive
  • Which actions should move to pending review
  • Which actions should hard stop
  • What the customer should see
  • What support agents should see
  • What evidence should still be captured
  • Why a safe refusal is better than a confident but unsupported answer

6. Evidence and Replay Question

Six months after an AI-assisted refund, a customer, executive, or auditor asks, “Why did the system allow this refund?”

Design the minimum Decision Replay Card needed to answer that question.

Your answer should include:

  • Correlation ID
  • Capability name
  • Decision mode
  • Acting principal and represented role
  • Authority envelope version
  • Policy and guardrail versions
  • Evidence sources used
  • Tool calls made
  • Commit token or proof of outcome
  • Customer-visible result
  • Reversal or remediation link

Explain why logs and transcripts alone are not enough.

7. Ownership and Stop Authority Question

DreamMart notices that AI-assisted order modifications are producing more customer disputes in one region. Support complaints are rising, ERP tool failures are above budget, and replay coverage is below 100% for high-risk actions.

Design the Stop Authority SOP.

Your answer should include:

  • Who owns the capability
  • Which signals should be monitored
  • Which thresholds should trigger action
  • Whether the response should pause, degrade, defer, throttle, or hard stop
  • How to scope the response by tenant, region, cohort, or tool path
  • What criteria are required before automation is re-enabled
  • Why ownership without stop authority is not real ownership

8. Senior Architect Design-Review Question

You are reviewing an AI-enabled order modification capability. The team says:

“The agent checks the customer request, calls the ERP, updates the order, and messages the customer. We also log everything.”

Use the six primitives to challenge and improve this design.

Your answer should include:

  • The authority envelope for the agent
  • The exact commitment point
  • The governing clock for fulfillment cutoff
  • The degradation ladder for ERP, WMS, or carrier delays
  • The Decision Replay Card fields
  • The stop authority and measurable triggers
  • One design decision you would defend strongly in an architecture review
  • One design decision you would keep flexible as the system matures
