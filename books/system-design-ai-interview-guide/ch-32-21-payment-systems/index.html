21.1 Introduction

Payments are often described as the movement of money. That description is convenient and wrong in the ways that matter most to architects. A payment system is a commitment engine operating within a landscape of delayed truth, partial authority, and conflicting observations. The merchant sees one state. The processor sees another. The card network, the issuer, the fraud engine, the customer, and your own ledger each hold a different slice of reality, and they do not receive it simultaneously. The system is still expected to act. The moment it acts, it creates obligations that may need to be justified long after the request itself is gone.

That is why payments feel different from most other system design problems. In many distributed systems, failure is loud. A request times out. A transaction aborts. A job crashes and gets retried. In payments, the more dangerous failures are often quiet. Services stay up. Success dashboards stay green. Throughput looks healthy enough to avoid panic. And yet the system double-captures, loses a refund, denies something it should have approved, or holds money it can no longer justify holding. Nothing looks broken in the moment. The damage appears later, when finance cannot reconcile, support cannot explain, or a customer dispute turns a vague internal story into a hard external question.

That question is brutally simple: prove why the system did what it did.

Everything in this chapter starts there. Not with providers. Not with protocols. Not with the usual checklist of gateways, ledgers, and webhooks. Those matter, but they are not the first design truth. The first truth is that every meaningful payment action crosses a boundary between attempt and liability. Before that boundary, the system is trying. After that boundary, the system owes an explanation. Senior payment architecture is the discipline of drawing that line clearly, naming the tokens that cross it, pinning the policy and evidence that authorized it, and making replay possible when time, retries, or partial failure try to blur the record.

If the answer to “why did we capture, refund, deny, or hold?” is a story, you do not have architecture. You have narration.

This chapter treats payments the way production eventually forces you to treat them: as a system of commitments, proofs, bounded ambiguity, and controlled recovery. We will look at where liability actually begins, why idempotency matters more than the exactly-once mythology, how to operate safely with ambiguous outcomes, and what it means to build a payment flow that can withstand scrutiny weeks later. Because payments rarely fail when a service goes down. They fail when promises outrun proof.

21.4 Chapter 21 at a Glance

  • Design payment systems as liability commitment engines, not money-movement pipelines.

  • Separate intent, authorization, capture, settlement, refund, denial, payout hold, and dispute handling.

  • Use tokens such as paymentIntentId, authId, effectId, captureId, refundId, refundDenyId, payoutHoldId, disputeId, and responseId.

    21.5 Why Payments Feel Different + Your Three Non-Negotiable Guarantees

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is the same Part III guarantee pattern applied to payments. Before discussing gateways, ledgers, queues, or providers, we name what the system must protect under pressure: defensible liability, bounded duplicates and ambiguity, and replayable side effects.

    Payments feel different because they live at the intersection of time, authority, and irreversibility. In most distributed systems, failure is visible. In payments, the more dangerous failures often arrive wearing the clothes of success. The bank approves, but your acknowledgment is lost. The capture succeeds, but your service restarts before the ledger is updated. The refund is accepted, but the settlement lags behind the customer-facing status. A dispute arises weeks later, forcing the platform to defend a decision made on older evidence and under a different policy bundle.

    That drift is what makes payment architecture hard. The challenge is not just moving messages between systems. It is preserving business truth while the systems involved disagree about what happened, when it happened, and whether it is final. Senior payment design does not begin with a provider comparison, a gateway integration, or a protocol diagram. It begins with guarantees.

    21.6 Tokens and Boundaries: Name the Moment You Stop Attempting and Start Owning

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is the commitment primitive and Chapter 10’s consistency lesson applied to payments. Attempted is not the same as captured, and processed is not the same as proven.

    Payments become dangerous the moment the system stops describing what it tried and starts asserting what is true. We attempted to capture is recoverable. We captured is a liability. We initiated a refund as part of the workflow. We denied a refund, which is a customer outcome you may be forced to defend later. The fastest way for payment systems to become indefensible is by letting those states blur.

    Senior payment design draws one hard line and refuses to let anything cross it without proof: the side-effect boundary. On one side, the system is validating, evaluating, and attempting. On the other hand, it claims that money was moved, a refund was denied, or a payout was held. That is the moment the platform stops trying and starts owing explanation.

    The way you make that line real is with explicit tokens. paymentIntentId names the intent to pay. authId records provisional permission. The real shift happens with liability tokens such as captureId, refundId, refundDenyId, or payoutHoldId. Underneath them sits the token that makes distributed systems survivable: effectId. This is the identity of the side effect itself, for example, capture(orderId, attempt=1). Messages may replay. Acks may vanish. Consumers may restart. Without a durable effectId, retries can manufacture a new reality. With it, retries re-encounter an existing claim and must respect it.

    paymentIntentId and authId describe permission to try. effectId protects the side effect. captureId, refundId, refundDenyId, and payoutHoldId are liability claims the platform must defend. Authorization gives the platform permission to try. Capture gives the platform something to defend.

    This figure makes the payment side-effect boundary visible: validation and authorization are still pre-liability, while capture, refund, denial, and payout hold require durable idempotency, policy pinning, and replay proof.

    Figure 21-3 separates the Attempt, Boundary, and Liability phases. The Attempt phase validates the request, applies risk and policy checks, and mints pre-liability identifiers such as paymentIntentId and authId. The boundary checks whether proof exists, computes effectId, consults the idempotency store, pins the policy, and mints the liability token. Only then does the Liability phase execute the side effect, write the durable state, and emit the Decision Replay Card. The figure’s core message is that irreversible monetary actions require proof before execution, not explanation after damage.

    This Table 21-2 is the second half of the Liability Action Matrix. It tells the reader how each payment action should behave under retries, when the system must stop, and what minimum proof must be preserved in the replay card.

    The table’s central message is that not every payment action warrants the same retry behavior or stop posture. Authorization, capture, refund, refund denial, payout hold, and chargeback response all carry different liability, so each needs its own operational rule.

    Authorize is relatively reversible and expires naturally. The system can retry a few times with jitter, then defer. If uncertainty remains, it can escalate to the user, offer an alternative payment method, or route to human assistance. The replay card must preserve the payment intent, authorization ID, policy version, evidence timestamps, provider response, and decision reason codes.

    Capture is the real money-moving liability boundary. The retry budget is much tighter. If capture cannot be proven quickly, the system should freeze capture for that intent and route to review or pending-capture status. The replay card must include the payment intent, auth ID, capture ID, effect ID, policy version, ledger state, provider request/response IDs, outcome, and compensation pointer.

    Void is time-sensitive because authorization reversals only work within a limited window. The system can retry within that auth window, but if risk or state conflicts appear, it should step up or route to human review. The replay record must preserve auth freshness and provider outcome.

    Refund is a high-liability money movement. It can retry briefly, but after that, it should move to case handling. If refund issues spike, the system can freeze refunds for a cohort or capability. The replay card must preserve settlement proof, reason code, returns/dispute linkage, outcome, and reversal window.

    Refund denial is a customer-trust liability action. It should not loop. One immediate decision is allowed only when the proof is strong. If uncertainty exists, the system should ask for more proof, step up, route to human review, or freeze denials during spikes. The replay card must preserve the evidence snapshot, reason codes, appeal path, and acting principal or mode.

    Payout hold affects seller’s livelihood, so it must be tightly governed. The system should avoid repeated automated holds and route quickly to case handling. If reversal spikes occur, it should freeze holds or shift to step-up. The replay card must include KYC or identity state, risk signals, reason codes, release criteria, and appeal path.

    Chargeback response is legal and audit-sensitive. It must happen within SLA windows, but high-value cases should go to human review, and automation should freeze if replay gaps exist. The replay card must include the dispute ID, response ID, evidence pack hash, submission timestamps, network or acquirer references, and outcome tracking.

    The key message is: retry budgets protect the system from recursion, stop postures protect the business from unsafe liability, and replay fields protect the platform from future disputes. This table turns payment correctness into an executable operating contract.

    21.8 Ledger Truth + Delayed Truth: Reconciliation Is the Price of Reality

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is the Time primitive, Chapter 10 consistency, and Chapter 11 latency operating together. Payment truth arrives in lanes, and the ledger records what the platform committed before external truth fully settles.

    In payments, truth is never a single row in a single database. It is an agreement assembled across systems that were never designed to see the world at the same moment or from the same vantage point. Your application has one view. Your ledger has another. The processor, card network, issuer, settlement rail, and eventually the dispute system each add their own version of events, often later and sometimes in conflict. The only truth you truly own is the truth you can prove you recorded when you crossed a liability boundary. That is what the ledger is for. Not just accounting. Not just balances. The ledger is the platform’s courtroom memory.

    Everything else arrives on its own clock. Authorization can look final and still be reversed. Capture can succeed while the acknowledgment disappears. Settlement can lag hours or days behind the customer-facing moment. A chargeback can reopen a decision weeks later. This is why reconciliation is not back-office cleanup. It is correctness recovery.

    The design rule is simple: commit what you can prove now, reconcile what you can only confirm later, and never let delayed truth trick the system into inventing a second reality.

    21.9 Idempotency and Ordering: Scoped Truth, Not Global Guarantees

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is Chapter 10 consistency and Chapter 17 side-effect idempotency applied to payments. Ordering is scoped to the business entity, and idempotency belongs at the money-changing side-effect boundary.

    Payments do not need global ordering. They need a scoped truth: ordering only where the business becomes ambiguous without it. Did we capture before we refunded? matters per orderId. Did we deny a refund after already approving it? matters per refundRequestId. Did we pay out twice? matters per merchantId. If you chase global ordering, you will pay for latency, availability, and complexity to protect a fiction.

    Idempotency is the non-negotiable partner to scoped ordering. Delivery will repeat. Retries will cross deploys. Acknowledgments will vanish. Providers will time out after succeeding. Your job is not to stop duplicates from existing in the transport. Your job is to make duplicates harmless where money becomes liability. That means durable idempotency for irreversible actions such as capture, refund, refund denial, and payout hold. Redis may help with hot dedupe windows, but it cannot be the truth for money.

    The practical pattern is simple. Compute an effectId that names the side effect, not just the event. Check the durable store. Write IN_PROGRESS before execution. Execute once. Then write COMMITTED and emit the replay receipt. Every other exactly-once story is marketing. This is how payment systems survive retries, replays, and partial failure without manufacturing duplicate money.

    This table explains where ordering is actually required in a payment system. The key lesson is that payments do not need global ordering everywhere. They need scoped ordering only where the business truth would become ambiguous without it.

    For orderId, ordering is needed per order. Capture, refund, void, and cancellation must occur in the correct sequence. For example, a refund should not be processed before capture exists, and a cancellation should not conflict with a completed capture.

    For paymentIntentId, ordering is needed per payment intent. Authorization, capture, retry, and reconciliation all belong to the same payment story. The system must preserve the causal state so it does not treat an old retry or delayed provider response as a new truth.

    For merchantId, ordering is sometimes needed per payout batch. Payout holds, releases, and corrections must avoid duplicate seller outcomes. The system may not need strict ordering across all merchant activity, but it does need control where payout obligations are grouped.

    For refundRequestId, ordering is needed per refund request. Refund approval, denial, appeal, and reversal must not conflict. Otherwise, the platform can create a customer trust failure: one part of the system says “refund denied,” while another says “refund approved.”

    For analytics/reporting, strict ordering is usually not required. Late events can be corrected through windows, reconciliation, and adjustment logic. Reporting needs an accurate final truth, not necessarily a globally ordered arrival.

    The key message is to pay for ordering only where business truth requires it. In payments, correctness comes from scoped ordering, durable idempotency, reconciliation, and replay—not from pretending the whole payment world can be globally ordered.

    21.10 Retry Budgets + DLQ + Ambiguous Outcome Handling

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is Chapter 12’s failure-behavior lesson and Chapter 11’s latency lesson applied to payment side effects. A timeout is not truth; it is uncertainty that must be parked, reconciled, or proven before re-execution.

    Retries are not resilient by themselves. Retries are a bet that reality will become knowable soon. Without declared budgets, that bet turns into recursion, and recursion turns into duplicate captures, missing refunds, and human cleanup of money that should never have gone wrong in the first place.

    The most dangerous failure class is an ambiguous outcome. A timeout on capture does not mean capture failed. It means capture may have succeeded, and your system lost the proof. In the event of ambiguity, the platform must not blindly try again. The right move is to park the workflow, enter reconciliation, and prove whether the side effect occurred using authoritative evidence: processor query APIs, settlement files, webhook confirmations, or ledger comparison.

    DLQ is where this discipline becomes operable. A DLQ is neither a trash can nor a pressure-release valve for throughput. It is a quarantine lane for messages whose correctness cannot be established inside the retry budgets. DLQ items must carry pinned schema and policy versions; replay must require explicit simulate or execute mode; and every replay must emit a receipt.

    A transient network error before the side effect means the failure occurred before the money was actually moved. In this case, the system can retry within the declared count, time, and freshness budget. Short technical hiccups can be safely recovered from if the side effect has not crossed the liability boundary.

    Provider timeout after the capture request is sent is much more dangerous. The timeout does not mean capture failed. It means the capture may have succeeded, but the platform lost proof. The correct action is to park ambiguity and reconcile, not retry blindly. This prevents duplicate charges.

    Retry budget exhausted means the system has tried enough. Continuing would turn resilience into recursion. The correct action is to park the item, move it to DLQ, or route it to human review.

    An idempotency store unavailable is a hard stop for irreversible actions. If the platform cannot check whether the side effect has already happened, it cannot safely execute again. The action should freeze until proof is restored.

    Webhook/provider state conflict means the external truth and internal truth disagree. The system should reconcile with the ledger and provider references rather than inventing a second reality.

    DLQ replay requested means someone wants to rerun failed work. The table requires simulation first, then execution only with explicit approval and a receipt. Replay must not secretly rerun liability.

    The key message is: retry transient failures, park ambiguous outcomes, reconcile before re-execution, and treat DLQ replay as a controlled proof process—not a hidden second payment path.

    This figure makes the adult payment posture visible: retry transient failures, park ambiguous outcomes, reconcile before re-execution, and replay only through a controlled proof lane.

    Figure 21-6 shows how a payment failure should be classified before action. Transient failures can retry inside budgets. Ambiguous outcomes move to park and reconcile, because the side effect may already have occurred. Reconciliation queries the processor, settlement files, webhooks, and ledger. DLQ is reserved for cases that cannot be safely resolved inside the declared budgets, and replay requires explicit mode and receipt emission.

    21.11 Operate It: Stop Authority + ROCS Loops

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is ownership and Chapter 8’s ROCS loop applied to payment operations. Ownership means the ability to freeze capture, refund, deny, or place a payout hold when proof boundaries degrade.

    Payments are not trustworthy just because the code is elegant. They become trustworthy because the system can protect itself when reality turns messy. Stop authority should never be global by default. Money usually does not fail everywhere at once. It fails at a specific liability edge. So you do not stop payments. You freeze the exact action that is becoming unsafe: capture, refund, payout, or automated denial.

    ROCS makes this operational rather than theoretical. Reliability means bounded retries, ambiguity parking, reconciliation paths, and capability-scoped freeze controls. Observability means replay receipts, pinned policy versions, effect IDs, provider references, and ledger state. Cost means retry budgets, DLQ quarantine, scoped freezes, and careful replay modes. Sustainability means the platform can absorb pressure without drifting into harmful behavior.

    The operational posture is clear: Normal ➤ Step-up only ➤ Freeze irreversible actions ➤ Human review only ➤ Re-enable with bumped version. The system stays trustworthy because it knows when to keep going, when to slow down, and when to stop claiming certainty.

    21.12 Worked Example: One Payment in Motion

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is the customer-journey lens turned into a payment proof trace. An order moves through intent, authorization, capture attempt, ambiguity, reconciliation, ledger commit, and replay.

    At 2:09:14 PM, DreamMart emits OrderCreated(orderId=DM-771204). Nothing here moves money yet. It is intent, not liability. The next step is PaymentAuthorized: the system mints paymentIntentId=PI-55219 and receives authId=AUTH-884193 from the processor. This is meaningful, but it is still not the liability boundary. It is permission to attempt.

    At 2:09:41 PM, fulfillment requests funds movement, and the system crosses into the zone where money can duplicate. Before it calls the processor, it mints the side-effect identity: effectId = capture(orderId=DM-771204, attempt=1, amount=129.37, currency=USD). The consumer hits the durable idempotency store using that effectId. No record exists, so it writes IN_PROGRESS(effectId) and only then sends CaptureAttempt to the processor.

    Now the failure shape arrives: the processor times out. The network drops. The platform does not receive a definitive response. This is ambiguity. The naive system tries again. The adult system parks the capture: it transitions the payment into CAPTURE_PENDING_RECONCILE, schedules reconciliation, and emits a replay receipt that explicitly states: attempted capture; outcome unknown; parked; no second attempt allowed until reconciliation confirms state.

    At 2:10:23 PM, reconciliation runs. It queries the processor by paymentIntentId/authId and discovers the capture actually succeeded at 2:09:42 PM. Now the system can commit without duplicating money. It returns to the idempotency store, reads IN_PROGRESS(effectId), writes COMMITTED(effectId, captureId=CAP-77102, processorRef=PR-33F2), and updates the ledger as the system-of-record truth. Weeks later, if a customer disputes the charge, the platform does not argue from logs. It replays.

    21.13 Scar Box: The Night Everything Was Green and Money Duplicated

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is Chapter 12’s failure lesson and Chapter 13’s economics lesson in payment form. The system is not down; it is successfully multiplying liability.

    It was not an outage. That was the trap. Latency was elevated but still within the range people probably call “okay.” Success rates looked normal enough to avoid panic. Dashboards stayed green. Meanwhile, the payment pipeline was drifting into the one failure shape that matters more than availability: incorrect liability. Consumer lag crept upward. Processor calls stretched into the timeout boundary. Then a routine restart landed at exactly the wrong moment. A capture succeeded at the processor, but the acknowledgment never made it back to the service that owned the ledger. The message redelivered. The consumer did what the architecture had quietly trained it to do under uncertainty: try again. Nothing crashed. The platform recovered. The customer was charged twice.

    The cost was not only the duplicate capture. It was chargebacks, support load, reconciliation work, refund operations, customer trust, and the internal cost of proving what should have been impossible.

    We did not discover it because alarms fired. We discovered it because the downstream reality started to disagree with our internal story. Chargebacks rose. Support tickets clustered around duplicate charges. Reconciliation began surfacing pairs of captures that should have been impossible. The real wound became visible: replay coverage for liability actions was incomplete, and capture lacked a durable idempotency claim. We had built at least once a delivery on top of at-most-once proof.

    The fix was not more partitions, more threads, or better retry tuning. The fix was architectural and specific. Every capture attempt now mints an effectId and writes IN_PROGRESS and COMMITTED to a durable store that survives restart and replay. The platform learned the adult response to ambiguity: park, reconcile, then commit once. And the stop authority became real: if the liability-action replay coverage is not at 100% within the rolling window, capture freezes. If you cannot prove what happened, you no longer have the right to keep moving money.

    This figure should be read as a stop-authority trigger map: when consumer lag, timeout straddles, duplicate attempts, and replay gaps converge, the system must freeze capture before recovery behavior becomes money duplication.

    Figure 21-9 shows the life cycle of a duplicate-capture incident. Consumer lag rises, processor timeouts straddle the capture boundary, a restart triggers message redelivery, and duplicate capture occurs. Detection signals include replay gaps, duplicate attempt spikes, reconciliation mismatches, chargebacks, and support spikes. Stop authority freezes captures, parks ambiguous cases, reduces concurrency, and routes cases to review. Remediation adds effectId-based idempotency, a durable state store, receipt coverage, and controlled re-enablement.

    21.14 Interview Lens: What Seniors Sound Like

    Earlier Lens in Action: This is the 7-Step Method compressed into an interview answer: start with the promise, name the actors and liability actions, define the attempt-to-liability boundary, expose scale and failure pressure, choose controls, and prove the outcome.

    If you open a payment-system interview with “we use Stripe, Kafka, and a ledger,” you will sound informed, but not yet senior. You are naming components. Senior architects name obligations. They start with the liability boundary: the exact moment an attempt becomes a claim, the business may have to defend later. They explain what proof must exist at that moment, what token marks the action, what idempotency gate prevents duplicate money, and what happens when the outcome is ambiguous.

    What to Say in the Interview: I am not designing only a gateway, ledger, queue, and webhook system. I am designing a payment commitment engine. Intent and authorization are permissions to try; capture, refund, refund denial, and payout hold are liability actions. Every liability action must mint explicit tokens, pin policy and schema versions, pass a durable idempotency gate keyed by effectId, write to the ledger, and emit a replayable receipt. I scope ordering only where business truth needs it, such as orderId, paymentIntentId, refundRequestId, or merchantId. If capture times out after the provider call, I do not blindly retry. I park ambiguity, reconcile against the processor, settlement, webhook, and ledger evidence, and then commit once. If replay coverage, idempotency state, processor health, or reconciliation integrity degrades, capability-scoped stop authority freezes the unsafe action before money duplicates or customer harm spreads.

    21.15 Chapter Summary

    A payment system is not a money-movement pipeline. It is a liability commitment engine. The architecture must distinguish between attempt, authorization, capture, settlement, and dispute resolution.

    The senior move is not to draw gateways, ledgers, queues, and webhooks first. It is to draw the liability boundary: which action creates the obligation, what token identifies it, what effectId protects it from duplication, what evidence was admissible, what policy version governed it, what ledger state was written, and what proof exists if the outcome is later challenged.

    If the platform cannot replay why it captured, refunded, denied, held, parked, reconciled, or compensated under a pinned policy and evidence snapshot, it does not control payments. Payments control the platform.

    21.16 Remember This

    • Payments are not money movement; they are promises under uncertainty.

    • Attempt is negotiable. Liability is not.

    • Authorization is permission to try; capture is a liability boundary.

    • Exactly-once is not a strategy. Idempotency is.

    • effectId belongs to the side effect, not just the message.

    • A timeout after a provider call is ambiguity, not failure.

    • Ambiguous outcomes should be parked and reconciled before re-execution.

    • The ledger is courtroom memory.

    • Reconciliation is correctness recovery, not back-office cleanup.

    • If you cannot replay why money moved, you cannot defend the platform.

    21.17 Architect’s Reflection

    The hardest truth in payments is that the system can be alive and still be wrong. A request can return 200, a consumer can process a message, a provider can accept a call, and a dashboard can remain green while the business creates a liability it cannot defend. That is why the design center is not throughput. It is proof under adversarial time.

    Carry one question into every payment design review: Where does the system stop trying and start owing? If that boundary is not visible, tokenized, idempotent, replayable, and stoppable, the architecture will eventually let ambiguity become money movement.

    We can replay why we captured, refunded, denied, or held under this policy version, with admissible evidence, and we can freeze liability actions when proof is incomplete. That is what mature payment architecture sounds like.

    21.18 Interview Practice and Assignments

    Use the following questions to test whether you can design payment systems like a senior architect. A strong answer should not start with “use Stripe, Kafka, a ledger, and webhooks.” It should explain attempt versus liability, side-effect boundaries, idempotency, effect IDs, ledger truth, delayed truth, reconciliation, retry budgets, DLQ replay, stop authority, and replayable proof.

    1. Payments As a Liability Commitment Engine

    A candidate says, “A payment system moves money between a customer and a merchant.”

    Why is that incomplete?

    Your answer should include

    • Why payments are commitment engines, not merely money-movement pipelines

    • Why authorization is permission to try, while capture is a liability boundary

    • Why “provider accepted” is not the same as “platform can defend the outcome”

    • Which actors are involved: customer, merchant, processor, acquirer, card network, issuer, fraud engine, ledger, finance, reconciliation, support, and dispute operations

    • Why payment failures can happen while dashboards remain green

    • What evidence must exist before the platform claims captured, refunded, denied, held, or reconciled

    • What must be replayable later

    2. Token Chain and Side-Effect Boundary

    Design the payment token chain using paymentIntentId, authId, effectId, captureId, refundId, refundDenyId, payoutHoldId, disputeId, and responseId.

    Your answer should include

    • What each token proves

    • Why paymentIntentId and authId are pre-liability tokens

    • Why effectId belongs to the side effect, not merely the message

    • Why captureId, refundId, refundDenyId, and payoutHoldId are liability claims

    • Why the idempotency store must be durable

    • What happens if the provider succeeds but the platform loses the acknowledgment

    • What the Payment Decision Replay Card should contain

    3. Idempotency versus Exactly-Once Question

    An interviewer says, “We can solve duplicate payments by using exactly-once delivery from Kafka and making the consumer idempotent.”

    Analyze this answer.

    Your answer should include

    • Why exactly-once delivery is not enough at the external side-effect boundary

    • Why duplicates can still happen through retries, restarts, provider timeouts, webhook redelivery, or DLQ replay

    • Why idempotency must be anchored by effectId

    • Why the idempotency record must write IN_PROGRESS before execution

    • Why COMMITTED must include provider reference, liability token, ledger state, and replay receipt

    • Why Redis-only dedupe is unsafe for money-moving truth

    • How duplicate capture should become a no-op rather than a second charge

    4. Ambiguous Capture Timeout Question

    A capture request is sent to the processor. The processor times out. The customer-facing order flow is waiting, and the message is redelivered after a consumer restart.

    What should the system do?

    Your answer should include

    • Why timeout after provider call is ambiguity, not failure

    • Why blind retry can create duplicate capture

    • Why the system should park the workflow as CAPTURE_PENDING_RECONCILE

    • How to reconcile using processor query API, webhooks, settlement files, and ledger comparison

    • How to update the idempotency store after truth returns

    • How to communicate pending status without lying to the customer

    • What replay receipt must be emitted

    5. Ledger Truth and Delayed Truth Question

    A system records a capture in its ledger. Settlement arrives two days later with a mismatch. A chargeback arrives three weeks later.

    How should the architecture handle this?

    Your answer should include

    • Difference between event time, processor time, ledger time, settlement time, and dispute time

    • Why the ledger is the platform’s courtroom memory

    • Why settlement and dispute events should not overwrite original truth

    • How reconciliation confirms, corrects, or compensates

    • How dispute evidence links back to original policy version and evidence snapshot

    • Why delayed truth improves correctness without inventing a second reality

    • What fields must be preserved for audit and support
