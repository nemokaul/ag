Chapter 1. Hello AI Engineering!

A few years ago, adding real AI to a product meant months of work: gather data, label it, train a model, tune it, and pray it survived contact with the real world. Today, that model often already exists. One API call reaches a system that writes code, answers questions, summarizes documents, and reasons through problems that used to demand a dedicated research team.

That did not just make the work easier. It moved the hard part. When a powerful model is one call away, the challenge is no longer training it. It is building a reliable system around it: how you prompt it, how it retrieves accurate information, how it uses tools, how you evaluate it, how you keep it safe, and how you keep it fast and affordable under real traffic. That is the discipline of AI engineering, and it is what this book is about.

This shift did not come from nowhere. AI engineering grew out of machine learning engineering: the practice of taking models from experiments and notebooks into production by building the surrounding systems for data collection, labeling, feature engineering, training, validation, deployment, monitoring, and retraining. Figure 1-1 shows that lifecycle.

That model-centric workflow still matters. But with the rise of foundation models, especially large language models, many teams are no longer training sophisticated models from scratch for every new use case. Instead, they integrate powerful pre-trained models and build systems around them. This shifts the center of gravity: in classical ML engineering, the core challenge was often to train a task-specific model and maintain the lifecycle around it; in AI engineering, the model is usually already available through an API, an open-weight release, or a managed platform, so the focus moves from building the model itself to adapting, orchestrating, evaluating, and deploying it inside a larger application.

In that sense, ML engineering often asks, “How do we build and operate the model?” AI engineering more often asks, “How do we build a reliable system around a powerful model?” Prompt design, retrieval, tool use, guardrails, latency, cost, and system-level evaluation all become first-class engineering concerns.

GenAI: The Core of AI Engineering

Generative AI sits at the core of AI engineering because foundation models have made powerful AI capabilities broadly accessible. A small number of large models now provide abilities that once required building separate systems from scratch, abilities such as generation, summarization, reasoning, coding, retrieval, and multimodal understanding. Because these models are available through APIs, open-source releases, or managed platforms, engineers can incorporate them into products far more easily than in previous eras of AI. This has changed the center of gravity of the field.

Before diving into how generative AI works, we need to understand where it fits in the bigger picture. Generative AI is not an isolated breakthrough; it is the product of decades of progress in artificial intelligence, machine learning, and deep learning.

This chapter traces that evolution, showing how statistical methods gave way to neural networks, which scaled into today’s large language models. By understanding this progression, you will better appreciate what makes modern generative AI special, why it is advancing so rapidly, and where it is headed next. You will also see where you might fit into this rapidly evolving field.

Let’s get started.

AI is a broad and evolving field; it formally came into existence in 1956, during the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, held at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire. Over the decades, the field of AI has matured into a structured ecosystem of subfields. At the heart of this structure lies a hierarchy of increasingly specialized domains, depicted in Figure 1-2.

AI is dedicated to building machines that can perform tasks that require human-like intelligence. A core subfield of AI is Machine Learning, which focuses on enabling systems to learn from data. ML began to gain widespread adoption in the 1990s and 2000s with the development of powerful statistical models and advances in computational resources.

A specialized subset of ML is Deep Learning, which rose to prominence in 2012 with the success of deep neural networks in large-scale image classification tasks.

GenAI represents the most recent evolution of Deep Learning: models that are not only capable of analyzing information, but also of creating new content. Although GenAI encompasses models that can generate text, images, audio, video, and even multimodal experiences, the most transformative breakthroughs have come from language models, which give machines the ability to understand and produce human language.

Because language is a universal medium for representing knowledge and reasoning, language models (LMs) form the foundation upon which many other generative systems are built. The next section traces the evolution of language models, from early statistical techniques to today’s large-scale transformer-based architectures that power modern GenAI.

Language Modeling: The Core of GenAI

At the heart of GenAI lies language modeling (LM), which is a foundational approach to teaching computers to understand and communicate using human language. LM focuses on modeling the generative likelihood of word sequences by predicting the probability of a word based on its surrounding context, i.e. the words that come before or after it. LM has been studied for over 70 years and has evolved significantly. We can broadly divide the development of the LM field into three major eras, as depicted in Figure 1-3.

The Era of Statistical Language Modeling

From the 1950s through the early 2010s, statistical approaches dominated LM. One of the most modeling techniques was n-gram modeling, which is about estimating the probability of a word by looking at the previous n-1 words. For example, a trigram model would predict each word based only on the two preceding words. These models have two key characteristics. One is a short context window, which is often just one or two words. For example, in a trigram model, only the two previous words are used to predict the next. The second is their limited capacity for syntactic or semantic understanding. Since statistical modeling techniques are not able to look very far back in the sentence, they are limited in understanding meaning or grammatical structure.

The Era of Neural Language Modeling

In the early 2000s neural networks began to replace traditional statistical methods. A pivotal breakthrough came with the work of Bengio et al. (2003), which introduced the idea of training neural networks to learn distributed word representations, known as embeddings, and use them to predict sequences of words. This approach enabled models to generalize better by capturing semantic relationships between words in a continuous vector space.

During the following decade, neural models advanced rapidly. Techniques like Word2Vec and GloVe popularized dense word embeddings. Meanwhile, recurrent architectures, such as RNNs, LSTMs, and GRUs, allowed models to process sequences of arbitrary length, improving their ability to learn from context.

In 2017, the field underwent another transformative leap with the introduction of the Transformer architecture, which replaced recurrence with self-attention mechanisms. This innovation made it possible to scale models efficiently while capturing long-range dependencies in text, laying the groundwork for today’s large-scale pre-trained models.

The Era of Large Language Models (LLMs)

Starting around 2018—​2020, the field entered the era of Large Language Models. Although LLMs are still fundamentally neural language models, what distinguishes them is scale in both model size and training data. These models range from billions to trillions of parameters and are trained on massive, web-scale corpora.

This dramatic increase in scale has led to surprising behaviors, known as emerging capabilities, in LLMs. These are behaviors that LLMs were not explicitly trained for but are nonetheless able to exhibit. For instance, LLMs can perform in-context learning, where they generalize from just a few examples provided at inference time, without any additional training. As a result, LLMs provide general-purpose foundation models that are capable of performing diverse tasks with minimal supervision.

As the field entered the era of LLMs, a rich ecosystem of models emerged from research institutions, technology companies, and open communities. In the next section, we look at how these models differ in architecture, scale, openness, and intended use cases.

Large Language Models

Large language models (LLMs) are the centerpiece of this book. They represent the foundation on which nearly all modern generative AI systems are built. While the rest of the book will explore their inner workings in depth, this section provides a high-level overview.

Industry Landscape and Communities

The modern landscape of LLMs is shaped by a rich ecosystem of industry leaders, research organizations, and open-source communities.

At the forefront of large language model development are a handful of influential organizations. OpenAI pioneered the GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) series and set the stage for general-purpose, instruction-following models with ChatGPT. Anthropic, founded by former OpenAI researchers, emphasizes safety and alignment through its Constitutional AI approach and the Claude family of models. Google DeepMind leads with the Gemini series, a powerful but closed-source line of multimodal models. Meta AI, in contrast, took an open approach with its LLaMA models and sparked a wave of open-source innovation across the AI community, while Google also ships open-weight models in its Gemma family alongside the closed Gemini line. Meanwhile, newer players have entered the scene with competitive models that push the boundaries of efficiency and accessibility: Mistral AI and Cohere in Europe, xAI with its Grok series, and China’s DeepSeek, whose open-weight reasoning models demonstrated frontier-level capability at a fraction of the usual training cost.

Parallel to corporate efforts, open-source communities have played a key role in democratizing generative AI. Hugging Face serves as the central hub for sharing, benchmarking, and deploying models, with its Hub, Transformers, and Datasets libraries powering a broad range of research and production systems. Lesser-known but highly influential groups like EleutherAI and LAION (Large-Scale Artificial Intelligence Open Network) have also been instrumental. EleutherAI is a volunteer research collective that has created open models such as GPT-Neo and Pythia, while LAION, a nonprofit organization in Germany, builds and shares large open datasets that have supported image-generation models such as Stable Diffusion. The open-source ecosystem also extends beyond models and datasets into deployment infrastructure. Tools such as Ollama have made it easier for developers to run open models locally, while vLLM has become a widely used inference and serving engine for deploying large language models efficiently. Together, these communities and tools have turned AI from a closed research field into a global and collaborative ecosystem where anyone can build, share, deploy, and improve the next generation of models.

Open-Weight vs Closed Models

Large language models can broadly be grouped by how they are accessed and governed: open-weight models and closed, proprietary models.

Open-weight LLMs are models whose trained parameters are publicly available for download, inspection, fine-tuning, or self-hosted deployment, subject to the terms of their licenses. In many cases, these models also publish architecture details, model cards, evaluation results, and technical reports, although the full training data and training pipeline may not always be released. Representative open-weight model families include Meta’s Llama series, Mistral’s Mistral and Mixtral models, Alibaba’s Qwen models, Technology Innovation Institute’s Falcon models, Google’s Gemma models, and others. Because openness varies across licenses and releases, the term open-weight is often more precise than open-source.

Closed, proprietary LLMs, in contrast, do not publicly release their model weights. Access is typically provided through cloud APIs, hosted applications, or integrated platforms. Their model weights, training data, and alignment pipelines remain controlled by the provider. Prominent examples include the models behind ChatGPT from OpenAI, Claude from Anthropic, and Gemini from Google DeepMind. These systems are often among the strongest available models in terms of capability, usability, alignment, and reliability, but they offer less transparency and less direct control to external researchers and developers.

Figure 1-4 shows representative open-weight and closed large-language models, positioned by release year and parameter scale. As the field evolved, model size increased dramatically. Earlier models such as BERT and GPT-2 had hundreds of millions to a few billion parameters, while later generations moved into tens, hundreds, and eventually thousands of billions of parameters.

The distinction between open-weight and closed models is not simply philosophical; it affects how models are studied, customized, and deployed. Open-weight models give researchers and practitioners more control over fine-tuning, inference, auditing, and deployment environments. Closed models, meanwhile, often provide stronger hosted performance, product integration, safety layers, and operational reliability. The coexistence of both paradigms has shaped the modern generative AI landscape: open-weight models democratize experimentation and deployment, while proprietary systems continue to push frontier capability and large-scale productization.

Architectures

Any large language model follows one of three main architecture types: encoder-only, decoder-only, or encoder—​decoder. While we will dive deeper into how each of these works in Chapter Chapter 7, here is a quick overview to give you a sense of the big picture.

Encoder-Only Models

Encoder-only models are like expert readers. Their job is to take in text, understand it, and turn it into a rich representation that captures meaning, relationships, and context. These models do not generate new text; they interpret the existing text.

At a high level, the encoder reads all the tokens (words) in a sentence at once and learns how each word relates to every other word. For example, in the sentence “I want to meet a new friend,” the encoder learns that “I” is the subject, “want” is the verb, and “meet” is the action. The result is a vector representation of each word that captures their semantics. Figure 1-5 illustrates the high-level diagram of an encoder-based LLM.

Since encoder-only models focus on understanding rather than generating, they excel at tasks like text classification (e.g., sentiment analysis or spam detection) and named entity recognition (identifying names, dates, organizations). Well-known examples of encoder-only models are BERT, RoBERTa, and DistilBERT. These are models that form the backbone of many modern natural-language understanding systems.

Decoder-Only Models

Decoder-only models, like GPT, LLaMA, Mistral, and Falcon, are designed for text generation. They read the input text from left to right and predict the next word one at a time, using everything that has come before as context. This step-by-step process is called autoregressive generation, which means it generates text one token at a time, always using its previous outputs as part of the input for the next prediction. Figure 1-6 shows a high-level overview of decoder-only models.

The autoregressive nature makes decoder-only models natural storytellers; they are great for writing, reasoning, and answering questions in a conversational way.

These models have shown impressive behaviors where they learn new tasks on the fly. This is called emergent behavior, and examples of it include zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot learning. In zero-shot learning, the model is asked to perform a task it has not been explicitly trained for, like summarizing a paragraph or translating a sentence, and it can still do it reasonably well just from understanding language. In few-shot learning, you give the model a few examples, such as showing it how to answer two or three math problems, and it quickly picks up the pattern to solve the next one. We discuss these further in depth in Chapter Chapter 3.

This ability to generalize from little or no prior instruction is what makes modern language models so flexible and powerful.

Encoder-Decoder Models

Encoder-decoder models such as T5, BART, and mT5 combine both architectures. The encoder processes the input bidirectionally, building a rich understanding of the source material, while the decoder generates the output autoregressively, creating the response one token at a time. This structure is flexible for tasks that involve both deep understanding of input and generation of output.

Machine translation is a classic use case in which the encoder understands the source-language sentence completely, then the decoder generates the translation. Text summarization works similarly, with the encoder processing a long document and the decoder producing a concise summary. Paraphrasing and question answering also benefit from this architecture.

Encoder—​decoder models were widely used before the rise of today’s large decoder-only models. Decoder-only models have largely taken their place because they simplify the architecture without losing capability. As models grew in size, researchers found that a single decoder stack could handle both understanding and generation provided it was trained on a large and diverse text corpus.

Building Large Language Models

Building a large language model involves a multi-stage process that spans data engineering, large-scale training, fine-tuning, deployment, and continuous improvement. Although the exact stages may vary depending on the use case and resources, the following four stages, as shown in Figure 1-8, represent the core of how modern generative AI systems are developed and maintained.

Stage 1: Pretraining

Pretraining is the foundational stage where a model learns to understand and generate language (or other modalities) by being exposed to a massive and diverse dataset. This dataset often includes content from the web, books, code repositories, scientific articles, and more. During this phase, the model learns statistical patterns in text and is able to do text completion.

Pretraining typically requires enormous computational resources, from hundreds of GPUs or TPUs for smaller models to tens of thousands for frontier ones (Llama 3.1 405B, for instance, trained on roughly 16,000 H100 GPUs), running for weeks or months, and it results in a foundation model that has a broad understanding of language but is not yet optimized for specific tasks. Chapter 2 covers the pretraining process, while Chapter 8 focuses on the data preparation required for pretraining.

Stage 2: Fine-Tuning

After pretraining, a model can be adapted further for a specific use case, but this step is not always required. Many practical applications, such as summarization, named entity recognition, classification, or information extraction, can often be handled by an existing foundation model using prompting, few-shot examples (see Chapter 3), or retrieval-augmented generation (see Chapter 4). Fine-tuning, becomes useful when the available model does not perform reliably enough for the target domain, task, output format, latency requirement, or deployment constraint.

Fine-tuning is the broad process of continuing training from a pre-trained model on a smaller, more targeted dataset. The goal is not to teach the model language from scratch, but to adapt its behavior to a particular setting.

One form of adaptation is domain adaptation, where a model is exposed to data from a specific field, such as biomedical literature, legal documents, financial reports, or enterprise documentation. This helps the model better understand the terminology, style, and concepts of that domain. When this training is done on raw domain text, it is often referred to as continued pretraining or domain-adaptive pretraining.

Another form is task-specific fine-tuning, where the model is trained on examples of a particular task, such as summarization, question answering, sentiment analysis, or named entity recognition. This helps the model produce outputs in the desired format and improve performance on that task.

Instruction tuning is a specific type of supervised fine-tuning in which the model is trained on instruction-response pairs. Instead of merely continuing text, the model learns to follow user requests, answer questions, and respond in a helpful format. This stage is especially important for turning a base language model into an assistant-like model.

Compared with pretraining, fine-tuning usually requires far less data and compute. In practice, teams may skip fine-tuning entirely, apply it once for a specific task, or run multiple rounds of adaptation depending on the quality, reliability, and deployment requirements of the application. See Chapter 10 for more details.

Stage 3: Alignment

The alignment stage is the final step in training a large language model. At this point, the model already knows how to understand and generate language, but it does not yet know how to behave in ways that are useful, safe, or aligned with human expectations. Alignment fine-tunes the model’s behavior so that its responses are helpful, truthful, and respectful.

To achieve this, developers use techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). In these methods, humans evaluate the model’s outputs, for instance choosing which of two responses sounds more helpful or less biased, and the model learns to prefer those kinds of answers in the future. The result is a model that can follow instructions, refuse unsafe requests, and engage in natural and cooperative conversation. Chapter 11 reviews this topic comprehensively.

Stage 4: Inference and Deployment

Once a model has been trained and adapted, it must be made available for use. Inference is the process of running the model on new inputs to generate outputs, either in real time or in batch mode. This includes the mechanics of token generation, decoding, batching, memory usage, and other runtime optimizations. Chapter 13 explores these concepts in detail.

Serving or deployment refers to the system-level work required to make inference available to users or applications. This includes hosting the model on cloud infrastructure or edge devices, exposing it through APIs, managing latency and throughput, scaling the service, monitoring reliability, and optimizing cost.

You may be asked in an interview which stage of the LLM lifecycle interests you most and why. This question tests your self-awareness and role fit. A strong answer names a specific stage (pretraining, fine-tuning, alignment, deployment, evaluation) and explains what draws you to it with concrete examples. If you say “deployment,” explain what about serving LLMs at scale interests you: is it the latency optimization, the cost management, the reliability engineering?

The interviewer is checking whether your interests align with the role. A candidate for an AI Engineering role who says “pretraining” should explain the connection, for instance how understanding pretraining data composition helps you debug unexpected model behavior in production. If there is a mismatch between your stated interest and the role, address it directly rather than hoping the interviewer won’t notice.

Evaluation as a Continuous Process

Together, these four stages, Pretraining, Fine-Tuning, Alignment, and Inference and Deployment, form the core lifecycle of building a large language model. The first three shape the model’s weights; the fourth serves the finished model to users. They typically occur in sequence, each building upon the previous stage to shape the model’s behavior and usability.

At the same time, evaluation runs horizontally across the entire process. From data quality checks during pretraining to safety audits during alignment and performance monitoring after deployment, evaluation ensures that the model remains reliable and aligned with its intended goals at every stage of development.

During pretraining, it helps identify data biases and coverage gaps. In instruction tuning and alignment, it measures how well the model follows instructions and produces safe, helpful responses. And once deployed, continuous monitoring detects issues such as performance drift or unintended behaviors in real-world use. Evaluation is not a one-time task; it is the feedback loop that keeps the entire LLM lifecycle improving over time.

Current State of Performance

The open-source community maintains several leaderboards to track model performance across standardized benchmarks. While benchmarks have their limitations—they do not capture everything that matters in practice—they provide a useful snapshot of relative capabilities. The Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard is one of the most widely referenced leaderboards that evaluates models on tasks like reasoning, knowledge, and language understanding.

As of early 2025, the landscape shows interesting patterns across different model sizes and training approaches. Rather than simply racing for the largest parameter counts, the field has matured to optimize for efficiency and practical deployment. In other words, the question has shifted from “how big can we make it?” to “how good can we make it for a given size?”

Performance Among Pre-trained Models

Large language models can be grouped into broad size classes based on their number of parameters: small, mid-size, large, and very large. Looking at pretrained models before instruction tuning, several families dominate their respective size classes.

  • Small language models (SLMs) around 1—​3B parameters are increasingly capable, challenging the assumption that you need massive models for everything. Google’s Flan-T5-Large at just 0.8B parameters remains competitive, demonstrating that strong pretraining data and objectives matter more than pure scale. Qwen 2.5-3B leads the approximately 2B parameter class, offering surprisingly strong performance for on-device and edge deployment scenarios where you simply cannot fit larger models.

  • Mid-size models between 7B and 14B parameters represent a sweet spot for many applications. They are large enough to handle complex tasks but small enough to deploy on accessible hardware. Qwen3-8B is a strong open-weight model in the 7B/8B range, with notable capabilities in multilingual use cases, mathematical reasoning, and agentic workflows. At 14B parameters, models like Qwen 2.5-14B and Falcon 3 MoE demonstrate that careful architecture choices can match larger models.

  • Large models from 20B to 72B parameters are where open-weight models increasingly compete with closed ones. Qwen 2.5-72B demonstrates that well-trained open models can rival GPT-3.5 and early GPT-4 variants on many benchmarks. These models are large enough for complex reasoning, maintaining coherence over long contexts and handling multi-step problems, but still deployable on accessible hardware. A 72B model quantized to 4-bit can run on a single high-end consumer GPU.

  • Very large models above 100B parameters push the boundaries of what is possible with open weights. Qwen 1.5-110B and Mistral Large at 123B show that the open-source community can train models at scales previously exclusive to well-funded labs. These models compete directly with GPT-4 on many tasks, though they still trail on the most challenging reasoning problems. The fact that they exist at all represents a democratization of AI capabilities that seemed unlikely just a few years ago.

Performance Among Instruction-Tuned Models

After pretraining, most production models undergo instruction tuning. This additional training phase dramatically improves their ability to follow instructions and avoid harmful outputs. The difference between a pretrained model and its instruction-tuned version is often night and day: one completes text mechanically, the other acts like a helpful assistant.

The leaderboard shows that instruction tuning can elevate smaller models to outperform larger pretrained-only models. Qwen 2.5-3B-Instruct, for instance, competes with much larger pretrained models on practical tasks. Phi-4 from Microsoft, despite being only 14B parameters, uses high-quality synthetic data during instruction tuning to punch above its weight class. Mistral-Large-Instruct rivals GPT-4 on many benchmarks despite being open-weight, demonstrating that the gap between open and closed models is narrowing.

We cover instruction tuning in depth in Chapter Chapter 10, but it is worth noting here that the gap between pretrained and instruction-tuned models is often larger than the gap between different model families. A well-instruction-tuned 13B model frequently outperforms a raw 70B model on practical tasks involving instruction following and conversation. This has important implications for model selection: do not assume bigger is always better.

In Figure 1-9, the teal dashed curve (instruction-tuned models) sits consistently above the pretrained curve, indicating that instruction tuning delivers a consistent performance uplift at a given model size, i.e. better average benchmark scores without increasing parameter count.

What the Leaderboard Doesn’t Tell You

While benchmarks are useful, they do not capture everything that matters in practice. Latency, throughput, and inference cost are crucial for production systems. A model might score well on standard benchmarks but be too slow for real-time chat, too memory-intensive to serve efficiently, or too expensive per request at production scale. Calibration matters too; some models are confidently wrong and state incorrect facts with high certainty, while others appropriately express uncertainty. Robustness is another gap: performance on clean benchmarks does not always predict performance on messy real-world data with typos, unclear phrasing, missing context, or unexpected formats.

Safety is largely absent from standard benchmarks. High benchmark scores do not guarantee safe, aligned behavior. A model might excel at reasoning benchmarks while being easy to jailbreak or prone to generating harmful content.

Cost varies dramatically between models of similar capability; inference costs, memory requirements, and operational complexity can differ by orders of magnitude. A model that is 2% better on benchmarks but 10x more expensive to run is not necessarily the right choice.

The best model for your application depends on your specific requirements, constraints, and evaluation criteria, not just leaderboard position. Sometimes a smaller, faster, cheaper model that is 95% as good is the right choice. Sometimes you need the absolute best regardless of cost. The leaderboard provides data points, but you still need judgment.

Emerging Trends

Several trends are reshaping how we think about model performance and architecture. Here are a few of the most influential ones.

First, mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures, like Mixtral and Falcon 3 MoE, activate only a subset of parameters for each input. This dramatically reduces inference costs while maintaining capability. For instance, instead of running all 56B parameters, Mixtral uses only about 12.9B per token. This design is becoming standard for efficient large models because it offers the best of both worlds: the capacity of large models with the speed of smaller ones.

Second, model merging, which combines multiple fine-tuned models into one, is another emerging trend. It often produces surprising performance gains. The technique feels almost like alchemy: take two models trained for different tasks, mathematically blend their weights, and sometimes end up with a model that performs better at both. Community leaderboards now feature several merged models competing with single models trained with far more compute. Although the theoretical understanding behind it is still evolving, the practical results are impressive enough that merging has become a standard tool in the community toolkit.

Third, continued pretraining extends a base model’s training on domain-specific data, and creates specialized versions more efficiently than training from scratch. For example, BgGPT-Gemma was created by continuing Gemma’s pretraining on Bulgarian-language data, resulting in a model that excels in Bulgarian while preserving general capabilities. This method is especially valuable for underrepresented languages and specialized fields where good-quality data exists but is limited in size.

Lastly, synthetic data is increasingly used for both pretraining and fine-tuning. Models like Microsoft’s Phi series demonstrate that small, carefully curated, or synthetically generated datasets can outperform much larger web-scraped ones. The idea is to use strong models to generate high-quality training data for smaller models, a process known as knowledge distillation. This shift not only improves data efficiency but also raises fascinating questions about what it means for models to learn from other models instead of purely human-created content.

Career Paths in Generative AI

“What kind of work will I actually be doing in generative AI?” is a common and critical question for anyone entering the field. Careers in generative AI are not static; they evolve. As models grow in capability and reach, new job categories are emerging while others are becoming more specialized.

Generative AI has given rise to a diverse ecosystem of roles, each requiring a distinct blend of technical and analytical expertise. In this section, we explore the most prominent roles in the field, the core skills they require, and the interview formats commonly used to evaluate candidates.

Roles, Responsibilities, and Required Skills

Although job titles vary across organizations, they cluster around five core functions:

  1. Research, typically represented by roles such as Research Scientist or Applied Scientist. They are focused on advancing model capabilities through new architectures, training methods, and optimization techniques.

  2. Infrastructure, encompassing roles like Machine Learning Engineer or MLOps Engineer. They are responsible for building the systems and pipelines that enable large-scale model training, deployment, and evaluation.

  3. Application, where titles such as Prompt Engineer, LLM Application Developer, or RAG Engineer specialize in integrating models into real-world use cases through prompting, retrieval, and orchestration.

  4. Product, led by roles such as AI Product Manager or Technical Program Manager, focused on shaping product direction by aligning user needs with model capabilities and responsible innovation.

  5. Safety, which includes Evaluation Specialist, Red Team Member, or AI Policy Analyst, dedicated to evaluating model safety, reliability, and ethical alignment through rigorous testing and audit frameworks.

These core functions lie on a spectrum, ranging from building models themselves to building products and systems with models. Figure 1-10 illustrates how these roles are distributed across that spectrum: Research and Infrastructure sit closer to the model layer, while Application and Product focus more on translating model capabilities into practical use cases and user-facing systems. Safety spans both ends, since it requires evaluating not only model internals but also system-level behavior in real-world deployment.

After positioning these five core functions along the spectrum from building models to building with models, it is also useful to consider how they are represented in industry hiring. Figure 1-11 provides an approximate view of the distribution of roles across these functions. As it suggests, much of today’s hiring is concentrated in application and infrastructure roles.

Interview Types and Preparation

Interviews in generative AI typically fall into three categories:

  • Technical Competency Interviews: These assess your foundational knowledge, such as machine learning algorithms, model architectures, programming, and data manipulation. Questions are role-specific. A research scientist may be asked to derive a loss function or explain attention mechanisms, while an ML engineer might implement a tokenizer.

  • Design Interviews: These simulate real-world problems to evaluate structured reasoning, trade-off analysis, and implementation decisions. You might be asked how to integrate a generative model into a customer service system, or how to balance model quality with inference latency.

  • Behavioral Interviews: These explore your prior experiences and decision-making style. Common themes include collaboration, navigating ambiguity, and resolving challenges in cross-functional environments.

Knowing these interview types helps you anticipate the questions you will face, choose the most relevant experiences to highlight, and demonstrate that you are ready to contribute in the ways the role demands.

It is also wise to clarify expectations early in the process. Ask the recruiter about the interview structure, evaluation focus, and how the team defines success for the role. Doing so helps you tailor your preparation and avoid misalignment with the team’s needs.

To prepare effectively, start by identifying the core responsibilities of your target role. Interview preparation is most effective when aligned with the expectations of the target role.

Summary

In this chapter, we introduce AI engineering, the practice of building reliable products around powerful pre-trained models. We trace the field’s evolution from traditional machine learning and follow the development of language models from statistical methods to neural networks, Transformers, and modern LLMs.

We also introduce the three main model architectures—encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder–decoder—and explain the four stages of the LLM lifecycle: pretraining, fine-tuning, alignment, and deployment. We show how evaluation supports every stage by measuring quality, safety, cost, and reliability.

Finally, we compare open and closed models, examine practical model-selection trade-offs and emerging trends, and introduce the major career paths in generative AI. Together, these topics give readers a clear overview of the technologies, roles, and decisions that shape modern generative AI.

Interview Questions and Answers

The questions in Threads 1 and 2 are open-ended. There is no single correct answer. For each, we describe what interviewers are looking for and how to structure a strong response.

Thread: Why AI?

  1. What excites you about generative AI, and why do you want to work in this field?

    Interviewers ask this to separate genuine motivation from hype-chasing. A strong answer connects three things:

    • A specific technical interest (“I find the evaluation problem fascinating, because measuring whether an LLM is actually reliable is still largely unsolved”)

    • A concrete personal experience (“I built a RAG prototype at my last job and saw how much the retrieval quality mattered”)

    • An honest awareness of open challenges (“hallucination and safety are not solved, and that is part of what draws me in”)

    Avoid vague answers like “AI is the future” or “I want to change the world.” Interviewers hear those constantly and they reveal nothing about you. Be specific about what you have done, what you found interesting, and what you want to work on next.

  2. How do you stay current with the rapid pace of change in AI?

    The interviewer wants to see that you have a sustainable learning system, not just a bookmark folder. Describe the mix: primary sources you follow (specific researchers, labs, or conference proceedings), hands-on experimentation (replicating a paper, building a small project with a new technique), and how you filter signal from noise (“I skim many papers but only deeply read ones relevant to my current work or that shift a fundamental assumption”).

    Mention specific communities or resources that have shaped your thinking, whether that is Hugging Face, arXiv, a reading group, or a blog like Lilian Weng’s or Chip Huyen’s. What matters is demonstrating active engagement, not passive consumption.

  3. What concerns you most about the current direction of AI development?

    This tests intellectual maturity and critical thinking. Strong answers address real concerns such as safety and alignment gaps, concentration of capabilities in a few labs, the environmental cost of large-scale training, reliability of deployed systems, or economic displacement. Pick one concern you have genuinely thought about, explain why it worries you, and describe what thoughtful responses to it look like.

    Avoid two failure modes: dismissiveness (“nothing, everything is great”) and performative doom (“AI will destroy everything”). The interviewer is looking for someone who can hold complexity, acknowledge uncertainty, and still want to do the work.

Thread: Career Tracks and Role Selection

  1. How would you describe the difference between an ML Engineer and an AI Engineer?

    The core distinction is building models versus building with models. ML Engineers work on the model layer: training, fine-tuning, optimization, distributed systems, and the infrastructure that supports these. AI Engineers work on the application layer: prompt engineering, RAG pipelines, agent orchestration, evaluation frameworks, and system-level integration that turns model capabilities into reliable products.

    In practice, the boundary is blurry. Many roles blend elements of both. But the interview expectations differ sharply. An ML Engineer interview tests training pipelines, loss functions, and distributed compute. An AI Engineer interview tests system design, prompt strategies, evaluation methodology, and how you would handle model failures in production.

  2. Where in the LLM lifecycle do these roles overlap, and where do they diverge?

    Both roles engage deeply with evaluation and deployment. Evaluation is everyone’s problem: ML Engineers evaluate training loss, benchmark scores, and fine-tuning quality; AI Engineers evaluate end-to-end system behavior, user satisfaction, and failure modes. Deployment sits at the intersection too, since serving a model efficiently requires both infrastructure expertise and understanding of how the application uses the model.

    The divergence is clearest at the extremes of the pipeline. Pretraining, architecture design, and distributed training are firmly ML Engineering and Research territory. Prompt engineering, RAG system design, agent orchestration, and context engineering are firmly AI Engineering territory. Fine-tuning sits in the middle: an ML Engineer might run the training loop, while an AI Engineer defines the evaluation criteria and curates the training data.

  3. Tell me about a project where you used a language model to solve a real problem.

    Expect this question in nearly every AI interview loop. Structure your answer using the STAR format: Situation (what was the problem and context), Task (what were you specifically responsible for), Action (what you built and the key decisions you made), Result (what happened and what you learned), and then briefly close with your key learning from the project.

    Strong answers include: which model you chose and why, what challenges arose (hallucination, latency, cost, inconsistent outputs), how you evaluated whether the system was working, and what you would do differently with hindsight. What sets strong answers apart is engineering judgment, not just “I called the OpenAI API.” Show that you thought carefully about trade-offs, handled failures, and measured outcomes.

Thread: Model Landscape and Selection

  1. Walk me through the major categories of model architectures. When would you choose one over another?

    Three main architecture types:

    • Encoder-only models (BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT) process the full input bidirectionally and produce rich representations of each token. They excel at understanding tasks: text classification, sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, and generating embeddings for retrieval. They cannot generate text.

    • Decoder-only models (GPT, LLaMA, Mistral, Falcon) generate text autoregressively, one token at a time. They dominate general-purpose use because, at sufficient scale, a single decoder stack handles both understanding and generation. These are what people usually mean by “LLMs.”

    • Encoder-decoder models (T5, BART, mT5) combine both: the encoder builds a representation of the input, and the decoder generates output from it. They were widely used for translation and summarization but have been largely displaced by decoder-only models for general tasks.

    As a rule of thumb: for retrieval and embedding, encoder-only models are still the standard. For generation and general-purpose tasks, decoder-only dominates. Encoder-decoder models remain useful in specialized pipelines, particularly for translation and structured sequence-to-sequence tasks.

  2. When would you choose a closed-source API model over an open-source model you host yourself?

    Five factors affect this decision:

    1. Performance ceiling: frontier API models (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) still lead on the hardest reasoning tasks, though the gap is narrowing.

    2. Data privacy: self-hosted models keep all data on your infrastructure, which matters for regulated industries.

    3. Cost structure: API costs scale linearly with usage (pay per token); self-hosted has high fixed costs (GPUs, engineering) but lower marginal costs at scale.

    4. Customization: fine-tuning requires open weights.

    5. Latency control: self-hosted gives full control over serving infrastructure, batching, and caching.

    Many production systems use both: route straightforward queries to a smaller self-hosted model and escalate complex ones to a frontier API. The evaluation challenge is defining the boundary between “straightforward” and “complex” for your specific use case.

  3. How would you decide which model handles which query?

    A common goal in production systems is to send easy queries to a cheaper model and reserve the more expensive model for cases that truly need it. In practice, there are three common routing strategies.

    Classifier-based routing: Train a small, fast model to predict properties such as query difficulty, domain, or expected failure risk, and then route the query accordingly. This can be the most accurate approach, but it depends on having labeled examples and a clear definition of what makes a query “hard” in your application.

    Confidence-based routing: Send the query to the cheaper model first, then evaluate its answer. If the model’s confidence is too low, or if a separate evaluator gives the response a poor quality score, escalate the query to a stronger model. This is often easier to set up than training a dedicated router, but it increases latency for the queries that need escalation.

    Rule-based routing: Use deterministic rules based on features such as query type, length, domain, or user tier. This is the most lightweight option and works well when the categories are easy to distinguish.

    In practice, the hardest part is usually not the routing logic itself. The harder question is how to decide whether the cheaper model’s answer is good enough. That requires clear evaluation criteria for your application, which is why routing and evaluation are closely connected in real systems.

Thread: LLM Lifecycle and System Thinking

  1. Walk me through the stages of building an LLM from scratch.

    There are four main stages:

    • Stage 1: Pretraining. Train on a massive, diverse corpus (web, books, code, articles) using next-token prediction. The result is a foundation model that understands language patterns broadly but cannot follow instructions or behave helpfully. This stage requires enormous compute: hundreds of GPUs for smaller models, up to tens of thousands for frontier ones, running for weeks or months.

    • Stage 2: Fine-tuning. Adapt the foundation model on a smaller, targeted dataset. The most common form is instruction tuning, training on instruction-response pairs so the model learns to follow directions, answer questions, and produce useful outputs. This stage can also include domain adaptation (specializing for medicine, law, or a specific language) and task-specific tuning (optimizing for summarization or classification).

    • Stage 3: Alignment. Shape the model’s behavior using human preferences, typically through RLHF or DPO. The goal is a model that is helpful, honest, and harmless: it follows instructions, refuses unsafe requests, and avoids generating misleading content.

    • Stage 4: Deployment. Serve the model to users via APIs or applications. This involves quantization for efficiency, serving infrastructure for throughput and latency, cost optimization, and monitoring for drift or failures.

    Evaluation is not a fifth stage but a continuous process that runs across all four: data quality audits during pretraining, benchmark evaluations during tuning, safety audits during alignment, and performance monitoring after deployment.

  2. What is the difference between fine-tuning for alignment and fine-tuning for a specific task?

    Task-specific fine-tuning aims to improve performance on a particular application, such as medical question answering, legal document analysis, or code generation. The model is trained on domain-specific input-output examples, and success is usually measured with task metrics such as accuracy, F1, or ROUGE.

    Alignment fine-tuning aims to shape behavior. The goal is to make the model more helpful, honest, safe, and responsive to instructions. Instead of training only on task examples, alignment often relies on preference data, such as judgments that one response is better than another. Success is evaluated through human feedback, safety benchmarks, and behavioral tests.

    The two serve different purposes and often happen in sequence: first task-specific tuning to build capability, then alignment tuning to shape behavior. The data format differs too: task tuning uses input-output pairs, while alignment uses pairwise comparisons or ratings. A model can perform well on a task while still being poorly aligned, for example by producing confident but unsafe medical advice. That is why both stages matter.

  3. What would you evaluate at each stage?

    Evaluation is not a single step performed at the end of development. It changes as the model moves through its lifecycle, because each stage has a different goal and therefore requires different metrics.

    During pretraining, the focus is on whether the model is learning general language patterns from large-scale data. Common checks include perplexity on held-out text, which measures how well the model predicts unseen tokens, as well as data coverage across domains and languages. Teams also examine the training corpus for bias and test whether the model is memorizing specific examples instead of learning patterns that generalize.

    During fine-tuning, the focus shifts from general language modeling to task performance. At this stage, you typically use task-specific metrics such as accuracy, F1, BLEU, or ROUGE, depending on the application. You also evaluate instruction-following quality: does the model actually carry out the user’s request correctly? Just as importantly, you run regression checks to make sure improvements on one task do not degrade performance on others.

    During alignment, the emphasis moves to helpfulness, harmlessness, and behavioral quality. Common evaluations include human preference judgments, safety benchmarks that test whether the model appropriately refuses harmful requests, sycophancy tests that measure whether the model agrees too readily with the user, and over-refusal tests that check whether the model is blocking benign requests that it should answer.

    After deployment, evaluation becomes operational as well as model-centric. You monitor latency, throughput, and cost per query, since a strong model that is too slow or expensive may not be practical. You also track user satisfaction, hallucination rates on real queries, drift over time, and adversarial robustness to see whether users are discovering new ways to break the system or bypass its guardrails.

    Note that metrics shift across stages. Perplexity matters in pretraining but tells you nothing about alignment. Chatbot Arena scores matter for deployed chat models but are irrelevant for embedding models. Choosing the right metrics at each stage is itself a core competency. Each stage is covered in depth in the corresponding chapters throughout this book.

  4. If you had to build a customer-facing GenAI product from scratch, what are the primary key decisions you would make first?

    There are three decisions we have to decide on first:

    1. First, define the evaluation criteria before building anything. You need to know what “good” looks like for your use case. Without clear metrics, you cannot tell whether your system is improving or regressing. This means identifying the failure modes that matter most (hallucination? latency? refusal of valid queries?) and building a test set of representative inputs with expected outputs.

    2. Second, choose your model strategy. API, open-source, or hybrid? This constrains everything downstream: cost structure, latency profile, data privacy posture, and customization options. Start with the simplest option that meets your requirements (usually an API) and add complexity only when you have evidence that you need it.

    3. Third, design your retrieval and grounding strategy. Most production applications cannot rely on the model’s parametric knowledge alone. They need RAG, tool access, or structured data integration to be accurate and up-to-date. Deciding what external knowledge the model needs access to, and how to provide it, shapes the entire system architecture.

    These three decisions, evaluation criteria, model strategy, and grounding approach, determine the architecture, cost profile, and quality ceiling of the product. Everything else (prompt engineering, agent design, serving infrastructure) follows from them.
