Chapter 2. Pretrained Models

When we talk about modern generative AI systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or the Llama chat models, it is easy to forget that they were not built from scratch for a single task. They were not originally designed only to chat, summarize documents, or answer questions. Instead, they began as pretrained foundation models: large models trained on massive and diverse datasets of text, code, and other data before being adapted for specific behaviors.

As discussed in Chapter 1, an LLM is shaped in three training stages: pretraining, fine-tuning, and alignment. A fourth phase, inference and deployment, is where the finished model actually serves users; we treat it separately because it does not change the model’s weights. In this chapter we focus on the first stage, pretraining, the stage that produces the pretrained model, often also called a foundation model or base model. Figure 2-1 highlights this stage.

Why Pretraining Matters

Imagine trying to teach someone calculus before they have learned arithmetic. Training a language model for complex reasoning or conversation works much the same way; it will fail without a broad foundation in language and general world knowledge. Pretraining provides that foundation.

Pretraining matters for three main reasons:

  1. General knowledge acquisition. By reading vast amounts of text, from books and news to code and online discussions, the model learns grammar, vocabulary, reasoning patterns, and factual knowledge about the world. This background lets it make sense of new tasks even before it is fine-tuned or prompted for them.

  2. Reusability across tasks. A pretrained model is a reusable base for many downstream applications: summarization, classification, translation, dialogue, and more. Even without fine-tuning, it can often solve a task simply by seeing a few examples in the prompt, a phenomenon known as in-context learning (Brown et al., 2020).

  3. Scalability and efficiency. Pretraining makes the whole workflow economical. Instead of training a separate model from scratch for every task, an organization invests once in a foundation model and adapts it many times. The investment is steep; GPT-3 was estimated to cost roughly $4.6 million in compute for a single training run. Because the resulting weights are reusable, downstream teams build on that one investment through fine-tuning or prompting, which is much cheaper.

In practice, nearly every modern model is built this way. Gemma instruct models are fine-tuned from Gemma base; Llama chat models build on the Llama base; DeepSeek-R1 is trained on top of DeepSeek-V3-Base. Pretraining is not just a preliminary step; it is what makes all later adaptation possible.

Pretraining Datasets

A pretrained model is only as good as the data it learns from. Models are trained on datasets containing hundreds of billions to tens of trillions of tokens. For perspective, the full text of English Wikipedia is around 6 billion tokens (under a modern byte-level BPE tokenizer, as of 2026), and frontier models now train on corpora a thousand times larger. So we could read Wikipedia cover to cover and still have seen well under one percent of what a frontier model consumed during training. Figure 2-2 shows growth of pretraining data over time.

As you can imagine, quantity of data is important, but quality matters more. A carefully curated, filtered corpus routinely beats a much larger but noisier one, which is why the field’s mantra shifted from “bigger is always better” to “better data builds better models.” To that end, several quality-control techniques have become the standard practice:

  • Deduplication, removes exact and near-duplicate text. This stops the model from memorizing repeated passages and wasting compute on redundant tokens.

  • Filtering removes boilerplate, low-quality text, toxic content, and material that violates policy or licensing.

  • Language detection keeps the corpus within the model’s intended linguistic scope.

  • Domain balancing keeps the mix diverse, avoiding over-representation of any single source.

  • Quality classifiers score documents for usefulness, often using a small model trained to imitate human or LLM quality judgments, and up-weight the high-scoring text.

Quality classifiers, in particular, reflect a major recent shift toward selecting and weighting training data based on estimated usefulness rather than treating all collected text equally. As a result, the community has moved from raw web dumps toward heavily filtered corpora. Table 2-2 lists the corporas you are most likely to be asked about in an interview.

A subtle point that interviewers love to probe is that source size is not the same as sampling weight. Filtered Common Crawl made up about 82% of GPT-3’s raw token pool, but it was deliberately down-weighted to 60% of the tokens the model actually trained on, while tiny high-quality sources were up-weighted far above their raw share. Wikipedia, for instance, was under 1% of the raw pool but 3% of the training mix, so the model cycled through it several times.

Note

When you read that a model “trained mostly on web data,” check whether that refers to the raw pool or the sampled mixture; they are usually different.

Data choice also carries legal and licensing weight. Training on copyrighted books, paywalled news, or community content can constrain how a model may later be used. Some organizations now pretrain only on permissively licensed or public-domain data to guarantee commercial usability. Chapter 8 covers these data-processing pipelines in depth; here we focus on why they matter.

How Pretraining Works

The pretraining corpus is far too large to label by hand, so models learn through self-supervised learning where the training signal comes from the raw text itself. In this paradigm, parts of the input are hidden, and the model must predict the missing pieces from the surrounding context. In doing so it gradually absorbs the structure, grammar, and meaning of language, with no human labels at all. See “Learning Paradigms” for more details on this.

There are two dominant ways to set up that prediction task.

  1. Masked language modeling (MLM). Hide a fraction of tokens and let the model use context on both sides to fill them in. BERT and RoBERTa use MLM. The bidirectional view produces strong representations for language understanding tasks such as classification and search.

  2. Causal, or autoregressive, language modeling (CLM). Show the model only the tokens to the left and ask it to predict the next one. The term autoregressive means each prediction is fed back in as input for the following step, so the model generates one token at a time, conditioning on everything it has produced so far. GPT, Llama, Mistral, and Qwen all use this objective. Because generation is the training task itself, autoregressive models are natural text generators.

A third, less common setup, prefix language modeling, reads a prefix bidirectionally and then generates the rest causally; T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer, Raffel, C., et al. 2020) and UL2 (Unifying Language Learning Paradigms, Tay, Y., et al. 2022) use variants of it for sequence-to-sequence work. Chapter 7 covers these objectives and the architectures behind them in detail.

Most modern LLMs use the causal objective because it scales cleanly, aligns directly with how we use the model at inference, and still supports understanding tasks when they are framed as prompts (for example, “Is this review positive or negative?”). An encoder cannot easily be coaxed into generating text; a decoder can be coaxed into understanding. That asymmetry is why the field converged on decoder-only autoregressive models, at least for pure-text LLMs. Multimodal systems still pair a vision (or audio) encoder with a decoder language model, so encoders have not disappeared, they have moved to the perceptual front end.

The pretraining run itself is enormous. Frontier models use clusters of tens of thousands of accelerators for weeks or months; Llama 3.1 405B, for example, was trained on a cluster of roughly 16,000 H100 GPUs. Even smaller open models still take hundreds to thousands of GPUs. Training optimization techniques, discussed in Chapter 9, such as mixed-precision training, gradient accumulation, and distributed parallelism make this large scale training feasible.

The single most useful framing for an interview is that pretraining is an offline, one-time, high-cost process, and everything afterward (fine-tuning, prompting, retrieval augmentation, alignment) is how we amortize that cost. If you can explain why self-supervision removes the need for labels, and why the autoregressive objective won for general-purpose models, you have covered most of what gets asked here.

How Text Becomes Tokens

Before a model can predict the “next token,” we have to decide what a token even is. That choice, tokenization, converts raw text into the integer IDs the model actually processes, and it quietly shapes cost, speed, and even what the model can do.

Why not just use whole words? A word-level vocabulary would have to be enormous to cover every inflection, compound, and proper noun, and it would still break on anything unseen. Going to the opposite extreme, one token per character, avoids that but makes sequences very long, which is expensive because attention cost grows with sequence length. Subword tokenization splits the difference: frequent words stay whole, while rare words decompose into familiar pieces (for example “unhappiness” becomes “un” + “happiness”). With subword tokenization any input in any language can be represented, and the vocabulary stays manageable, typically 32,000 to 128,000 tokens.

The dominant subword tokenization method is byte-pair encoding (BPE), which builds its vocabulary bottom-up: start from individual characters (or bytes), repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair, and merge it into a new token until the vocabulary reaches the target size. Modern decoder-only models (GPT-2 onward, Llama) use byte-level BPE, which starts from raw bytes rather than characters. The payoff is that any possible input, any script, emoji, or unusual symbol, is representable, so there is never an out-of-vocabulary “unknown” token. There are other subword tokenization methods too (WordPiece in BERT, Unigram and SentencePiece elsewhere) that we compare them in the Q&A.

Two consequences are worth holding onto, because interviewers probe them. First, the average number of tokens per word, called fertility, decides how much text fits in the context window and what an API call costs; a tokenizer trained mostly on English is far less efficient on other languages, which is a real fairness and cost issue for multilingual products. Second, because digits and characters often disappear inside larger tokens, models can struggle with arithmetic and with character-level tasks like counting the letters in a word. When a model fails at something that looks trivial, the tokenizer is often the first place to look. Thread 5 of the “Interview Questions and Answers” goes deeper on all of this.

Scaling Laws and Compute

How big should a model be, and how much data should it see? For years this was guesswork. Scaling laws turned it into a budgeting exercise.

Scaling-law experiments at OpenAI (Kaplan et al., 2020) showed showed that loss falls as a smooth power law in three quantities, model parameters, training tokens, and compute, as long as the others are not bottlenecked. For a fixed compute budget, their analysis recommended spending most of it on more parameters and comparatively less on data. That philosophy shaped GPT-3 as a 175 billion parameters model trained on 300 billion tokens.

A later analysis revised that conclusion, finding that the earlier recipe had under-valued data (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The Chinchilla results showed that, for compute-optimal training, parameters and tokens should scale more evenly, roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. By that rule, GPT-3 was substantially undertrained and should have seen closer to 3.5 trillion tokens. DeepMind demonstrated this with Chinchilla, a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens that outperformed the 280B Gopher despite being four times smaller. This shifted the field toward training models on substantially more data rather than simply increasing parameter count.

A handy estimate ties these together. Training a dense transformer costs roughly C≈6ND FLOPs (floating point operations), where N is parameter count and D is the number of training tokens (the factor of 6 is the forward pass plus the roughly twice-as-expensive backward pass). This one formula lets you sanity-check almost any training-budget claim.

But compute-optimal is not the whole story, and this is where modern practice diverges from Chinchilla. Compute-optimal minimizes loss per unit of training compute. It ignores inference. If you will serve a model to millions of users, every parameter is paid for on every request, forever. So labs deliberately overtrain small models far past the Chinchilla point to get a cheaper-to-serve model of a given quality. Llama 3 8B was trained on roughly 15 trillion tokens, on the order of 1,800 tokens per parameter, roughly 90 times the Chinchilla-optimal ratio, precisely because the training cost is paid once and the inference savings recur forever.

Cite both laws and the tension between them. Kaplan (2020) favored parameters; Chinchilla (2022) corrected it to about 20 tokens per parameter for compute-optimal training. Then add the senior-level point: compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal. Models like Llama are overtrained on purpose because training cost is one-time while inference cost recurs. Knowing the C≈6ND heuristic lets you back-of-the-envelope any training run.

Mixture of Experts

As models grew, a structural question became unavoidable: do we really need to run every parameter for every token? Mixture-of-experts (MoE) answers no.

An MoE layer replaces one big feedforward network with many smaller “expert” networks plus a small router. For each token, the router picks the top few experts (often two) and only those run. The model therefore has a large total parameter count (its full capacity) but a much smaller active parameter count (what actually computes per token). You get the knowledge capacity of a huge model at the compute cost of a small one. Figure 2-3 shows the idea.

Mixtral 8x7B made this concrete and is the canonical interview example. It has eight experts per layer and routes each token to two of them. The “8x7B” name is misleading arithmetic: experts share some components, so the model is about 46.7 billion total parameters, of which only about 12.9 billion are active per token (commonly rounded to 47B / 13B). If you say “56 billion,” an interviewer who knows the model will catch it.

By mid-2026 the largest open models are nearly all MoE, separating total from active parameters in their very names. As Table Table 2-3 shows, modern open-weight MoE models vary widely in total parameter count, but only a fraction of those parameters are activated for each token

Evolution of Pretrained Model Families

Progress over the past several years did not follow a single line; it came from several directions at once, architecture, scale, open-weight releases, efficiency, alignment, and multimodality. Figure 2-4 sketches representative milestones.

The story begins with BERT (2018), which showed that bidirectional pretraining sharply improved language understanding. GPT-2 (2019) demonstrated that a decoder-only model could generate coherent long-form text. GPT-3 (2020), at 175 billion parameters, made in-context learning vivid: one general model could perform many tasks from a few prompt examples, without retraining. That was the moment the foundation-model era began.

After GPT-3, the ecosystem widened fast. Meta’s Llama series proved that open-weight models trained on carefully curated data could rival closed systems, igniting a wave of community fine-tuning. Mistral and its MoE successor Mixtral showed that efficient architectures deliver competitive quality at a fraction of the cost. Anthropic’s Claude family emphasized safety and alignment; Google’s Gemini and Gemma lines pushed multimodality and open weights respectively; Alibaba’s Qwen focused on multilingual coverage and a broad open-weight lineup; and DeepSeek showed that architectural and training-efficiency innovations (multi-head latent attention, MoE, FP8 training) could reach the frontier at a fraction of the usual hardware budget. The newest twist, covered in Chapter 12, is the rise of reasoning models (such as DeepSeek-R1) that are post-trained with reinforcement learning to think before answering.

Rather than chase every release, which would date this chapter within months, it is more useful to hold onto the enduring patterns:

  • Scaling laws give predictable returns from more data, parameters, and compute.

  • Decoder-only autoregressive architectures are the default for pure-text LLMs.

  • Efficiency techniques such as MoE and FP8 keep cost growth in check.

  • Alignment methods (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI) make models safe and usable.

  • Reasoning post-training adds a new axis: spending compute at inference time, not just training time.

By the time you read this, there will be newer releases than the ones named here, successive versions of Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama, Qwen, and DeepSeek among them. That is exactly why the next section is about how to look things up rather than memorizing a leaderboard. In an interview, naming the current top model matters far less than being able to reason about why it leads and how you would verify the claim.

When discussing model families in an interview, keep three dimensions separate: training stage, architecture, and access. A model may be a pretrained base model or a post-trained, aligned model; it may use an encoder-only, decoder-only, or encoder-decoder architecture; and its weights may be openly available or accessible only through a closed API. These are independent properties. Keeping them separate helps you avoid common mistakes, such as referring to a downloadable “base GPT-4” when closed providers expose only their post-trained models.

Choosing the Right Pretrained Model

Choosing a model is not about chasing the top benchmark score. The “best” model in the abstract can be entirely wrong for your application. Every project balances capability, cost, latency, openness, and risk, and a systematic approach beats guessing.

Task Requirements

Start from what the model must actually do. A classification task such as sentiment or spam detection may need nothing more than a compact encoder like DistilBERT, fast and cheap. Multi-turn chat needs a decoder LLM that maintains conversational state. Summarization can suit an encoder-decoder like T5. Open-ended generation rewards a large decoder-only model.

Next, ask whether the task emphasizes understanding or generation: encoders excel at the former, decoders at the latter, and some tasks need both. If your output must follow a strict structure (JSON, XML), favor models known for reliable format adherence. If your application involves images, audio, or video, start from a multimodal model.

One 2026-specific caveat is that for genuinely multi-step problems such as math, code, and planning, a smaller reasoning model can often outperform a larger non-reasoning one. “Reasoning-heavy means pick the biggest model” is no longer automatically true; sometimes it means pick a reasoning model. Chapter 12 covers when that trade-off pays off.

Deployment Constraints

In real deployments, practical limits narrow your choice of model before capability does. For example, real-time chat demands low latency, which can rule out the largest models. Similarly, if you need to run the model on-device, it must fit within the hardware’s memory budget, often requiring a smaller model and quantization. To put this in perspective, a 4-bit-quantized 7B–8B model needs only a few gigabytes of memory and can run on sufficiently capable consumer hardware, whereas a 70B model in 4-bit requires roughly 35–40 GB just for the model weights and typically needs much more capable hardware.

Another key decision that shapes your choice of model is whether to self-host an open-weight model or call a managed API. Self-hosting gives control over infrastructure, data locality, model customization, and cost at high volume. Managed APIs, on the other hand, remove operational burden and often add enterprise features such as data-retention controls, system and organization controls 2 (SOC 2), health insurance portability and accountability act (HIPAA), or general data protection regulation (GDPR) support, depending on provider and plan. Another big difference is inference cost. A self-hosted model can be cheaper at high, steady utilization but inefficient at low utilization, while per-token API pricing can be economical at low volume but expensive at scale.

Licensing and Openness

Licensing is the hidden constraint. Need commercial rights? That rules out research-only models. Want to fine-tune and host privately? You need a model whose weights you can download and whose license permits it. Open-weight families such as Llama, Mistral, Qwen, and Gemma can provide that flexibility, while closed API models such as GPT, Claude, and Gemini trade some of it for convenience. Always read the actual license. Apache 2.0 and MIT are permissive, while some open-weight licenses impose conditions on commercial use and others may prohibit it altogether. Do not assume an entire model family shares the same license; variants can differ.

A Practical Decision Framework

A pragmatic process for choosing the right pretrained model involves five steps and is shown in Figure 2-5.

  1. List essential requirements and separate must-haves from nice-to-haves.

  2. Apply hard constraints first. Apply latency, cost, and licensing to eliminate non-starter models before deep testing.

  3. Test two or three candidates on your own data. Measure both qualitative factors (consistency, fluency, reasoning) and quantitative ones (accuracy, token efficiency) to pick the best models.

  4. Estimate total cost of ownership, including infrastructure, maintenance, and integration, not just per-token price.

  5. Plan for iteration: design your system so swapping the underlying model is a configuration change, not a rewrite.

Many teams prototype quickly with a frontier API, validating the product before worrying about infrastructure, then migrate to an open-weight model for cost control once usage stabilizes. Others start with open models if they have in-house ML expertise. There is no single right answer, only the right trade-off for your constraints.

“How would you choose a model and a host for production?” is a common system-design question. A strong answer treats model selection and hosting as separate decisions: choose the model based on quality for your task, using your own evaluation set and sanity-checking against public leaderboards such as Artificial Analysis or LMArena. Then choose the host based on latency, price, and performance consistency, using sources such as Artificial Analysis for speed and price and Vellum for P50/P95 latency. Finally, decide between self-hosting and an API based on volume, privacy, and customization needs. For local deployment, you can also name the serving engine: Ollama for single-user workloads and vLLM for concurrent serving.

Summary

Pretrained models are the foundation of modern generative AI. They give us starting points that already understand language, facts, and reasoning patterns, capabilities that would be prohibitively expensive to rebuild per task. Pretraining is the one-time, high-cost stage; fine-tuning, prompting, retrieval augmentation, and alignment are how we amortize it.

The field has matured from a handful of closed models into a rich ecosystem. Open-weight families (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral, Gemma, Phi) publish base checkpoints you can adapt; closed providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google’s Gemini) ship only finished, aligned products. Mixture-of-experts now dominates the largest open models, separating total capacity from active per-token compute. Scaling laws explain the size-versus-data trade-off, and deliberate overtraining reflects the reality that inference cost recurs while training cost is paid once.

Success depends on understanding trade-offs rather than chasing a single “best” model. The next chapters build directly on this foundation: prompt engineering (Chapter 3), then fine-tuning (Chapter 10), then retrieval-augmented and agentic systems (Chapter 4).

Interview Questions and Answers

Thread: The Pretrained Model Landscape

  1. What is a pretrained language model, and why does pretraining matter?

    A pretrained language model is a neural network, almost always a transformer, trained on a massive corpus of unlabeled text using a self-supervised objective such as next-token prediction. It learns grammar, factual knowledge, reasoning patterns, and stylistic conventions, all without task-specific labels.

    Pretraining matters for three reasons. First, knowledge acquisition: exposure to trillions of tokens teaches the model far more about the world than any single downstream dataset could. Second, reusability: one pretrained model can be adapted to summarization, classification, translation, code, and dialogue through fine-tuning or prompting, which amortizes the enormous training cost across many applications. Third, emergent capabilities: at sufficient scale, models develop abilities that were never explicitly trained, such as in-context learning, which GPT-3 demonstrated and GPT-2 could not, despite sharing the same objective.

    GPT-3’s training run was estimated at around $4.6 million in compute (a third-party 2020 estimate of GPU time, not the full project cost). Because the weights are reusable, downstream teams build on that one investment for thousands of dollars (fine-tuning) or essentially nothing (prompting). Pretraining is the foundation the rest of the pipeline assumes.

  2. What are the main pretraining objectives, and how do they differ?

    Three objectives dominate. Causal language modeling (CLM), also called autoregressive modeling, predicts the next token from all preceding tokens; the model sees only leftward context, which matches how we generate text at inference, one token at a time. GPT, Llama, and Mistral use it, which makes them natural generators. Masked language modeling (MLM) masks a fraction of tokens (about 15% in BERT) and predicts them from both sides; the bidirectional view yields strong representations for understanding tasks but cannot generate fluently. Prefix language modeling treats a prefix bidirectionally and the rest causally; T5 and UL2 use variants for sequence-to-sequence tasks.

    The field converged on CLM for general-purpose models. A decoder trained causally can still handle understanding tasks through prompting (“Is this positive or negative?”), whereas an encoder cannot easily be made to generate. CLM also scales cleanly: one attention pattern, one loss, and direct alignment with the generation use case.

  3. What is the difference between encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder-decoder architectures?

    These are three ways to wire a transformer; Chapter 7 covers the internals. Encoder-only models (BERT, RoBERTa) attend bidirectionally and excel at understanding, classification, embeddings, extraction, but cannot generate autoregressively. Decoder-only models (GPT, Llama, Mistral, Qwen) attend only to previous tokens via causal masking and are built for left-to-right generation; by framing a task as “produce the next tokens,” they also handle understanding through prompting. Encoder-decoder models (T5, BART) read the input bidirectionally and generate the output while cross-attending to it, which fits sequence-to-sequence tasks like translation.

    Modern general-purpose LLMs are almost exclusively decoder-only, because one architecture scales efficiently and handles both understanding and generation. Encoder-only models remain the best choice for embedding-based search, where generation is unnecessary.

  4. Why have decoder-only autoregressive models dominated the LLM landscape?

    Several factors converged. The objective is unified: causal language modeling is the only loss needed, whereas encoder-decoder models require more complex objectives and two modules to orchestrate. Scaling behavior favors decoders: few-shot in-context learning and chain-of-thought appeared most clearly in GPT-3 and its successors, which were all decoders, while encoder-only models like BERT were never scaled comparably. Generality comes for free, because any task can be cast as text generation, eliminating task-specific heads. And the infrastructure is simpler: one causal self-attention path, one forward pass, one KV cache at inference.

    The caveat is “for pure-text LLMs.” Encoder-decoder models still win on some structured tasks like machine translation, encoder-only models still produce the best sentence embeddings, and multimodal systems pair a vision or audio encoder with a decoder language model. Decoders won the general-purpose text crown, not every crown.

  5. What is a foundation model, and how does it relate to a pretrained model?

    The term foundation model, introduced by Stanford’s HAI center in 2021, describes a model trained on broad data at scale that can be adapted to many downstream tasks. Every modern LLM that serves as a starting point qualifies. A pretrained model becomes a foundation model when it is general enough to support many applications: a Llama base model is a foundation model, while a small BERT fine-tuned only for sentiment is pretrained but not usually called one, because it was specialized from the start.

    The distinction frames how the industry divides labor. Foundation-model providers invest in one expensive pretraining run; downstream teams adapt the result through fine-tuning, prompting, or retrieval. The foundation model is shared infrastructure; everything on top is application-specific.

  6. What is the difference between open-weight and truly open-source models, and which providers release base checkpoints?

    Open-weight means the trained parameters are downloadable; you can run, fine-tune, and deploy the model, but you may not have the training code, data, or full recipe. Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral, Gemma, and Phi are open-weight. Open-source in the strict sense means the full code, recipe, and data are available so you could reproduce the model; very few LLMs meet that bar (OLMo and BLOOM are notable exceptions).

    A closely related and very common interview point: closed providers do not release base checkpoints at all. OpenAI (GPT), Anthropic (Claude), and Google’s Gemini line ship only the final aligned model behind an API; there is no downloadable “base GPT-4” or “base Claude.” So when a question hinges on fine-tuning your own base model, the realistic options are the open-weight families. Always check the specific license too, and do not assume an entire family shares one (variants differ); some are fully permissive (Apache 2.0), some allow commercial use only under conditions, and some are research-only.

Thread: Scaling Laws and Training Decisions

  1. What are the Kaplan scaling laws, and what did they reveal?

    Kaplan et al. (2020) at OpenAI measured how language-model loss depends on three quantities: parameters N, training tokens D, and compute C. Each follows a power law when the others are not bottlenecked. The practical takeaway was that, for a fixed compute budget, you should make the model substantially larger and increase data more slowly. This shaped GPT-3’s design (175B parameters on 300B tokens) and the 2020-2022 “scale the parameters” era. Their lasting contribution was giving the field a way to predict performance before paying for a training run.

  2. How did the Chinchilla scaling laws change the field?

    Hoffmann et al. (2022) at DeepMind found Kaplan’s recipe had under-valued data. The compute-optimal balance scales parameters and tokens equally, roughly 20 tokens per parameter. By that standard GPT-3 was significantly undertrained; it should have seen around 3.5 trillion tokens. DeepMind proved it by training Chinchilla, a 70B model on 1.4T tokens, which beat the four-times-larger 280B Gopher on most benchmarks. The field’s priorities shifted from “build it bigger” to “feed it more and better data,” and open models like Llama followed the data-heavy recipe.

  3. What does “compute-optimal” mean, and why do labs deliberately overtrain past it?

    Compute-optimal training allocates a fixed compute budget between model size and token count to minimize training loss. Chinchilla’s answer was a roughly equal split, about 20 tokens per parameter.

    The catch is that compute-optimal ignores inference. Training compute is spent once; inference compute is spent on every request for the life of the model. So if you will serve a model at scale, you deliberately overtrain a smaller model far past the Chinchilla point, paying more in training to get a cheaper-to-serve model of the same quality. Llama 3 8B trained on roughly 15 trillion tokens, on the order of 1,800 tokens per parameter, roughly 90 times the Chinchilla-optimal ratio, and still matched larger models that saw less data. The lesson: Chinchilla-optimal minimizes loss per training FLOP, overtraining minimizes loss per inference FLOP, and the right choice depends on whether training or serving cost dominates.

  4. How do you estimate the compute needed to train a model?

    The standard approximation for a dense transformer is C≈6ND FLOPs, where N is parameters and D is training tokens; the 6 is the forward pass (about 2ND) plus the backward pass (about 4ND). For example, a 65B model on 1.4T tokens is about 6×65×109×1.4×1012≈5.5×1023 FLOPs. Divide by your cluster’s sustained throughput, accounting for 40-60% real utilization, to get wall-clock time. Chapter 9 covers parallelism and utilization in depth. The value of the formula in an interview is that it lets you sanity-check any “we trained X on Y GPUs” claim on the spot.

  5. How should you weigh data, parameters, and compute against each other?

    All three matter and none fully substitutes for another. Too few tokens and a large model memorizes instead of generalizing; too little capacity and the model underfits no matter how much data it sees; more compute helps only if split wisely between the two. Recent evidence has tilted emphasis toward data quality: Microsoft’s Phi models showed that small models trained on filtered, “textbook-quality” data can match much larger models trained on noisier web text. The practical framing is a triangle of parameters, data, and compute; scaling laws tell you how to move through it efficiently, and Chinchilla showed the field had been moving through it poorly by over-investing in parameters.

Thread: Mixture of Experts

  1. What is a mixture-of-experts model, and what problem does it solve?

    A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model replaces a dense feedforward layer with many smaller “expert” networks plus a small router that selects only the top few experts (often two) for each token. The result is a model with a large total parameter count (its full knowledge capacity) but a much smaller active parameter count (what actually runs per token). It decouples capacity from compute: you get the knowledge of a huge model at roughly the inference cost of a small one. That is why nearly every frontier-scale open model in 2026 (Llama 4, DeepSeek-V3, the Qwen3 MoE variants) is an MoE.

  2. Mixtral 8x7B is often quoted as “56B parameters.” Is that right?

    No, and it is a common trap. Mixtral 8x7B has eight experts per layer and routes each token to two of them, but the experts share components such as attention, so you cannot just multiply 8 by 7. The correct figures are about 46.7 billion total parameters and about 12.9 billion active per token (often rounded to 47B / 13B). The “56B” number comes from naively computing 8x7, which double-counts shared weights. Stating the correct totals signals you understand how MoE parameter counting actually works.

  3. What is the main engineering challenge in training an MoE, and how is it handled?

    Load balancing. The router learns which experts to use, and left unchecked it tends to favor a few, so most tokens pile onto a handful of experts while the rest sit idle, wasting the capacity that motivated MoE in the first place. The standard fix is an auxiliary load-balancing loss that penalizes uneven routing, nudging the router toward roughly equal expert utilization; some recent models use a bias-adjustment scheme instead to avoid the auxiliary loss interfering with the main objective. Other practical challenges include the memory cost of holding all experts (you store the full total parameter count even though you compute with a fraction) and the communication overhead of routing tokens to experts that may live on different devices.

Thread: The Frontier and How to Track It

  1. What are the current frontiers in pretraining and model development?

    Several trends define the frontier as of 2026. Mixture-of-experts at scale has become the default for the largest open models, separating total capacity from active compute. FP8 mixed-precision training has been used at frontier scale (DeepSeek-V3) to cut training cost. Data-centric pretraining continues, with quality classifiers and curated open corpora (FineWeb, Dolma) replacing raw web dumps, and high-quality data annealed in near the end of training. Multimodality pairs vision and audio encoders with decoder LMs. And the biggest recent shift, covered in Chapter 12, is reasoning post-training: models like DeepSeek-R1 are trained with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR, often using GRPO) to produce a thinking phase before answering, which introduces a second scaling axis, inference-time compute, on top of training-time scale.

  2. The model landscape changes monthly. How do you stay current and compare models in practice?

    You do not memorize a leaderboard; you learn which board answers which question. For quality, the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index and LMArena (human-preference Elo) give cross-model rankings. For cloud-API speed, price, and the fact that the same open model differs across hosts (Groq vs Together vs Fireworks vs Bedrock), Artificial Analysis tracks tokens-per-second and price over a rolling window. For production tail-latency stability (P50 vs P95 under load), Vellum’s provider tracker. For local hardware speed and memory under quantization, the Hugging Face LLM-Perf leaderboard. And for local serving, the engine choice itself matters: Ollama for single-user local use (it wraps llama.cpp and runs well on Apple Silicon’s unified memory), vLLM for concurrent serving (PagedAttention plus continuous batching of many simultaneous requests).

    The senior move is to add the caveats: human-preference Elo can reward style over substance, a latency board may test from one location rather than your users’ regions, and benchmark contamination inflates accuracy numbers. Use leaderboards as a first filter, then confirm on your own evaluation set and workload.

  3. How reliable are benchmarks like MMLU and HumanEval for comparing models?

    Useful as a coarse filter, but limited. Contamination: if benchmark data leaked into pretraining, scores inflate, and at web scale some leakage is nearly inevitable. Narrow coverage: MMLU tests factual knowledge across 57 subjects and HumanEval tests 164 Python problems, neither captures multi-turn reasoning, long context, instruction following, or safety. Saturation: top models now exceed 90% on several classic benchmarks, so small differences are within noise, which is why newer, harder benchmarks keep appearing. Gaming: models can be tuned to a benchmark without real-world gains. The practical recommendation is to use public benchmarks to build a shortlist, then evaluate candidates on your own domain data. Chapter 5 covers building robust evaluation pipelines.

Thread: Tokenization

  1. What is tokenization, and why is it a critical design decision?

    Tokenization converts raw text into integer IDs the model processes; every input passes through it and every output is decoded back. It defines the model’s atomic units of language. Word-level tokenization needs an enormous vocabulary and still fails on unseen words; character-level avoids that but produces very long sequences, which is costly because attention scales quadratically with length. Subword tokenization splits the difference: common words stay whole, rare words decompose into familiar pieces (“unhappiness” becomes “un” + “happiness”), and any input in any language can be represented while the vocabulary stays manageable (typically 32K-128K tokens).

    The choice ripples through the whole system. Vocabulary size sets the embedding and output-layer dimensions; the average tokens-per-word ratio (fertility) determines how much text fits in the context window; and API pricing is per token, so an inefficient tokenizer inflates both cost and latency.

  2. How does Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) work, and what is byte-level BPE?

    BPE (Sennrich et al., 2016) builds a vocabulary bottom-up. Start from individual characters (or bytes), count every adjacent pair in the corpus, merge the most frequent pair into a new token, and repeat until you reach the target vocabulary size. Frequent sequences become single tokens early; rare ones stay split into pieces. At inference the learned merges are applied greedily.

    Byte-level BPE, used by GPT-2 onward and by Llama, starts from raw bytes rather than Unicode characters. The payoff is that any possible input, any script, emoji, or unusual symbol, is representable as a sequence of byte tokens, so there is never an out-of-vocabulary “unknown” token. This is why modern decoder-only models handle arbitrary text gracefully. OpenAI’s tiktoken is a widely used fast byte-level BPE implementation. Vocabularies have grown over time, from GPT-2’s ~50K toward 128K+ in recent Llama and other models, because larger vocabularies compress text better (fewer tokens per word) at a modest embedding-parameter cost that is negligible for large models.

  3. How do WordPiece, Unigram, and SentencePiece differ from BPE?

    WordPiece (BERT) is similar to BPE but chooses merges to maximize training-data likelihood rather than raw frequency; continuation pieces are marked with “##”. Unigram (Kudo, 2018) works top-down: start from a large candidate vocabulary and prune the tokens whose removal least hurts a unigram language model, keeping pieces that earn their place. SentencePiece is not an algorithm but a framework that operates on the raw byte/character stream with no pre-tokenization into words, so it handles languages without whitespace (Chinese, Japanese, Thai) gracefully; it can run BPE or Unigram internally. The compact summary: BPE merges bottom-up by frequency, WordPiece merges bottom-up by likelihood, Unigram prunes top-down by likelihood, and SentencePiece is a language-agnostic wrapper around either.

  4. How does tokenization affect multilingual models and overall behavior?

    A tokenizer trained mostly on English learns English-centric merges, so other languages, especially non-Latin scripts, fragment into many more pieces. This fertility gap means a sentence that is 20 tokens in English might be 60 in Hindi, which triples the effective cost, shrinks how much non-English text fits in the context window, and gives the model fewer semantic units per training example in that language. Fixes include training the tokenizer on a balanced multilingual corpus, using byte-level or SentencePiece tokenizers, and growing the vocabulary; Qwen, for instance, was designed multilingual from the start and shows more uniform fertility.

    Tokenization also shapes behavior beyond efficiency. Because digits are often merged (“2024” as one token), arithmetic and character-level tasks (“how many r’s in strawberry?”) are harder, since the model never sees individual digits or letters. Tokenization is deterministic, so small wording changes (“ChatGPT” vs “Chat GPT”, trailing spaces) shift token boundaries and can change outputs. When a model fails on a seemingly trivial task, the tokenizer is often the first thing worth inspecting.

Thread: Synthetic Data, Bias, and Practical Deployment

  1. What role does synthetic data play in pretraining, and what is the risk of model collapse?

    Synthetic data, text generated by an existing model, has become a major ingredient. Microsoft’s Phi models are the headline example: trained heavily on filtered, “textbook-quality” synthetic data, a 14B model (Phi-4) matches much larger ones on reasoning and code. Synthetic data lets you target gaps, generate clean instructional text, and distill a larger model’s behavior into a smaller one.

    The risk is model collapse. Shumailov et al. (2024, in Nature) showed that when models are trained recursively on their own (or other models') output, without enough fresh human data, quality degrades over generations: the distribution narrows, tails are lost, and the model forgets rare but real patterns. Dohmatob et al. (2024) and others analyzed the same effect. The practical guardrail is that synthetic data works as a supplement to a strong base of human data and careful filtering, not as a wholesale replacement for it. An interviewer raising synthetic data is often probing whether you know this failure mode, not just the upside.

  2. How do you handle bias and fairness in pretrained models, and what does alignment actually fix?

    Bias enters wherever data reflects human prejudice or imbalance, and web-scale corpora contain plenty. Mitigation starts in data collection (filtering, deduplication, representation balancing) and continues after deployment (auditing, monitoring, feedback).

    A precise distinction earns points here: alignment methods such as RLHF and Constitutional AI mainly reduce harmful output, what the model says, but they do not necessarily remove bias in the underlying representations, what the model has internalized. A model can be trained to refuse slurs while still encoding stereotyped associations that surface in subtler ways. Measuring representational bias needs dedicated probes and benchmarks (for example BBQ for question-answering bias, or BOLD for generation), not just a check that the model avoids obviously toxic text. Fairness is an ongoing maintenance process, not a one-time filter.

  3. When should you use a base model versus an instruction-tuned model?

    A base model is the raw output of pretraining; it completes text but does not follow instructions. Prompt it with “Summarize this article” and it might continue with more instruction-like text rather than actually summarizing, because that is what it saw in training. An instruction-tuned (chat) model has been further trained on instruction-response pairs and usually aligned, so it interprets prompts as tasks.

    Use the instruction-tuned model for most applications, chatbots, QA, document processing, anything where the model must follow user intent. Use the base model when you intend to fine-tune for a specific downstream task, because starting from the base avoids an instruction “persona” that can fight your target behavior. Remember, though, that this choice only exists for open-weight families; closed providers expose only the aligned product. Chapter 10 covers picking the right starting checkpoint.

  4. How do you choose between a closed API and a self-hosted open model?

    Closed APIs (GPT, Claude, Gemini) give the highest capability with zero infrastructure overhead and transparent per-token pricing, ideal for prototyping and low-to-moderate volume. Self-hosted open models (Llama, Qwen, Mistral) require GPU infrastructure and MLOps skill but give control over privacy, latency, customization, and cost at scale.

    The crossover is mostly about volume: at low volume, API costs are modest and self-hosting overhead is not worth it; at high, steady volume, self-hosting becomes dramatically cheaper. Other factors can override the math: regulated industries may be unable to send data to an external API at all; fine-tuning for domain behavior requires open weights; co-located self-hosting removes network round-trips; and API providers can change pricing or terms. A common production pattern is hybrid, route complex, low-volume queries to a frontier API and high-volume, simpler queries to a self-hosted model. The Serving chapter in the book’s GitHub repository https://aiengineeringinterviews.github.io/ covers model routing and cost optimization in more detail.
