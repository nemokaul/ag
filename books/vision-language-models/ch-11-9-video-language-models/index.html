Chapter 9. Video-Language Models

The when and how of events unfolding over time is what separates interpreting a photo from understanding a story: the world doesn’t stand still. What is the deer in Figure 9-1 doing in the street? Looking at the multiple frames, we see it is in a park, and people are not scared but are enjoying observing and interacting with it.

Videos contain everything images do plus time. The temporal dimension is deceptively expensive: 300 frames per 10-second clip, quadratic attention costs that explode with sequence length, and motion patterns that require understanding not just what objects are but how they move. A strong image classifier might nail every frame individually but completely miss the action that ties them together.

In this chapter, you will learn how video-language models tackle these challenges. You will see how temporal modeling mechanisms capture motion and event sequences, how attention strategies scale to handle hundreds of frames while fitting in a GPU, and how to combine embedding and generative models into practical pipelines that work at scale.

By the end of this chapter, you will:

  • Understand how video models evolved from 3D convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to modern transformer architectures and why factorization is the recurring design principle

  • Know how temporal attention mechanisms (factorized, hierarchical, cross-modal) and position encoding let models reason about time

  • Choose between embedding models and generative models based on your task, and understand the cost implications of each

  • Build a complete Video-RAG pipeline: segment, embed, index, rerank, and generate grounded answers

  • Fine-tune a video-language model on your domain data using QLoRA with video-specific considerations like frame sampling and label masking

Grab some popcorn, and let’s teach your models to understand the time dimension!

Text-to-video retrieval

Now let’s search videos using text queries. This is the video equivalent of CLIP (which you met in Chapter 1): map both modalities into a shared embedding space and compute similarity scores.

Here you will use Qwen3-VL-Embedding, a modern vision-language embedder that handles video natively:

# Qwen3-VL-Embedding ships a helper script in its model repo.
# We download it and import the Qwen3VLEmbedder class directly.

script_path = hf_hub_download(
repo_id="Qwen/Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B",
filename="scripts/qwen3_vl_embedding.py",
)
spec = importlib.util.spec_from_file_location("qwen3_vl_embedding", script_path)
mod  = importlib.util.module_from_spec(spec)
spec.loader.exec_module(mod)
sys.modules['qwen3_vl_embedding'] = mod
Qwen3VLEmbedder = mod.Qwen3VLEmbedder
embedder = Qwen3VLEmbedder(
model_name_or_path="Qwen/Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B",
torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16
)
videos = [spaghetti_mp4, minecraft_mp4]
# Embed one text query and two videos in a single call.
# The embedder returns L2-normalized vectors, so dot product = cosine similarity.
inputs = [
{"text": "a person eating spaghetti"},
{"video": videos[0], "fps": 1.0, "max_frames": 32},
{"video": videos[1], "fps": 1.0, "max_frames": 32},
]
emb = embedder.process(inputs)  # (3, D) tensor, L2-normalized
q = emb[0:1]   # query embedding   (1, D)
V = emb[1:]    # video embeddings  (2, D)
scores = (V @ q.T).squeeze(1)
rank   = scores.argsort(descending=True).tolist()
print("Query:", inputs[0]["text"])
for i in rank:
print(f"  score={scores[i].item():.3f}  video={videos[i].split("/")[-1]}")

If you run it, at the output, you will see:

Query: a person eating spaghetti
  score=0.750  video=eating_spaghetti.mp4
  score=0.164  video=09KmKSz4r_Y.mp4

The pattern is simple: embed query, embed videos, dot product, rank. In “Video-Language Models in Practice”, you will see how to scale this to thousands of videos with FAISS indexing and retrieve-then-rerank pipelines.

Captioning, summarization, and video QA

These three tasks use the same generative pattern. You feed the model a video and a text prompt, and it generates natural-language output. The only difference is what you ask for:

  • Captioning: “Describe what happens in this video.”

  • Summarization: “Give me the key moments in three bullet points.”

  • QA: “What is the person doing?”

Modern video-language models handle all three with a unified chat interface. Let’s explore this with SmolVLM2:

model_id  = "HuggingFaceTB/SmolVLM2-500M-Video-Instruct"
processor = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained(model_id)
model = AutoModelForImageTextToText.from_pretrained(
   model_id,
   dtype=torch.bfloat16,
   device_map="auto"
).eval()
def ask_video(video_path: str, prompt: str, num_frames: int = 8) -> str:
   messages = [{
      "role": "user",
       "content": [
           {"type": "video", "path": video_path},
           {"type": "text",  "text": prompt},
       ],
   }]
   inputs = processor.apply_chat_template(
       messages,
       tokenize=True,
       add_generation_prompt=True,
       return_dict=True,
       return_tensors="pt",
       do_sample_frames=True,
       num_frames=num_frames,
   ).to(model.device)
   if "pixel_values_videos" in inputs:
       inputs["pixel_values_videos"] = inputs["pixel_values_videos"].to(dtype)
   with torch.no_grad():
       out = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=128,
do_sample=False)

prompt_len = inputs["input_ids"].shape[-1]
return processor.batch_decode(
out[:, prompt_len:], skip_special_tokens=True
)[0].strip()

# Captioning
print("CAPTION:\n", ask_video(minecraft_mp4,
"Write a one-sentence caption for this video."))

# Summarization
print("\nSUMMARY:\n", ask_video(minecraft_mp4,
"Summarize the video in 3 bullet points."))

# Video QA
print("\nQA:\n", ask_video(spaghetti_mp4,
"What is the person doing? Answer briefly."))

Notice the generative pattern: build chat messages with {"type": "video"} + {"type": "text"}, call apply_chat_template() with do_sample_frames=True to let the processor sample frames from the video file, then model.generate(). You will use this exact pattern again when we build Video-RAG pipelines in “Video-Language Models in Practice”.

You have now seen videos through the prism of different models. You might notice that a generative model could technically cover all the cases: classify by asking, “Which of these labels best describes the video?” and then retrieve by generating descriptions and matching them. So why bother with specialized models? The answer comes down to cost. An embedding model produces one vector per video, which you compute once and reuse forever. A generative model runs full autoregressive decoding for every query-video pair. At 10,000 videos, that difference is the gap between milliseconds and hours. This distinction between embedding models and generative models is the single-most important architectural decision you will make when building video-language pipelines, and it’s what drives the rest of “Video-Language Models in Practice”.

From Images to Video: Core Concepts

Let’s introduce the most important video concepts that remain constant across approaches. Pack your suitcase, because we are traveling in time.

In the prehistory of the transformer, when CNNs were highly popular for images, the first attempts to understand video were essentially extending those CNNs to an additional dimension: time. This feels exciting. Let’s look at it in more detail.

What can we learn from 3D CNNs?

As you learned in Chapter 1, a standard 2D convolution slides a filter over a single image. 3D convolution slides it over a block of consecutive frames. Instead of detecting “edge at this position,” you detect “hand moving left over these eight frames.” Motion becomes a pattern that the network can learn directly from raw pixels. That was a brilliant idea but costly:

  • The 3D convolution is expensive: a simple CNN that’s ~5 million parameters for images becomes ~33 million when extended to 3D for video.

  • You are relearning what CNNs already know by ignoring it and training from scratch.

Those costs were quickly answered with clever efficiency tricks. Here, you can see two of them:

  • (2+1)D convolution (Figure 9-2) became the workhorse solution: instead of one big 3D convolution, split it into two operations. First, apply 2D convolution within each frame (spatial processing). Then apply 1D convolution across frames (temporal processing). You have factored the problem and dramatically reduced the number of parameters while preserving expressive power.

  • Inflated 3D (I3D) took a different approach: start with a 2D CNN pretrained on images and “inflate” its filters to 3D by copying weights across the temporal dimension, then fine-tune on video. You’re transferring image knowledge efficiently rather than learning from scratch.

Note

The key insight that survives to today: you don’t need to model all space-time relationships jointly. Factorizing spatial and temporal processing is often more efficient without sacrificing performance. Modern transformer architectures still use this exact principle, just with attention instead of convolution.

While 3D CNNs learned motion implicitly through spatiotemporal patterns, some approaches explored making motion a first-level citizen. Let’s explore that in search of more learning!

Motion as an explicit signal

Some researchers in the field made a bold claim: motion deserves its own processing pipeline, and a very good example of it (literally) is the two-stream model approach. As you can see in Figure 9-3, the architecture is split into two parallel branches, one of them being focused on temporal changes in the video.

Here are the two branches:

  • Branch 1, or spatial stream, processes regular video frames:. “What objects are present?”

  • Branch 2, or the temporal stream, processes optical flow: maps showing exactly how pixels move between frames.

Each branch is a chain of CNNs, and both streams are fused at the end.

Note

If this is the first time you read optical flow, know that it is something quite simple: a way to encode motion. For example, if a hand moves from position (50, 100) in frame t to (55, 103) in frame t + 1, the flow vector at (50, 100) encodes that displacement: [+5, +3].

Static backgrounds have zero flow [0, 0]. Moving objects have large flow vectors.

Two-stream models proved that optical flow provides complementary insights to video frames. This model could easily distinguish between doors opening and closing.

The practical problem? Computing optical flow was painfully slow, and deploying those models was a nightmare.

Note

The key insight that endures today: appearance and motion are fundamentally different signals that warrant different processing. Modern video transformers honor this principle, though they learn motion representations implicitly rather than computing optical flow explicitly.

Everything changed when transformers arrived, and now you have the foundations to understand why.

Attention over time (the transformer revolution)

Transformers had already revolutionized natural language processing and image understanding through self-attention: every token can attend to every other token, learning relationships directly from data. For video, this was interesting (at least intellectually): imagine each frame attending to every other frame, learning temporal dependencies end to end.

This approach had a major problem: vanilla self-attention has a quadratic cost in sequence length. For text (hundreds of tokens) or images (hundreds of patches), that’s manageable. For video, let’s just take 300 frames, each divided into 196 patches. That’s 58,800 tokens. A quadratic cost implies 3.4 billion pairwise comparisons in a single attention layer. Your GPU just caught fire.

What can we learn from the prehistory of CNNs? What if you factorize attention just like you factorized convolution? Early video transformers like TimeSformer and ViViT demonstrated the solution.

Instead of attending to all space-time positions at once:

  • Apply spatial attention within each frame (each patch attends to other patches in the same frame).

  • Apply temporal attention across frames (each patch attends to the same spatial position in other frames).

This reduces complexity from O((H × W × T)²) to O(H × W × T × (H × W + T)), where H × W is the spatial resolution, and T is the number of frames. For our 300-frame video, that’s the difference between 3.4 billion comparisons and 17 million. Not bad; your GPU survives.

OK, now you may ask: what if my video is longer than 300 frames? For longer videos, hierarchical attention emerged: break the video into segments, process each segment independently with full attention, then attend between segment-level summaries. An hour-long video is divided into 120 thirty-second segments. Each segment is manageable; 120 segment summaries are trivial to attend to.

Note

The patterns behind factorized processing and hierarchical aggregation are evident in every modern video-language model we discuss. They are the fundamental strategies for making video tractable.

In this section, you reviewed innovations that made the VLMs we have today possible. Let’s take a look at how this combination of language and video emerged and evolved.

The Evolution to Video-Language Models

The innovations that you reviewed in “From Images to Video: Core Concepts” advanced video understanding tasks like recognizing actions or detecting events, but they didn’t connect video to language in a meaningful way. That final leap came from combining video encoders with language models. Let’s take a look at Figure 9-4.

 Here are some of the models depicted in Figure 9-4:

  • CLIP (2021) you should know well! It revolutionized image-language modeling. It learned a joint embedding space in which images and their captions are close together. Though CLIP was trained only on static images, its features proved surprisingly effective for video tasks when applied frame-by-frame.

  • VideoCLIP (2021) extended CLIP’s contrastive approach directly to video-text pairs, introducing temporal information during pretraining. Instead of matching individual frames to text, it matched video clips to descriptions. “A person opens a door” becomes grounded in actual motion, not just static frames.

  • LanguageBind (2023) and InternVideo (2022–2024) advanced the integration of multiple modalities (video, audio, depth) into unified representation spaces. InternVideo2 showcased the power of combining three training objectives: masked modeling (predicting missing frames), contrastive learning (matching videos to text), and next-token prediction (generating captions). The synergy between these approaches created stronger video-language foundations.

  • Qwen2-VL, VideoLLaMA, Apollo, and other modern video large language models (LLMs) follow a generative paradigm: a video encoder (often built on pretrained image encoders with temporal modules added) feeds representations into an LLM decoder. This enables rich interactions, enabling video question answering, detailed captioning, instruction following, and multiturn conversations about video content.

You can see the evolution: from frame-based CLIP features to proper temporal encoding to full generative models that can reason about video in natural language. Each step built on the previous, maintaining the core principles while adding capability.

With these foundations in place, let’s dive deeper into the heart of video understanding: how modern models actually handle time through temporal modeling mechanisms.

Temporal Modeling

You have seen how video models evolved from CNNs to transformers, and you have run inference on real tasks. Now comes the crucial question: how do video-language models actually understand time?

Just to give you a sense of the complexity that time adds to the equation: your video model needs to connect frame 1 (person lights fuse) to frame 200 (explosion), distinguish fast action (basketball dunk) from slow motion (time-lapse sunset), ignore 9 minutes of boring content to focus on 10 seconds of relevant action, and understand that frame order matters (shuffling frames should break everything).

This is temporal modeling. Let’s explore its challenges.

Deciding where model attention goes is key to those challenges, especially in transformers, which provide a flexible way to relate any part of the video to any other.

Unlike a fixed-size convolution or the one-step-at-a-time long-short-term memory (LSTM), a self-attention layer can learn long-range dependencies in one go: each frame’s representation can directly attend to frames before or after it (even far away) if needed. This helps connect distant temporal parts of the video because the model isn’t constrained to a local temporal neighborhood. Attention can learn to focus on keyframes by assigning higher weights to them within a task (this is effectively what learned temporal attention does in many video transformers: some frames will receive higher attention scores). Also, multi-head attention (MHA) can capture different aspects of temporal relations: one head might focus on short-term motion, another on global context.

There is only one issue: the amount of data in a video (24 or more frames per second). Vanilla attention has a quadratic cost in the number of tokens (and therefore frames). For long videos, this is problematic (100 frames would mean 10,000 pairwise comparisons in a single layer). How do you tackle this? You will learn about it in “Attention Mechanisms for Video”, which covers mechanisms for temporal modeling and cross-modal attention.

Attention Mechanisms for Video

Remember the factorization principle from “From Images to Video: Core Concepts”? We separated spatial and temporal processing in 3D CNNs to cut costs. The same principle saves us here: instead of attending to all space-time at once, we factorize attention.

Spatiotemporal attention: Three strategies

Modern video models use three main approaches for spatiotemporal attention, each with different cost-performance trade-offs:

Fully joint space-time attention

This is the simplest approach, serving as a baseline for other methods. In this approach, models treat each image patch in each frame as a separate token and apply MHA on the whole video.

Factorized attention

This is the most pragmatic and widely used because it gets the best balance between performance and computational feasibility. You apply attention along one dimension at a time (spatial dimension, then temporal dimension, or vice versa). This reduces computation while still coupling the information.

Hierarchical attention

Factorized attention gets you to 300 frames. But what about hour-long videos? What about 108,000 frames at 30 fps? Hierarchical attention breaks the video into segments:

  1. Process each 30-second segment (900 frames) independently with factorized attention. This produces one segment representation vector per segment.

  2. Apply attention between the 120 segment vectors (for an hour-long video).

Now that you have reviewed the three main approaches to spatiotemporal attention, let’s see it in action. VideoLLaMA3 in Figure 9-5 shows how modern models combine those concepts as part of the architecture:

Dynamic resolution input (left)

Handles spatial processing within frames independently. Different frames can have different resolutions based on content

Dynamic vision tokens

Performs temporal factorization by comparing only consecutive frames, pruning redundant tokens—if frames t and t + 1 are nearly identical (static background), drop the redundant tokens

This combines factorized attention (spatial, then temporal) with smart token pruning (relevance filtering) to further reduce costs.

Cross-modal attention: When language guides vision

Another temporal modeling aspect is how language and video interact. For example, in video question answering, the model might use the question to attend to the relevant frames in which the action in the question occurs. This is often done by cross-modal attention: the question features act as queries and the video frames act as keys/values in an attention computation, or vice versa. This way, temporal modeling is guided by language: the model learns which parts of the temporal sequence correspond to the text.

Cross-modal attention is key in tasks like “find the segment of video described by a sentence”: the model needs to align the textual description to the correct time window in the video.

Temporal position encoding

Here is a detail that’s easy to overlook but essential: self-attention doesn’t know about order. Shuffling the frames and vanilla attention produces the same output. That’s a problem when you’re trying to understand temporal sequences. So what’s the solution? Positional encoding, borrowed from transformers for text.

For each frame, add an encoding that represents its position in time:

  • Frame 0 gets encoding e₀

  • Frame 1 gets encoding e₁

  • Frame 299 gets encoding e₂₉₉

These encodings get added to the patch embeddings before attention, giving the model a “timestamp” for each token. There are many ways to actually implement this:

Learned embeddings

Train a lookup table of position vectors

Sinusoidal encoding

Use fixed sin/cos functions of different frequencies

Text-formatted strings

Encode “frame 15” or “00:00:15” as text tokens

It might sound mundane, but without this, your model can’t distinguish “person enters room” from “person exits room” because both have identical frames in different orders.

Putting it all together

Let’s connect everything you have learned. A modern video-language model combines all these mechanisms:

  • Vision encoder (ViT or 3D CNN) extracts patch features from frames.

  • Temporal position encoding is added to give frames timestamps.

  • Factorized attention encodes the video (spatial within frames, temporal across).

  • Text decoder uses cross-attention to generate captions, attending to relevant video frames at each word.

Let’s see this concretely in SmolVLM2 (from Chapter 2). Table 9-2 maps SmolVLM2’s architecture to the temporal mechanisms you just learned.

Not all video-language models are built the same way, and choosing the wrong family for your task is the most expensive mistake you can make. The choice comes down to a single question: what does the model output (see Table 9-3)?

Video-RAG (what YOU will build in “Video-RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Video QA”)

Now you know which family model to use to solve your problem. As a savvy person once told me, understanding how things work will help you make better decisions in the long term. Very briefly:

Embedding models are trained with contrastive objectives

Place similar video-text pairs together in the embedding space, push nonmatching pairs apart. The result is a model that produces a single fixed-size vector per input. In practice, these models enable you to compute each video’s embedding once, store it, and reuse it forever.

Generative video-language models are trained with autoregressive objectives

Predict the next token given everything before it. The output is variable-length text, and the functionalities are way more diverse: those models can reason, explain, and follow complex instructions. It has a crucial operational cost: every query-video pair requires a full forward pass through the language model. You can’t precompute the answer to a question you haven’t asked yet.

Imagine you have 10,000 video segments and a user asks, “Which clip shows someone opening a gift?” Here is how each family in Table 9-3 solves the problem:

  • With an embedding model, you embed the query (one forward pass) and dot product against 10,000 stored vectors (milliseconds).

  • With a generative model, you feed each of the 10,000 segments plus the question through the LLM and generate an answer (10,000 forward passes).

  • With a hybrid pipeline, you use the cheap embedding model to narrow 10,000 candidates down to 5, then use the expensive generative model to reason deeply over those 5. Retrieval handles breadth; generation handles depth. This retrieve-then-reason pattern is exactly what you’ll build in “Video-RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Video QA”.

Note

If this reminds you of how text-based retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) works (retrieve relevant documents, then generate an answer grounded in them), that’s because it’s the same principle. Video-RAG just replaces “text chunks” with “video segments” and “text embeddings” with “video embeddings.” The engineering patterns transfer directly.

Retrieval Pipelines That Scale

In “Video-Language Tasks”, you embedded two demo videos and computed similarity. That works for a proof of concept. In production, you need to handle thousands of videos, many of which are minutes or hours long. This section shows you the engineering patterns that make retrieval work at scale.

The workflow has four steps:

  1. Segment videos into short clips (the unit of retrieval).

  2. Embed each segment offline.

  3. Index embeddings for fast approximate search.

  4. Query with text to retrieve the most relevant segments.

Remember that the full pipeline is ready to execute in the Chapter 9 notebook.

Step 1: Segment videos

Whole-video embeddings are too coarse. A 10-minute video about cooking might contain 30 seconds of chopping, 2 minutes of frying, and 7 minutes of plating and talking. If someone searches for “chopping vegetables,” a single embedding for the entire video buries that signal.

The solution: split videos into short segments (5–10 seconds is a good default) and embed each one independently. Here’s a practical segmenter using ffmpeg:

import os
import subprocess
from pathlib import Path
from typing import List

def segment_video(
video_path: str, out_dir: str, segment_seconds: int = 5
) -> List[str]:
"""
   Split a video into fixed-length MP4 segments using ffmpeg.
   Returns sorted list of segment file paths.
   """
out_dir_path = Path(out_dir).resolve()
out_dir_path.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
pattern = str(out_dir_path / "seg_%05d.mp4")

subprocess.run(
[
"ffmpeg", "-y",
"-i", video_path,
"-c", "copy",            # stream copy — fast, no re-encoding
"-map", "0",
"-f", "segment",
"-segment_time", str(segment_seconds),
"-reset_timestamps", "1",
pattern,
],
check=True,
stdout=subprocess.DEVNULL,
stderr=subprocess.DEVNULL,
)
# Return absolute paths for the segments
return sorted(str(p.resolve()) for p in out_dir_path.glob("seg_*.mp4"))
Note

Five-second segments work well for action-dense content (sports, cooking, surveillance). For slower content, such as lectures or nature documentaries, 10–15 seconds is more appropriate. The goal is for each segment to contain roughly one meaningful “event.” Shot-based segmentation (splitting at visual transitions) gives the best results but requires more tooling. We went for the practical: fixed-length segments are a strong default.

Step 2: Embed segments and build a FAISS index

Now embed each segment and store the vectors in a FAISS index for fast approximate nearest-neighbor search. This code builds on the Qwen3-VL-Embedding embedder you saw in “Video-Language Tasks”:

"""Embed a text query. Returns (D,) numpy array, L2-normalized."""
emb = embedder.process([{"text": text}])
return emb[0].detach().cpu().float().numpy()

def embed_video_segment(
path: str, fps: float = 1.0, max_frames: int = 32) -> np.ndarray:
"""Embed a video segment. Returns (D,) numpy array, L2-normalized."""
emb = embedder.process([{"video": path, "fps": fps, "max_frames": max_frames}])
return emb[0].detach().cpu().float().numpy()

#Segment, embed, and index our demo videos

all_segments: List[str] = []

)  # (N, D)

# Build FAISS index. Since embeddings are L2-normalized,
# inner product (IndexFlatIP) equals cosine similarity.
index = faiss.IndexFlatIP(vectors.shape[1])
index.add(vectors.astype(np.float32))

def search_segments(query: str, k: int = 5) -> List[Tuple[float, Dict]]:
"""Retrieve top-k segments for a text query."""
q = embed_text(query)[None, :]  # (1, D)
scores, ids = index.search(q.astype(np.float32), k)
return [
(float(s), segment_meta[i])
for s, i in zip(scores[0], ids[0])
if i >= 0
]

Scaling Beyond IndexFlatIP

For our two demo videos, exact search is fine. At 100K+ segments, switch to an approximate index like faiss.IndexIVFFlat or faiss.IndexHNSWFlat for sublinear search time. The embedding and indexing pattern stays identical; you only swap the FAISS index type.

Step 3 (optional): Retrieve then rerank

Embedding-based retrieval is fast but approximate: cosine similarity in a shared space is a rough proxy for “how relevant is this segment to this query?” For higher precision, add a reranking stage.

The idea is simple: use fast retrieval to pull the top-K candidates (say, 20–50), then use a more expensive cross-encoder model to rescore those K pairs precisely. The cross-encoder sees the full query and video together, not just their independent embeddings, so it can make finer distinctions.

Qwen3-VL-Reranker is designed exactly for this role:

import importlib.util
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download

# Download and import the reranker helper (same pattern as the embedder)
rerank_script = hf_hub_download(
repo_id="Qwen/Qwen3-VL-Reranker-2B",
filename="scripts/qwen3_vl_reranker.py",
)
spec = importlib.util.spec_from_file_location("qwen3_vl_reranker", rerank_script)
rerank_mod = importlib.util.module_from_spec(spec)
spec.loader.exec_module(rerank_mod)

reranker = rerank_mod.Qwen3VLReranker(
model_name_or_path="Qwen/Qwen3-VL-Reranker-2B",
)

def rerank_segments(
query: str, candidates: List[Dict], fps: float = 1.0, max_frames: int = 32
) -> List[Tuple[float, Dict]]:
"""Re-score retrieved segments with a cross-encoder for higher precision."""
payload = {
"query": {"text": query},
"documents": [{"video": c["segment_path"]} for c in candidates],
"fps": fps,
"max_frames": max_frames,
}
scores = reranker.process(payload)  # list[float], aligned with candidates
return sorted(zip(scores, candidates), key=lambda x: x[0], reverse=True)

# Two-stage retrieval
query = "someone eating pasta"

# Stage 1: fast approximate retrieval (embedding + FAISS)
stage1 = search_segments(query, k=10)
candidates = [meta for _, meta in stage1]

# Stage 2: precise reranking (cross-encoder)
ranked = rerank_segments(query, candidates)

print(f"Query: {query}")
print("Top reranked segments:")
for score, meta in ranked[:5]:
print(f"  rerank_score={score:.3f}  seg={meta['segment_path']}")

When is reranking worth the cost? If your top five retrieval results already look perfect, skip it. But when you have ambiguous queries (“the part where things go wrong”), closely competing segments, or your application demands high precision (medical video search, legal discovery), reranking is the cheapest way to improve quality without changing your embedding model.

Video-RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Video QA

This is where embedding models and generative models come together. Video-RAG is the most practical pattern for answering questions over long videos or large video libraries, and it follows directly from the key technical difference you learned in “Picking the Right Output Mode”:

  1. Retrieve the most relevant segments using the fast embedding pipeline (handles breadth).

  2. Generate an answer using a video-language model, feeding it only the retrieved segments (handles depth).

You already have all the pieces. The retrieval index from “Retrieval Pipelines That Scale” handles step 1.

For step 2, you’ll use SmolVLM2:

def answer_with_segments(
segment_paths: List[str],
question: str,
num_frames: int = 2, # Reduced num_frames further to ensure consistency
max_new_tokens: int = 128,
) -> str:
"""
   Feed multiple retrieved video segments plus a question to SmolVLM2.
   Each segment becomes a separate {"type": "video"} entry in the
   chat message, so the model sees all of them as context.
   """
content = [{"type": "video", "path": p} for p in segment_paths]
content.append({"type": "text", "text": question})

messages = [{"role": "user", "content": content}]

inputs = rag_processor.apply_chat_template(
messages,
tokenize=True,
add_generation_prompt=True,
return_dict=True,
return_tensors="pt",
do_sample_frames=True,
num_frames=num_frames,
).to(rag_model.device)

if "pixel_values_videos" in inputs and torch.is_floating_point(
inputs["pixel_values_videos"]
):
inputs["pixel_values_videos"] = inputs["pixel_values_videos"].to(dtype)

with torch.no_grad():
out = rag_model.generate(
**inputs, do_sample=False, max_new_tokens=max_new_tokens
)
prompt_len = inputs["input_ids"].shape[-1]
return rag_processor.batch_decode(
out[:, prompt_len:], skip_special_tokens=True
)[0].strip()

Now, the full pipeline: retrieve and then answer:

# Here is the pipeline - first retrieve and then answer

def video_rag(question: str, k: int = 5) -> str:
"""
   End-to-end Video-RAG: retrieve relevant segments, then generate
   a grounded answer using only those segments as context.
   """
# Step 1: Retrieve (fast — embedding + FAISS)
retrieved = search_segments(question, k=k)
segment_paths = [meta["segment_path"] for _, meta in retrieved]

# Step 2: Generate
# (expensive — but only over k segments, not the whole library)
prompt = (
"Answer the question using ONLY the provided video clips. "
"If the clips do not contain enough information, say so.\n\n"
f"Question: {question}"
)
answer = answer_with_segments(segment_paths, prompt)

print(f"Question: {question}")
print(f"\nRetrieved {len(segment_paths)} segments:")
for score, meta in retrieved:
print(f"  score={score:.3f}  {meta['segment_path']}")
print(f"\nAnswer: {answer}")
return answer

video_rag("Which food do you see in the video?", k=5)

Note

How many segments should you retrieve? This depends on context window limits and the nature of your question. For factual questions (“What color is the car?”), two to three segments usually suffice. Fine-Tuning a Video-Language Model for Your Domain

Pretrained Video-LMMs are strong generalists, but domain-specific data can make a dramatic difference. If your videos are surgical procedures, factory inspections, or drone surveys, the model has likely seen very few examples during pretraining. Fine-tuning bridges that gap.

This section shows you how to fine-tune SmolVLM2 with QLoRA—yes, the same parameter-efficient approach you used in Chapter 5 but with video-specific considerations.

What’s different from image-VLM fine-tuning?

Actually, most of the workflow transfers directly. The video just introduces three new concerns:

Frame sampling policy

You must decide num_frames at training time, and ideally it should match what you will use at inference. Sampling too few frames misses relevant actions; too many frames flood the context window and slow training. Eight frames is a good starting point for short clips (under 30 seconds).

Memory pressure from video tensors

Each training example contains num_frames full-resolution images stacked into a pixel_values_videos tensor. A batch of 4 examples, each with 8 frames, is 32 images’ worth of GPU memory. Gradient accumulation is your friend here: keep per_device_train_batch_size at 1–2 and accumulate over 8–16 steps.

Label masking

You want to train only on the assistant’s answer tokens, not on the user prompt or the video tokens. This requires computing the prompt length separately and masking those positions in the label’s tensor. The collator you will run in “Training” handles this correctly.

Dataset format

In this example, we will use the dataset TIGER-Lab/VideoFeedback. If you look into it, the format is quite simple: a JSONL file where each line contains a video path, a question, and the expected answer.

Feel free to craft a tiny dataset yourself, but just keep in mind the format. As an example:

{
    "video": "path/to/clip1.mp4",
    "question": "What does the person pick up?",
    "answer": "A spoon.",
}
{
    "video": "path/to/clip2.mp4",
    "question": "What happens after the door opens?",
    "answer": "A dog runs into the room.",
}

To close our video exploration, let’s look at some ideas to improve efficiency.

Efficiency in Video-Language Models

From 3D CNNs to factorized attention, every generation of video models has been shaped by the fact that video is expensive to process. In this section, you will see some extra practical details to deal with it. The challenges split into two buckets: token efficiency (how many visual tokens reach the language model) and training efficiency (how to afford the GPU bill).

Token Efficiency

You read this many times in this chapter: a single video can produce an enormous number of visual tokens, many of which carry redundant information. Unlike text, where each token typically contributes new semantic content, adjacent video frames are often nearly identical, making naively encoding every frame wasteful. Since self-attention cost is quadratic in sequence length, every extra token hits you twice: once in memory, once in compute.

Moreover, vision encoders output lower-dimensional embeddings (often 512–1,024 dimensions) compared to language models (commonly ~4,096 dimensions), so feeding visual tokens into an LLM requires projecting them into a higher-dimensional space. We used to use a linear projector to map all visual tokens to the LLM’s embedding size. This ensured compatibility but created an information bottleneck: projecting hundreds of tokens per frame flooded the LLM with high-dimensional tokens, many of which were redundant. In short, videos inherently produce too many tokens, and each frame is more costly to represent than text.

How can you reduce the visual token load before it reaches the language model? Here are some important strategies:

Spatial/temporal token pooling

Many Video-LMMs reduce the number of tokens by downsampling. For example, they might shuffle and drop some spatial tokens per frame or average features across N frames. See Figure 9-6 for more details.

Frame selection and frame efficiency

Rather than encoding every frame uniformly, intelligent frame sampling can improve efficiency. A new trend is to select only the most informative frames based on the text prompt or an agent’s reasoning. For example, VideoAgent in Figure 9-7 uses an LLM as a controller to iteratively choose which video frames to “look at” next, focusing on frames likely to contain the answer. This connects directly to the relevance challenge from Table 9-1: in a 10-minute video where only 2 seconds matter, smart frame selection avoids wasting compute on the other 9 minutes and 58 seconds.

Text-conditioned token resampling

Going beyond frame dropping, fine-grained token resampling methods use the text prompt to decide which visual tokens to keep. One of the first approaches, LLaMA-VID, utilized a Q-Former to downsample using the original text question. While both text-conditioned approaches are more efficient at a single-turn level, videos need to be resampled at each conversational turn, so as the number of turns and their complexity increase, this token efficiency goes down.

Training Efficiency

Training large video-language models is extremely resource-intensive. Even for small video LLMs (two to three billion parameters), we are talking about dozens of powerful GPUs working 24/7 for weeks. Some of them will already sound familiar:

Scaling consistency (efficient downscaling)

You already met this concept in Chapter 4 (“Ablations and Evaluation”), where Apollo’s team showed that mixture decisions made on 2–4B parameter models transfer reliably to 7B+ models once you hit roughly 500K training samples. The same principle applies to architectural decisions specific to video: you can tune the frame sampling rate, temporal attention design, or modality fusion method on a cheap 2–3B proxy and be confident that the winner will remain the winner at full scale. This shrinks the experimentation loop by roughly 10x, because you only train a large model once, with proven settings.

Low-precision and quantization strategies

In Chapter 6, you saw how QLoRA uses NF4 quantization to squeeze multibillion-parameter VLMs onto consumer GPUs for fine-tuning. The same idea extends to training from scratch. For example, NVILA (NVIDIA’s Efficient VLM family) uses FP8 mixed precision for matrix multiplications during both forward and backward passes. Using 8-bit floating point with appropriate loss scaling enables NVILA to increase batch sizes and training throughput on modern GPUs without compromising convergence. The trade-off between precision and memory that Chapter 6 introduced for post-training also applies here, just at a different scale.

Smarter training with better data

You don’t always need massive amounts of data: you need the right data. The EAGLE-2 project showed that smart data selection can be more effective than simply collecting more videos. Instead of grabbing everything available, they followed a “diversity first, then quality” approach: gather training examples from many different sources (180+), then carefully select and refine the best ones to maximize value from each sample.

With token pruning keeping sequence lengths manageable and scaling consistency keeping experimentation affordable, you now have the full picture: how video models represent time (“Temporal Modeling”), how they are built (“Video-Language Models in Practice”), how they are trained (“Efficiency in Video-Language Models”), and how to make all of that fit within a realistic compute budget.

Summary

You started this chapter knowing that a video is just “images plus time.” Now you understand why that innocent-sounding “plus time” kept you busy for dozens of pages.

The practical landscape splits into two families, and picking the wrong one is the most expensive mistake you can make. Embedding models produce one vector per video, computed once and reused forever. Generative models produce rich text but require a full forward pass for every query-video pair. The winning pattern might be to combine both. Retrieve with embeddings (which handle breadth), generate over just the top candidates (which handle depth). That’s Video-RAG, and you built it from scratch: segment, embed, index, rerank, then feed survivors to SmolVLM2 for grounded answers.

Fine-tuning transfers almost directly from image-VLMs. QLoRA, chat templates, and adapter merging: the workflow carries over. Video just adds three concerns—frame sampling policy, memory pressure from stacked video tensors, and label masking—so you train only on answer tokens.
