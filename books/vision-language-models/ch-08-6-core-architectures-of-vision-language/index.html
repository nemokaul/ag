Chapter 6. Core Architectures of Vision Language Models

In the previous chapters, you explored the history of vision language models (VLMs), looked at the different tasks they can perform, and learned how to benchmark their capabilities. You also trained them from scratch on large-scale datasets and post-trained them to steer specific behaviors. Now it’s time to open them up and see how they actually work inside.

In this chapter, we will explore the inner workings of VLMs. We will go past general ideas and look at how these models are built. How are visual and textual information combined? What common designs do people use when building these models? How do these design choices affect how well and how quickly a VLM can work? We will answer these questions and look at the main parts and strategies used in modern VLM designs.

Learning Objective

By the end of this chapter, you will clearly understand how vision and language parts work together in VLMs and know the common design patterns that make these models powerful.

The Key to Combining Information: Multimodal Attention

The core of many successful VLMs is the attention mechanism. If you’ve heard about transformers, you’ve likely heard about attention. Attention first became popular in language processing because it helps models understand how different words interact. In VLMs, attention helps the model find and connect important parts of visual and text data. Understanding attention is important because it’s a big reason why modern VLMs perform so well, especially when they use strong vision and language models that already exist.

The idea behind attention is surprisingly intuitive. Imagine you’re reading this sentence: “The AI community building the future.” When you read the word “AI,” your brain doesn’t treat every other word equally. You instinctively connect it more to “future” and “community” than to “the.” Attention lets models do the same thing: for each element in a sequence, figure out which other elements matter most and blend their information accordingly.

To do this, the model converts each element (a word, an image patch) into three vectors:

Query (Q)

Represents what the model wants to know about a specific element

Key (K)

Determines how relevant each element is to the others

Value (V)

Contains information about the element itself

These three vectors are produced by multiplying the element’s embedding by three learned weight matrices (W_q, W_k, W_v). So for a sentence with 6 words, each word’s embedding gets multiplied by these matrices, producing 6 queries, 6 keys, and 6 values.

The attention computation then has three steps:

1. Finding connections

The model compares each query against every key by taking their dot product. A high dot product means “these two elements are relevant to each other.” For our sentence, this produces a 6 × 6 matrix of raw scores: how much does each word care about each other word?

2. Assigning importance

These raw scores get passed through the softmax function, which turns each row into a probability distribution. softmax is used here (rather than simply dividing by the sum) because it sharpens the distribution: high scores get amplified and low scores get suppressed, letting the model focus crisply on the most relevant elements.

3. Creating a new representation

Finally, the model mixes all values based on their scores. This creates a new, richer representation of each word. The word “AI,” for example, becomes something like 0.6 × AI + 0.1 × community + 0.3 × future. It has absorbed information from the elements it attended to.

Here is the full operation (Equation 6-1) in one line.

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QKTdk)V

With this foundation, we can now look at the two main flavors of attention used in VLMs: self-attention and cross-attention.

Self-Attention: Finding Relationships Within a Sequence

In self-attention, the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence. The sentence attends to itself. This is the mechanism at the heart of every transformer, whether it’s processing text (BERT, GPT) or image patches (vision transformers):

def self_attention(x, W_q, W_k, W_v):
   Q = x @ W_q
   K = x @ W_k
   V = x @ W_v

scale = K.size(-1) ** 0.5
scores = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1) / scale
weights = torch.softmax(scores, dim=-1)
return weights @ V

While self-attention was first used within a single modality, it can also handle multimodal input. If you concatenate visual and text tokens into one long sequence, self-attention will naturally learn to relate image patches to words. This is the key insight behind the unified sequence VLM architecture we’ll see in “The Unified Sequence Approach: Self-Attention”.

Cross-Attention: Bridging Two Different Streams

Cross-attention is a way of getting different modalities like text and images to talk to each other. Think of it as one type of data “interviewing” another.

The difference from self-attention is simple but important: the queries come from one stream (say, text), while the keys and values come from a different stream (say, image features):

def cross_attention(text_features, image_features, W_q, W_k, W_v):
   Q = text_features @ W_q          # queries from text
   K = image_features @ W_k          # keys from image
   V = image_features @ W_v          # values from image

scale = K.size(-1) ** 0.5
scores = Q @ K.transpose(-2, -1) / scale
weights = torch.softmax(scores, dim=-1)
return weights @ V

This allows the text features to select the most relevant bits from the image features. It’s a direct bridge that enables focused information exchange between modalities.

Because Q and K/V come from different sequences, they don’t need to have the same length. This is useful beyond just bridging modalities: cross-attention can also serve as a compression mechanism. If you use a small, fixed set of learned queries to attend to a large set of image features, you get a compact summary of the image. This is exactly how models like Flamingo use a perceiver resampler, and we’ll see it in action in “The Adapter Approach: Cross-Attention”.

Modern VLM Blueprints: Connecting Pretrained Models

As transformers revolutionized natural language processing and then computer vision (with vision transformers, or ViTs), they were the natural choice to power the next generation of VLMs. Their success is built on a simple but powerful idea: everything can be a sequence of tokens. For VLMs, we have two types of tokens:

Text tokens

A sentence is broken down into a sequence of word or subword tokens.

Visual tokens

An image is typically divided into a grid of patches, and a vision encoder (like a ViT) processes these patches into a sequence of feature vectors. Each vector acts as a visual token, representing a piece of the image.

With both modalities speaking the same “language of tokens,” the transformer’s attention mechanism can get to work modeling the complex relationships within and between them.

However, the true paradigm shift for VLMs wasn’t just using transformers but was how we started building them. Instead of training a massive, monolithic model from scratch, the community asked: Why reinvent the wheel?

The most successful and resource-efficient approach today is to take two powerful, pretrained models—a state-of-the-art vision encoder that already understands images and a sophisticated large language model (LLM) that already handles language well—and teach them to communicate. This leverages the billions of parameters and immense knowledge already baked into these models.

This brings us to the central architectural question for any modern VLM designer: What is the best way to build the bridge between the vision model and the language model? The answer to this question has led to two dominant strategies, both of which rely on the attention mechanisms we discussed earlier. Let’s explore them now.

The Adapter Approach: Cross-Attention

Flamingo (see the article by Jean-Baptiste Alayrac et al.) popularized an adapter-style cross-attention architecture for connecting a frozen vision encoder to a frozen LLM. The fundamental idea is to enhance a pretrained LLM without compromising its language abilities. This is achieved by keeping the LLM and a vision encoder frozen (their weights are not updated during training) and only training new, cross-attention layers that connect them. Figure 6-1 (using an image from the Alayrac et al. article) shows the full architecture: images pass through a frozen vision encoder and perceiver resampler on the left, while the LLM stack on the right interleaves frozen layers with new gated cross-attention layers.

The architecture has two key components: a perceiver resampler that compresses visual information, and gated cross-attention layers that inject it into the LLM. Let’s build up from the first to the second.

The perceiver resampler: Compressing visual features

A vision encoder like SigLIP produces a large number of visual tokens, typically one per image patch. A 256 × 256 image with 16 × 16 patches gives you 256 tokens. Feeding all of them as keys and values into cross-attention at every LLM layer would be expensive.

The perceiver resampler solves this by using the compression trick we saw in “Cross-Attention: Bridging Two Different Streams”. It maintains a small, fixed set of learned query vectors (say, 64) and uses cross-attention to let them attend to the full set of vision encoder outputs. The result is 64 visual tokens that summarize the image, regardless of its original resolution. This is the cross-attention mechanism pulling double duty: bridging modalities and compressing information at the same time:

class PerceiverResampler(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, num_queries=64, vision_dim=768, llm_dim=576, num_heads=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.learned_queries = nn.Parameter(
            torch.randn(1, num_queries, vision_dim)
        )
        self.cross_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(
            embed_dim=vision_dim, num_heads=num_heads, batch_first=True
        )
        self.proj = nn.Linear(vision_dim, llm_dim)

Gated cross-attention: Injecting vision into the LLM
With the compressed visual tokens in hand, the next question is: how do we get them into the frozen LLM? Flamingo’s answer is to insert new, trainable gated cross-attention layers directly between the existing frozen LLM layers.
At each of these new layers, the process follows the cross-attention pattern from “Cross-Attention: Bridging Two Different Streams”:
  • The LLM’s current hidden state becomes the query (Q): “Given the text so far, what visual information do I need?”
  • The compressed visual tokens from the perceiver resampler provide the keys (K) and values (V).
  • The cross-attention mechanism compares Q to all visual keys, producing attention scores that highlight the most relevant parts of the image for the current text context.
  • These scores create a weighted average of the visual values, pulling in the relevant image information.
The critical design choice is what happens next. The cross-attention output is multiplied by a learned gating parameter, initialized to zero. This means that at the start of training, the cross-attention layers have no effect on the LLM at all. The model begins training with its language capabilities perfectly intact, and only gradually learns to open the gate as visual information becomes useful. Without this, the randomly initialized cross-attention weights would immediately corrupt the LLM’s pretrained representations:
class GatedCrossAttentionLayer(nn.Module):
   def __init__(self, hidden_dim, num_heads=8):
       super().__init__()
       self.attn_norm = nn.LayerNorm(hidden_dim)
       self.ffn_norm = nn.LayerNorm(hidden_dim)
       self.cross_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(
           embed_dim=hidden_dim, num_heads=num_heads, batch_first=True
       )
       self.ffn = nn.Sequential(
           nn.Linear(hidden_dim, hidden_dim * 4),
           nn.GELU(),
           nn.Linear(hidden_dim * 4, hidden_dim),
       )
       self.attn_gate = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))  # init at 0
       self.ffn_gate = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))   # init at 0

def forward(self, text_hidden, image_features):
# 1. Gated cross-attention
attn_in = self.attn_norm(text_hidden)
attn_out, _ = self.cross_attn(
query=attn_in, key=image_features, value=image_features
)
text_hidden = text_hidden + self.attn_gate.tanh() * attn_out

   <code class="c1"># 2. Gated feed-forward</code>
   <code class="n">ffn_in</code> <code class="o">=</code> <code class="bp">self</code><code class="o">.</code><code class="n">ffn_norm</code><code class="p">(</code><code class="n">text_hidden</code><code class="p">)</code>
   <code class="n">ffn_out</code> <code class="o">=</code> <code class="bp">self</code><code class="o">.</code><code class="n">ffn</code><code class="p">(</code><code class="n">ffn_in</code><code class="p">)</code>
   <code class="n">text_hidden</code> <code class="o">=</code> <code class="n">text_hidden</code> <code class="o">+</code> <code class="bp">self</code><code class="o">.</code><code class="n">ffn_gate</code><code class="o">.</code><code class="n">tanh</code><code class="p">()</code> <code class="o">*</code> <code class="n">ffn_out</code>

   <code class="k">return</code> <code class="n">text_hidden</code></pre>

After this gated fusion step, the representation moves up to the original, frozen self-attention and feed-forward blocks of the LLM layer above. By interleaving these carefully controlled cross-attention steps throughout the LLM, the model continuously grounds its text generation in visual evidence. And because only the cross-attention layers and the perceiver resampler are trained, this approach is highly parameter-efficient and preserves the LLM’s original capabilities. Figure 6-2 shows the full picture: each trainable block (red) sits below the frozen self-attention and feed-forward layers (blue), with residual connections bypassing each component. As you can see, both gate parameters are initialized to zero, so the LLM starts training unchanged.
The Unified Sequence Approach: Self-Attention
What if you could make the LLM treat image features as if they were just more words in a sentence? That’s the core idea behind this increasingly popular unified sequence approach, which we adopted for SmolVLM, as shown in Figure 6-3.
Here’s how it works:
  1. First, the image features from the vision encoder are transformed by a modality projection layer (usually a linear layer) to make them have the same shape as the LLM’s word embeddings. Think of this as translating image patches into “visual words.”
  2. Then, these “visual words” are simply stuck onto the sequence of actual text word embeddings. So you get a single, long sequence: [visual_word1, visual_word2, ..., text_word1, text_word2, ...].
  3. This whole combined sequence is fed directly into the LLM. The LLM’s own existing self-attention layers then process this mixed bag of tokens. It naturally learns to pay attention to text parts, visual parts, or combinations of both, just as it would within a long sentence. It’s a more “hands-off” fusion from our perspective: we set up the input, and the LLM’s self-attention does the rest.
In code, the entire architecture fits in a single class, similar to what we saw in Chapter 3:
class UnifiedSequenceVLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vision_encoder_ckpt, language_model_ckpt, tokenizer,
                 modality_input_dim=768, modality_output_dim=576):
        super().__init__()
        self.vision_encoder = AutoModel.from_pretrained(
            vision_encoder_ckpt
        ).vision_model
        self.modality_projector = nn.Linear(
            modality_input_dim, modality_output_dim, bias=False
        )
        self.tokenizer = tokenizer
        self.llm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(language_model_ckpt)
        self.llm.resize_token_embeddings(len(self.tokenizer))

Compare this to the Flamingo approach (see “The Adapter Approach: Cross-Attention”), which needs a perceiver resampler, gated cross-attention layers, and a custom forward loop through frozen LLM layers. Here, the bridge between vision and language is just one linear projection and a mask replacement. The simplicity is the point.
To handle large images easily, we usually shrink the visual data before combining it with text. In SmolVLM, we used pixel shuffle, as shown in Chapter 3. Pixel shuffle rearranges spatial information into the channel dimension, reducing the number of visual tokens while increasing each token’s dimensionality. After that, the image passes through a linear layer. This makes that layer bigger, but in exchange, it outputs fewer visual tokens. Many recent VLMs, like Qwen2.5-VL, DeepSeek-VL, and Idefics3, have embraced this design.
Comparing Architectures: Which Way to Go?
So, should you use the cross-attention or self-attention approach? As with most things in AI, the best choice can depend on the situation, especially how you plan to train your model.
Our study from Hugging Face (Idefics 2; see the article by Hugo Laurençon et al.) compared these two architectures using the same powerful LLM (Mistral-7B) and vision encoder (SigLIP). The cross-attention VLM (like Idefics) adds significantly more trainable parameters through its perceiver resampler and gated cross-attention layers, while the self-attention VLM (like SmolVLM) only introduces a single linear projection. Here are some things we learned when comparing these two approaches:
Frozen backbones
If the original LLM and vision encoder weren’t changed during VLM training, the cross-attention architecture performed significantly better, scoring 66.7 on an average of four benchmarks (VQAv2, TextVQA, OKVQA, and COCO), while the self-attention model only scored 60.3. With over 6 points separating them, the extra dedicated fusion layers clearly earned their keep when the backbones couldn’t adapt.
Fine-tuned backbones
If parts of the LLM and vision encoder were allowed to learn and adapt during VLM training (using a technique called LoRA to make this efficient), the self-attention architecture pulled ahead, scoring 69.5 on the same four benchmarks, while the cross-attention architecture barely moved, gaining just 0.6 points to reach 67.3.
The Idefics 2 numbers tell a clear story, but there’s nothing like seeing it with code. Let’s take the same backbone, tokenizer, and data slice from Chapter 3 and train both architectures side by side. Both models train all parameters (vision encoder, LLM, and their respective fusion layers), similar to the fine-tuned backbones condition from the Idefics 2 study. We won’t do LORA but full-model fine-tuning since we are using such small backbones.

Even in this simplified view, you can see that the forward pass requires manually iterating through each LLM layer to inject cross-attention at the right points. In a full implementation, this also means explicitly handling details such as causal masks, rotary positional embeddings, and KV cache updates throughout the decoder stack. The unified sequence approach avoids all of this with a single self.llm(inputs_embeds=...) call, because the visual features are already embedded directly into the token sequence. This is the engineering cost of interleaving.

unified_vlm = UnifiedSequenceVLM(vision_ckpt, language_ckpt, tokenizer)

Despite sharing the same LLM and vision encoder, the cross-attention model trains about 34M (15%) more parameters. The perceiver resampler and gated cross-attention layers add up to considerably more than the unified sequence model’s single linear projection.
Using the same training loop and packed batches from Chapter 3, we trained both for 5,000 steps on the same FineVision slice. Figure 6-4 shows the loss curves.
With all parameters unfrozen, both architectures converge to a very similar loss, with the cross-attention architecture consistently performing slightly better. The cross-attention model has 15% more trainable parameters, a perceiver resampler, gated fusion layers, and a considerably more complex forward pass, yet the end result is so close to a single linear projection feeding tokens into the LLM’s self-attention.
This initial comparison is also not perfectly controlled. Because the cross-attention model uses far fewer placeholder image tokens (1 instead of 256), each packed sequence contains slightly more text tokens. In practice, this means it receives more training signal per step. To control for this difference, we kept the 256 image tokens and retrained the cross-attention VLM, leading to Figure 6-5. Once the token budget is matched, the difference in loss almost completely disappears.
This illustrates something important about VLM development: at this scale and training regime, the architecture is not the bottleneck. The data, the learning rate, the batch size, and the amount of training all matter more than how the image gets into the LLM. The model adapting to the task overwhelms whatever advantage one fusion strategy might have over another. And even with carefully curated datasets, the loss will still be dominated by the text portion of the data. This is because most of the tokens we are training on do not require the image. Most of the question doesn’t require the image. However, even more than that, if the answer is, for example, “In this image, we can see a monkey eating a banana,” the only tokens that truly require the image are “monkey eating” and the rest can be inferred from the question and the answer thus far.
This is consistent with the Idefics 2 findings. The architectural difference mattered most with frozen backbones, where the cross-attention model’s extra fusion layers made a real difference (66.7 versus 60.3). Once the backbones could adapt, the gap nearly disappeared. At our baby scale with a tiny data slice, even that residual gap vanishes entirely.
The practical takeaway: when the backbones are trainable, the unified sequence approach achieves nearly identical results while requiring fewer new parameters and far simpler code. That simplicity is exactly why it has become the dominant approach in recent VLMs like Qwen-VL, DeepSeek-VL, and SmolVLM.
Ultimately, choosing your VLM’s attention strategy is a balancing act between simplicity, compute, and preservation of the LLM’s original skills. The self-attention approach is simpler and adds fewer new parameters, but fine-tuning the backbones requires more RAM and risks catastrophic forgetting if you’re not careful with your training data. Cross-attention with a frozen LLM preserves language capabilities cleanly, but those extra fusion layers add their own computational and engineering overhead. And both approaches need to manage the cost of long visual token sequences, whether through pixel shuffle, perceiver resampling, or other pooling strategies.
Foundational Concepts in VLM Design
Now that we have walked through two concrete VLM architectures, let’s zoom out and look at the design principles behind them. The choices made in SmolVLM and Flamingo aren’t arbitrary; they reflect a broader framework for thinking about how and when to combine different types of information. Understanding this framework will help you evaluate any new VLM architecture you encounter, not just the two we’ve covered.
The Fusion Framework: Early or Late?
At the heart of any system that reasons about both images and text lies a fundamental question: when do you bring these two very different types of information together? Do you combine image and text data at the start and let the model figure it out? Or do you analyze them separately and only compare their high-level outputs at the end? This “when” is the essence of fusion strategies, and the two main camps are called early fusion and late fusion.
To make this concrete, imagine you’re building a toxicity detector for a multiplayer video game. Players interact through both a video feed and a text chat. Toxic behavior might be purely visual (aggressive gestures), purely textual (abusive language), or a combination of both.
Early fusion: Integrating from the ground up
With early fusion (see Figure 6-6), you extract features from both streams. Then, a convolutional neural network (CNN) processes the video frames while a text encoder processes the chat messages and concatenates them into a single combined vector before feeding it to a classifier. The model sees everything at once and learns to find cross-modal patterns: maybe certain gestures tend to co-occur with certain words.
The big advantage is that the model can learn deep, subtle connections between modalities from the start. It builds a single, holistic representation of the scene. This simplicity was a big reason we chose this approach for NanoVLM.
The downside is that concatenating feature vectors creates high-dimensional inputs that are harder to learn from and more expensive to train. There’s also a modularity problem: if you want to swap your vision encoder for a better one, you typically have to retrain the whole system.
Late fusion: Independent strengths, unified decisions
With late fusion (see Figure 6-7), each modality gets its own dedicated model. The vision model analyzes the video and independently concludes “high confidence: angry player.” The language model analyzes the chat and independently flags abusive language. Only after each model has formed its own opinion do you combine their outputs to make a final decision.
The big advantage is modularity. You can swap in a better vision model or add an audio stream without retraining everything else. Each model can be a best-in-class specialist for its own modality.
The downside is limited cross-modal interaction. Because the modalities are processed in silos, the system can miss subtle correlations between what it sees and what it reads. If the vision model condenses a frame down to “it’s a person,” finer-grained details that might have been useful for cross-modal reasoning are lost before the combination step.
Connecting the spectrum to modern architectures
So how do these concepts map onto the VLM architectures from “Modern VLM Blueprints: Connecting Pretrained Models”?
The unified sequence approach (SmolVLM-style) is a form of early fusion. By projecting image patches into the same embedding space as words and concatenating them into one sequence, we mix the ingredients at the start. The LLM’s self-attention then learns the cross-modal connections.
The adapter approach (Flamingo-style) is intermediate fusion. The modalities start independently (the vision encoder runs separately) but then interact at multiple points throughout the LLM via gated cross-attention layers. This allows rich cross-modal learning without creating a massive combined representation up front.
Classic late fusion remains relevant when modularity is the priority. Imagine combining the output of a specialized VLM with other signals like a user’s location or purchase history to make a recommendation. That’s late fusion on top of an already multimodal system.
Understanding this spectrum isn’t about picking a single best method but about recognizing the design patterns and their inherent trade-offs between deep interaction and modular flexibility.
The Encoder-Decoder Pattern: Where It All Started
Early and late fusion describe when modalities are combined. But there is a separate question: what is the overall shape of the model? For VLMs that generate text from images, one architectural pattern has been influential from the very beginning: the encoder-decoder pattern.
The idea is simple. One model reads the image, and another writes the text:
  • The encoder (“the reader”) takes an image and compresses it into a visual representation that captures what is in the scene. In early VLMs, this was often a CNN producing a single vector. Today, it is usually a ViT producing a sequence of visual tokens.
  • The decoder (“the writer”) takes that representation and generates text, one token at a time. Each word choice is informed by the image representation and the words already generated. In early VLMs, this was a long-short-term memory (LSTM). Today, it is usually an LLM.
This pattern was pioneered by models like “Show and Tell” (see the paper by Oriol Vinyals et al.). Here is what a minimal version looks like:

There is no attention, no way for the decoder to look back at specific parts of the image. This is close to late fusion in spirit, because the image is fully encoded before the text model conditions on it. The evolution is clear: the encoder improved, the decoder became more powerful, and most importantly, the connection between them got richer. Instead of squeezing an entire image through a single-vector bottleneck, modern VLMs give the decoder access to hundreds of visual tokens that it can selectively attend to. The encoder-decoder pattern is still the basic skeleton behind many modern VLMs. The innovation lies in how the two halves are connected, which is exactly what “The Adapter Approach: Cross-Attention” (Flamingo) and “The Unified Sequence Approach: Self-Attention” (SmolVLM) explored.
Summary
This chapter opened up the architectural heart of modern VLMs. We started with the attention mechanism: self-attention for finding relationships within a sequence and cross-attention for bridging two different streams of information. These two operations, built from the same Q/K/V foundation, are the building blocks behind every architecture we explored.
We then walked through two dominant blueprints for connecting pretrained vision encoders and LLMs. The cross-attention approach (Flamingo) inserts gated fusion layers into a frozen LLM, using a perceiver resampler to compress visual features and zero-initialized gates to preserve the LLM’s stability. The unified sequence approach (SmolVLM) takes the simpler route: project image features into the token embedding space and let the LLM’s own self-attention handle everything. We built both in code and saw firsthand how different the engineering complexity is, from a one-line LLM call to a manual walk through every decoder layer.
When we compared the two architectures empirically, both at scale (the Idefics 2 study) and with our own baby VLM, a consistent picture emerged. With frozen backbones, cross-attention’s dedicated fusion layers provide a clear advantage. With fine-tuned backbones, the gap narrows or disappears entirely, because the LLM adapting to the task matters more than how the image gets in. This is why the unified sequence approach, with its simpler code and fewer new parameters, has become the dominant choice in recent VLMs.
We also placed these modern designs on a broader conceptual map. Early fusion mixes modalities from the start, enabling deep cross-modal learning at the cost of flexibility. Late fusion keeps modalities independent, offering modularity but limited interaction. The classic encoder-decoder pattern, from Show and Tell’s CNN + LSTM to today’s ViT + LLM, remains the skeleton behind all of these designs. What has changed is not the overall shape but the richness of the connection between encoder and decoder: from a single vector bottleneck to hundreds of visual tokens flowing through attention.
With these architectural patterns in hand, you can now read any VLM paper and understand the design choices behind it: why certain components are frozen or fine-tuned, why a particular fusion strategy was chosen, and what trade-offs were made. In the Chapter 7, we will look at how to deploy these models efficiently and bring them into production.
