Chapter 10. Fine-Tuning

Imagine you’ve hired a smart generalist: they communicate clearly, can reason through unfamiliar problems, and learn quickly but they haven’t worked in your domain. They don’t know your jargon or your policies yet. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the process of turning that generalist into a reliable specialist. For LLMs, SFT means taking a pre-trained model and continuing training on a focused dataset so it behaves the way you need for a particular task or product.

Before we get started, we want to clarify that fine-tuning can be supervised or preference-driven. The supervised fine-tuning is SFT, which is the focus of this chapter. In SFT, we train on examples of the form (input, ideal output). We show the model what a good answer looks like, and the model learns to imitate that behavior. Figure 10-1 highlights the SFT stage.

By contrast, preference-driven fine-tuning is often called preference fine-tuning, preference tuning, or simply alignment. This stage is optional and typically comes after SFT. In alignment, instead of providing a single “gold” response, you provide comparisons (or reward signals) that indicate which outputs are better—according to preferences such as helpfulness, harmlessness, or style. The key idea is that once a model is competent (happens during SFT), alignment methods shape which of the many plausible responses it chooses. Methods like RLHF and DPO live here. We will discuss alignment in Chapter 11.

Figure 10-1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) in the LLM post-training pipeline, followed optionally by preference-based alignment.

Throughout this book, we use “fine-tuning” to mean supervised fine-tuning (SFT). When we discuss preference-based methods, we refer to them as “alignment.” In the rest of this chapter, we’ll break down the main SFT approaches, what their training data look like, and SFT training objectives.

When to Fine-Tune vs Not?

Fine-tuning is powerful, but it is also the most expensive way to change model behavior—and one of the easiest ways to accidentally bake in regressions. In interviews, strong answers show that you can separate knowledge gaps from behavior gaps, and justify the lowest-cost, lowest-risk approach that meets the requirements.

Most teams have three levers: prompt engineering, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and fine-tuning. A useful rule of thumb is that if the model has the right information but behaves inconsistently, you have a behavior problem; if the model is missing essential information (private, proprietary, or changing), you have a knowledge problem; and if the model can succeed once you describe the task clearly, you mostly have an instruction problem. These map naturally to fine-tuning, RAG, and prompting respectively.

Finally, note that fine-tuning itself comes in degrees. You can update a small set of parameters, or you can do full fine-tuning where most or all model weights are updated. At the extreme, you can train from scratch, which requires orders of magnitude more data, compute, and engineering effort. Prompt Engineering: The First Step

Prompt engineering is the simplest and most accessible approach. It means carefully crafting the instructions and context you provide to a model within each request, without changing the model itself. You’re essentially learning how to communicate effectively with the model through better questions, examples, and instructions.

This should almost always be your starting point. It requires no training infrastructure, no dataset curation, and no special compute beyond what you already use to run the model. It also has the fastest iteration loop: you can test, refine, and roll back changes in minutes. Prompting is especially effective when the model already has the relevant skills and you are primarily shaping presentation: tone, structure, brevity, and light policies such as “ask a follow-up question when unsure.” We covered prompt engineering techniques in Chapter 3.

That said, prompts have limits. As requirements grow, prompts often become long and brittle. Different users phrase the same request differently, prompts drift over time, and edge cases appear that are hard to “patch” with more instructions. When the model’s behavior needs to be consistent across many inputs, you may need something stronger than prompt craft.

RAG: When You Need Current or Private Information

Retrieval-Augmented Generation sits between prompt engineering and fine-tuning in terms of complexity. With RAG, you build a searchable database of information and then, for each query, automatically retrieve relevant pieces of that information to include in your prompt to the model.

RAG shines when you need the model to reference information that’s constantly changing or wasn’t included in the model’s training data. If you are building a support assistant that must reflect the latest product policy, or an enterprise assistant that must answer from internal documents, fine-tuning is usually the wrong solution: you do not want to retrain the model every time a document changes. With RAG, updating knowledge is operationally simple—you add or refresh documents.

The trade-off is that RAG moves complexity to runtime. Retrieval adds latency and cost, and it introduces a new failure mode: even a strong model will answer poorly if the retrieved context is irrelevant or incomplete. In practice, production RAG lives or dies by retrieval quality and evaluation: are you retrieving the right passages, and does the model faithfully use them?

Fine-Tuning: When You Need Deep Customization

Fine-tuning becomes the right choice when prompt engineering and RAG cannot achieve the behavior you need, and the improvement is valuable enough to justify the added investment. The key idea is that fine-tuning primarily changes behavior: how the model responds, how consistently it follows instructions, and how reliably it produces a desired structure. In other words, fine-tuning is best suited for making a target behavior the model’s default.

However, fine-tuning is a weak mechanism for adding new factual knowledge or keeping information up to date. If the bottleneck is missing, private, or frequently changing facts, you generally should not retrain the model. Instead, prefer RAG, which can inject the right context at runtime and be updated simply by refreshing your underlying documents or database.

What fine-tuning is not good at

SFT does not reliably turn a language model into a factual database, nor does it handle rapidly changing or long-tail information well. Encoding large volumes of knowledge through SFT leads to stale behavior, frequent retraining, and brittle generalization. Fine-tuning also does not invent fundamentally new reasoning abilities; it primarily reinforces patterns the base model already partially exhibits.

One common reason to fine-tune is consistency. Many applications do not fail because the model is incapable; they fail because the model is inconsistent. You might need stable JSON that conforms to a schema, predictable tool calls with well-formed arguments, or responses that follow a multi-step template across a wide variety of inputs. Prompting can sometimes achieve this, but if correctness depends on output shape, small variance is costly. Fine-tuning can reduce that variance.

Another reason is domain behavior. In specialized settings—legal, medical, finance, internal enterprise workflows—you may want the model to use your terminology, adopt your internal style conventions, or follow your team’s preferred reasoning patterns. Fine-tuning can make these behaviors “default” so you do not pay a prompt tax every time.

Combining Approaches

These approaches are not mutually exclusive. In production, the strongest systems often use all three: prompt engineering to define the interaction contract, RAG to supply up-to-date or private knowledge, and fine-tuning to make desired behavior the default—especially for style, structure, and tool-use reliability.

A practical strategy is to start simple and add complexity only when it earns its keep. Begin with prompts. Add RAG when the bottleneck is missing or changing information. Consider fine-tuning when the bottleneck is consistency, format adherence, or “prompt tax” (long prompts and repeated examples) that you want to eliminate. In other words: use prompts to instruct, RAG to ground, and fine-tuning to standardize.

Do not jump straight to fine-tuning. First diagnose the bottleneck. If the model needs better instructions, start with prompting. If it lacks private, current, or frequently changing information, use RAG. Fine-tune when the model already has the underlying capability but needs more consistent behavior, formatting, style, or tool use. Strong interview answers justify the simplest approach that solves the actual problem.

Types of Fine-Tuning

Once you decide that fine-tuning is justified, the next question is which kind. Fine-tuning varies along the axis of the number of parameters you actually update. At one extreme, you can update the entire model (full fine-tuning). At the other extreme, you can keep the base model frozen and learn only a small number of additional parameters (parameter-efficient fine-tuning, or PEFT). In between lies selective fine-tuning, where you update only a subset of the original weights (for example, the last k layers).

In full fine-tuning, you update most or all of the model’s weights. This gives you more freedom to reshape behavior, but it increases cost and risk. Full fine-tuning is more likely to introduce regressions on general capabilities, requires stronger evaluation gates, and is harder to maintain across model upgrades. For many applications, PEFT provides most of the benefit of fine-tuning with a fraction of the operational burden; full fine-tuning is typically reserved for cases where PEFT cannot reach the desired quality ceiling or when you are building a highly specialized model with substantial training data and rigorous evaluation.

Selective fine-tuning updates only part of the base model, such as the final layers or a specific component. The motivation is to get some of the expressiveness of full fine-tuning while limiting compute. In practice, selective fine-tuning is less standardized than PEFT.

With parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), you keep the base model fixed and learn a small set of additional parameters—most commonly adapter weights such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). This is often the default choice in industry because it is cheaper, faster, and easier to operationalize. PEFT also supports modularity: you can maintain multiple adapters (by domain, customer, or task), swap them in and out, and roll back quickly if something regresses.

When comparing full fine-tuning with PEFT, frame the decision as a trade-off between adaptation capacity, cost, and regression risk. Start with PEFT when a small parameter update is sufficient. Move toward broader layer coverage, larger adapters, or full fine-tuning only when evaluation shows that the lower-capacity approach has reached a quality ceiling. More trainable parameters give the model more freedom to adapt, but also increase compute cost and the risk of unwanted drift.

Where the Data Comes From

SFT data can come from several sources. The most reliable source is human-written demonstrations. These are “gold” examples where a knowledgeable annotator writes the response you would want the model to produce in production. Human demonstrations are expensive, but they establish the target behavior clearly.

A second common source is existing logs from an application or internal workflow. Logs are attractive because they match real user distribution and contain realistic edge cases. The catch is that raw logs are noisy: they include incorrect answers, incomplete answers, inconsistent styles, and sometimes sensitive information. Using logs for SFT usually means a pipeline of filtering, sampling, and re-labeling.

A third source is synthetic data, where a stronger model generates candidate responses that you treat as training targets. Synthetic data can provide scale quickly. It works best when you already have strong specifications for correctness and format, and it works poorly when correctness is ambiguous or when the teacher model hallucinates confidently.

Quality and Guidelines

SFT is only as good as the examples you train on, so an effective SFT defines a labeling rubric. At minimum, it should have standards for correctness (is the answer factually and logically correct), completeness (does it address the user request fully), style and format (does it follow the required structure, tone, and schema), and safety (does it comply with policy, handle sensitive requests appropriately, and refuse when necessary). If you want consistent behavior, these guidelines must be explicit.

One of the most damaging failure modes is “helpful but wrong.” Models can produce plausible, confident answers even when the input is underspecified or the information is missing. Your dataset should teach the desired behavior in those moments: asking a clarifying question, stating assumptions, or saying “I don’t know” when appropriate.

Diversity matters as much as correctness. It should also include adversarial or messy inputs when relevant: contradictory instructions, ambiguous requests, long contexts, short contexts. Without this diversity, the model learns the happy path and fails exactly where real users push it.

Finally, balance matters. Datasets often become skewed toward whatever is easiest to collect or label: one domain, one response style, one task type, one level of difficulty. The result is a model that over-produces that style and under-performs elsewhere. A practical rule is to match the training distribution to the serving distribution, then deliberately overweight the high-cost failure cases that you most want to eliminate.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)

Full fine-tuning updates all model weights. While this often delivers strong accuracy, it is expensive to train, expensive to store, and operationally painful when you need many customized variants of the same base model.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) is a family of techniques that adapts a pretrained model by training only a small number of parameters—either newly introduced parameters (e.g., adapters, LoRA matrices, soft prompts) or a small subset of existing parameters—while keeping the original model weights frozen. In practice, PEFT is the default choice when you want customization without the cost and overhead of maintaining a separate full model per use case. Many production systems rely on PEFT because it turns fine-tuning from a heavyweight model release into a lightweight, frequent iteration loop: one shared backbone model plus many small “deltas” specialized for different tasks or audiences.

Adapters

An adapter is a small neural network block inserted into a Transformer layer. A common design is a bottleneck MLP as shown in Figure 10-4. A typical form is h=h+Wupσ(Wdownh) where Wdown∈ℝm×d and Wup∈ℝd×m with m≪d. Because the bottleneck dimension is small, the adapter adds relatively few parameters.

Figure 10-4. Adapter-based fine-tuning in a Transformer layer, showing where adapter modules are inserted and the adapter’s bottleneck architecture with down-projection, activation, up-projection, and a residual connection.

Adapters are inserted inside each Transformer block, often after the attention sublayer and/or after the feed-forward sublayer. Conceptually, they provide a task-specific “side transformation” of the hidden state while leaving the pretrained computations intact. This design makes adapters highly modular: to switch tasks, you keep the same backbone and swap adapter weights. The key benefit is that you get task adaptation without duplicating the full model.

Adapters typically add a small fraction of parameters relative to the base model, often <1% per task, though it can range higher depending on bottleneck size and placement.

Prompt Tuning

Prompt tuning, also called soft prompting, prepends a short sequence of continuous vectors to the input embedding layer and trains only those vectors. It freezes the rest of the language model and learns only a small set of trainable prompt embeddings. Figure 10-5 shows the concept.

Figure 10-5. Soft prompt vectors prepended to the input embeddings; only the prompt vectors are trained.

Unlike textual prompts, these prompts are not discrete tokens and do not map to words in the vocabulary. Instead, they are free parameters and optimized through gradient descent during training. A helpful way to think about prompt tuning is that it teaches the model how to start thinking about a task. The learned prompt acts like a compact, task-specific “instruction” that the model reads before seeing the real input.

Prompt tuning is attractive in production because it is one of the simplest PEFT methods. It enables lightweight customization: you store one shared backbone model plus a small learned prompt per task. Training and deploying a new prompt is far cheaper than full fine-tuning. Prompt tuning tends to work best with larger pretrained models, where the frozen backbone already contains broad knowledge and the prompt mainly needs to steer that knowledge toward the task.

Rule of thumb. Because prompt tuning only changes a small number of parameters at the input, it may not provide enough capacity for tasks that require large behavior changes or deep domain adaptation. In such cases, methods that intervene deeper in the network (e.g., prefix tuning or LoRA) often perform better.

Prefix Tuning

Unlike prompt tuning, which prepends continuous vectors only at the input embedding layer, prefix tuning injects trainable continuous vectors directly into the Transformer’s attention computation. In a Transformer, each attention layer computes queries, keys, and values. Prefix tuning adds trainable key and value vectors at every layer. During training, the rest of the model is frozen and only these prefixes are learned. During inference, the model attends not only to the actual input tokens, but also to this learned prefix, which nudges generation toward the desired behavior. Figure 10-6 illustrates this technique.

Figure 10-6. Trainable key/value prefixes added to attention at every Transformer layer.

In short, prompt tuning steers the model from the outside (input-level control), whereas prefix tuning steers the model from the inside (attention-level control).

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)

The most widely used PEFT method for LLMs today is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). LoRA starts from a simple observation: when we fine-tune a large model, we are trying to change its behavior by adjusting some weight matrices, but those matrices are huge. Updating and storing full copies of them is expensive. So what if we freeze the original weights (call them W) and learn only a small, low-rank “patch” (i.e., ΔW) to each weight matrix? LoRA then updates the weights as W’=W+ΔW.

The key trick is to constrain the update ΔW to be low-rank. To do that, LoRA models ΔW as the product of two low-rank matrices A∈ℝr×d and B∈ℝk×r, where the rank r is small, for example r∈4,8,16,64. Since r≪min(k,d), the number of trainable parameters becomes tiny compared to the full matrix, which has kd parameters. Figure 10-7 shows LoRA.

Figure 10-7. Low-rank update: freeze W and learn ΔW=BA with small rank r.

Where LoRA plugs into a Transformer

Transformers contain many large linear projection matrices, especially inside attention and MLP blocks. For example, in attention we have projections such as WQ, WK, WV, and WO. In practice, LoRA adapters are attached to one or more of these linear layers (and sometimes also to MLP projections). Each selected weight matrix gets its own low-rank update, but the rest of the model stays frozen.

QLoRA (Quantized LoRA)

QLoRA extends LoRA by keeping the base model in a quantized format (often 4-bit) during training while still learning LoRA adapters in higher precision. The big win is practical: you can fine-tune large models on limited GPU memory. The resulting artifact is still a small adapter; at inference time you can apply the adapter to the base model (quantized or not, depending on your serving stack).

SFT Training Basics

SFT uses labeled examples to adapt a pretrained language model to a target behavior. In practice, SFT usually means “teach the model to produce the right continuation” by training it on (input, output) pairs.

SFT optimizes the model to maximize the likelihood of the desired output tokens given the input context. Concretely, the model is trained with the standard next-token prediction (a.k.a. autoregressive cross-entropy) objective:

LSFT=−∑t∈target tokenslogpθ(yt∣x,y<t)

Where x is the prompt (i.e., instruction and optional context), and y is the target response.

Even though the model is always predicting the next token, in SFT we typically compute loss only on the response tokens, not on the prompt tokens. This is done by setting prompt tokens to an ignore index (e.g., −100 in PyTorch), so the model isn’t penalized for failing to “predict” the user instruction. Figure 10-8 illustrates this masking.

Figure 10-8. SFT loss masking: compute loss on response tokens, ignore prompt tokens.

Fine-Tuning Knobs That Actually Change Outcomes

The hyperparameters that we discussed in Chapter 9 (learning rate, effective batch size, and sequence length) matter for any gradient-based training. Fine-tuning adds a different set of knobs that are specific to adapting a pretrained model: what you allow to move, where you add capacity, how you format supervision, and how you prevent overfitting and forgetting. The most important ones are the following.

  1. What you train (parameter scope): Fine-tuning is fundamentally a decision about which parameters receive updates.
    • Full fine-tuning updates all weights. It has the highest capacity, highest risk of forgetting, and it is the most expensive.
    • Adapter-based fine-tuning (PEFT/LoRA) keeps the base model frozen and trains a small number of added parameters. This is cheaper, more stable on smaller datasets, and easier to maintain across tasks.
    • Hybrid strategies train adapters plus a small set of base parameters (e.g., the final layers, the output head, or select layer norms) when pure adapters underfit.
      This knob controls adaptation capacity, compute cost, and how much the model’s general capabilities drift.
  2. Where you train (target modules and layer selection): In PEFT, you must choose where to inject trainable capacity.
    • Target modules: e.g., attention projections only (often sufficient for style and instruction following) versus attention + MLP projections (more capacity for domain shifts).
    • Which layers: all layers versus only top- k layers. Training only higher layers can reduce forgetting and speed up training, but may underfit harder behavior changes.
      A practical mental model is: more modules / more layers ⇒ more capacity and more drift; fewer modules / fewer layers ⇒ cheaper and safer, but may saturate.
  3. Freeze strategy (what stays fixed): Even in “full” fine-tuning, selectively freezing components can improve stability and reduce unwanted changes.
    • Freeze embeddings to preserve token semantics (especially important when your data is small).
    • Freeze early layers to preserve general linguistic and reasoning capabilities, and adapt only later layers.
    • Norms and biases: training layer norms and biases can help optimization, but can also shift model behavior; many PEFT setups keep them frozen by default.
      Freezing choices are a fine-tuning-specific form of regularization; they limit how much the model can deviate from the pretrained solution.
  4. Regularization that matters more in fine-tuning: Fine-tuning datasets are often smaller, narrower, and more repetitive than pretraining corpora, so overfitting is common.
    • Weight decay helps prevent overfitting when updating many parameters; typically less important for pure adapters but still useful in some regimes.
    • Dropout is more helpful in fine-tuning than pretraining when examples are limited or highly templated.
    • Early stopping / checkpoint selection: fine-tuning can improve quickly and then degrade; evaluating periodically and selecting the best checkpoint often beats “train longer.”
  5. LoRA hyperparameters (adapter capacity and strength): When using LoRA, the most important hyperparameters are the ones that control adapter expressivity and update magnitude.
    • Rank r: the adapter’s capacity. Higher r can learn bigger behavior shifts, but increases memory and can overfit if data is small.
    • Scaling α: controls the effective strength of the LoRA update, typically applied as ΔW←αrBA. Larger α increases update magnitude without increasing parameter count.
    • LoRA dropout: regularizes the adapter pathway and can improve robustness on small datasets.
    • Target modules: choosing which matrices receive LoRA (e.g., attention projections) often matters as much as r and α.
    • Merge vs. keep adapters separate: at deployment you can either (i) load the base model plus adapters, or (ii) merge adapters into the base weights for simpler serving. Merging changes operational complexity, not the learned function, but it affects reproducibility and versioning.
  6. Supervision format knobs (prompting, masking, and packing): Fine-tuning is extremely sensitive to how you present examples.
    • Prompt/template: delimiters, system/instruction style, and consistent formatting strongly influence instruction-following behavior.
    • Loss masking: for instruction tuning, a common choice is to compute loss only on the assistant tokens (not the prompt). This reduces the incentive to “memorize” prompts and focuses learning signal on the desired outputs.
    • Example packing: packing multiple short samples into one sequence improves throughput and token utilization, but can introduce subtle boundary issues if separators are inconsistent.

A practical way to prioritize these decisions is to start with what you train (full fine-tuning vs. PEFT), then decide where you train (target modules and layer coverage), followed by the main LoRA capacity knobs such as r and α, and finally the supervision format, including templates and loss masking. Once these choices are well calibrated, the more general training hyperparameters—such as learning rate, batch size, and sequence length—are easier to tune.

If a LoRA fine-tune underperforms, do not assume that increasing the rank is the only answer. Rank controls adapter capacity, but target modules and layer coverage can matter just as much. A useful debugging order is: verify the data and evaluation first, then broaden the target modules or layer coverage, increase rank if needed, and consider full fine-tuning only after PEFT has demonstrably reached a quality ceiling. Use QLoRA primarily when GPU memory is the constraint.

Evaluation of SFT

We cover evaluation more broadly in Chapter 5. Here we focus on what is specific to SFT. Fine-tuning runs are short and often operate on narrow datasets; the model can improve quickly, plateau, and then overfit or drift. A good evaluation setup therefore has three levers:

  1. Validate against a fixed baseline: Always evaluate the fine-tuned model relative to the base model (or the previous production checkpoint) using the same prompts, inputs, and decoding settings. This avoids mistaking style changes for real quality gains.
  2. Cadence and checkpoint selection: SFT often reaches peak quality early and then overfits. Evaluate frequently enough to observe the peak (otherwise the “best” checkpoint may be missed). Define the “best checkpoint” criterion. Prefer a task metric (or format compliance rate) over validation loss when choosing the final checkpoint. Use loss primarily as a health signal for instability.
  3. Regression guardrail: Keep a small “do-not-break” set (general prompts or key product flows) and require performance to stay above a threshold. This catches catastrophic forgetting and unwanted drift.

How to tell whether fine-tuning worked. It worked if (1) the target behavior improves on held-out examples, (2) the improvement is robust to small prompt variations, and (3) regressions on the guardrail set remain within acceptable bounds.

Never claim that a fine-tune worked just because the training or validation loss decreased. Compare the fine-tuned model against the base model using the same evaluation inputs and decoding settings, measure the target behavior directly, and maintain a regression guardrail for capabilities you do not want to lose. The right checkpoint is the one that improves the target metric while keeping regressions within acceptable bounds.

Summary

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the most direct way to turn a general-purpose pretrained model into a reliable specialist. SFT trains the model on curated (input, ideal output) examples and makes the desired behaviors default rather than prompted. Choosing when to fine-tune, selecting the right fine-tuning method, and curating high-quality training data are the three decisions that most determine whether fine-tuning delivers real production value. In practice, SFT is what most teams mean by “fine-tuning”. In contrast, preference-driven methods such as RLHF and DPO are better viewed as a follow-up alignment stage that reshapes output preferences after the model is already competent.

Interview Questions and Answers

Thread: When and Why to Fine-Tune

  1. How do you decide between prompting, RAG, and fine-tuning?
    Start by diagnosing the bottleneck. If the model already has the right knowledge but responds inconsistently, you have a behavior problem: tone, format, style, or reliability. Fine-tuning addresses this by making the desired behavior the model’s default. If the model is missing key information, such as private documents, frequently changing facts, or domain-specific knowledge, you have a knowledge problem; RAG solves it by retrieving relevant context at runtime. If the model can succeed once you describe the task clearly, you mostly have an instruction problem; better prompts are the simplest fix.
    A practical rule is to start with prompts. Add RAG when the bottleneck is missing or changing information. Consider fine-tuning when the bottleneck is consistency, format adherence, or the prompt tax, meaning long prompts with repeated examples that you want to eliminate.
  2. When is fine-tuning the wrong solution?
    Fine-tuning is usually the wrong choice when the core problem is missing or frequently changing information, such as internal documents, policies, or user-specific state. Retraining every time a document changes is expensive and leads to stale behavior. Retrieval-based approaches solve this problem more directly.
    The risk compounds when success criteria are unclear. Without a concrete evaluation target, training can change style or verbosity without improving real correctness or reliability. If you cannot define what “better” looks like in a measurable way, fine-tuning becomes a blind investment.
  3. What does SFT actually change about a model?
    SFT primarily changes behavior, not knowledge. Pretraining gives the model its world knowledge and linguistic competence; SFT reshapes how it uses that knowledge in response to instructions. Concretely, SFT adjusts the model’s output distribution so that desired response patterns, such as structure, tone, format, and tool-calling conventions, become the default rather than something that must be coaxed through careful prompting.
    As a mechanism for injecting new factual knowledge, however, SFT is weak. The model may memorize a few facts from training examples, but it will not reliably recall them at inference time. For knowledge gaps, prefer RAG.
  4. Full fine-tuning vs. PEFT: how do you choose?
    PEFT, typically LoRA, is the default in most production settings. It is cheaper to train, cheaper to store, and much easier to operationalize: you keep one shared backbone and maintain many small adapters per task or customer. Shipping a new adapter is far lighter than releasing a full model, which speeds up iteration cycles and makes rollback straightforward, since you can simply revert to the previous adapter file. PEFT also limits how far the model can drift from the pretrained solution, which reduces the risk of catastrophic forgetting.
    Full fine-tuning gives more capacity to reshape behavior, which matters when the target domain is far from the pretraining distribution or when PEFT has hit a quality ceiling. However, it costs more, risks regressions on general capabilities, and requires a stronger evaluation suite. Most teams reserve full fine-tuning for cases where PEFT demonstrably cannot reach the required quality after increasing rank, broadening target modules, and expanding layer coverage.
  5. What is continual pretraining and how does it differ from SFT?
    Continual pretraining, sometimes called domain-adaptive pretraining, resumes the next-token prediction objective on a large domain-specific corpus, using the same loss as the original pretraining. The goal is to inject new domain knowledge into the model’s weights before any instruction tuning. A legal AI team, for instance, might continue pretraining on millions of pages of case law and regulatory filings.
    SFT, by contrast, trains on curated (input,ideal output) pairs and focuses on shaping response behavior. The two are complementary: continual pretraining teaches the model what to know, and SFT teaches it how to respond. Teams that need both deep domain knowledge and controlled output format often run continual pretraining first, then SFT.

Thread: PEFT Methods Compared

  1. How does prompt tuning differ from instruction/few-shot prompting, and does model size matter?
    Instruction prompting uses discrete natural-language tokens chosen by a human. Prompt tuning learns continuous vectors optimized by gradient descent. The learned “soft prompt” may not correspond to readable text, but because the vectors are trained end-to-end on task data, they can be more effective than handcrafted prompts, though stability varies with model size and task.
    Model size matters. Generally, soft prompting works better on large models. Large models have stronger pretrained representations and can adapt meaningfully from a handful of learned vectors at the input. Smaller models often need modifications to internal weights because soft prompts alone do not provide enough capacity to steer behavior.
  2. Prompt tuning and prefix tuning both add virtual tokens. How are they different?
    Prompt tuning adds soft tokens only at the input embedding layer. Prefix tuning adds trainable key and value vectors inside the attention computation at every Transformer layer. Because prefix tuning intervenes deeper in the network, it has more expressive control over the model’s internal representations. The cost is that prefix tuning trains more parameters and is slightly more complex to implement.
  3. How do adapters work inside a Transformer?
    An adapter is a small bottleneck MLP inserted into a Transformer layer, typically after the attention sublayer or the feed-forward sublayer. A common form is:
    h=h+Wupσ(Wdownh)
    where Wdown∈ℝm×d projects the hidden state down to a bottleneck of dimension m≪d, a nonlinearity σ is applied, and Wup∈ℝd×m projects back up. The residual connection means the adapter adds a task-specific correction without replacing the pretrained computation. Because the bottleneck is small, each adapter typically adds less than 1% of the base model’s parameters.
  4. Adapters and LoRA both freeze the backbone and train a small number of parameters. What is the key difference, and which adds more inference latency?
    Adapters add new computation blocks, namely bottleneck MLPs, that process hidden states through an extra forward pass per layer. LoRA learns a low-rank update to an existing weight matrix:
    W’=W+BA
    Adapters are sometimes described as “adding capacity,” while LoRA is “reparameterizing updates” in a low-rank form.
    The practical difference shows up at inference. Adapters always add per-layer compute at inference: an extra down-projection, nonlinearity, and up-projection that stay as separate blocks. Unmerged LoRA adds a similar down/up projection, but at deployment its update can be merged into the base weights, W←W+BA, eliminating any runtime overhead. For latency-sensitive serving, LoRA’s mergeability is a significant advantage.

Thread: LoRA Deep Dive

  1. What is LoRA, and what changes in the forward pass?
    LoRA, or Low-Rank Adaptation, is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that keeps the pretrained weights frozen and learns a small low-rank update to selected weight matrices:
    W’=W+ΔW,ΔW=BA
    where B∈ℝk×r and A∈ℝr×d with rank r≪min(k,d). Instead of training the full k×d matrix, LoRA trains only A and B, reducing the trainable parameter count dramatically.
    In the forward pass, for a linear layer h=Wx, LoRA modifies the computation to:
    h=Wx+αrBAx
    The backbone contribution Wx remains unchanged, computed from frozen weights, and LoRA adds a learned correction term scaled by α/r.
  2. Why does the low-rank constraint help?
    A full weight matrix W∈ℝk×d contains kd parameters. LoRA assumes the useful change needed for a new task lies in a small subspace. By constraining ΔW to rank r, LoRA learns only r(k+d) parameters instead of kd. For a 4096 × 4096 matrix with r=16, that is roughly 131K parameters instead of 16.8M, a 128× reduction. The low-rank structure acts as an implicit regularizer, which also helps prevent overfitting on small datasets.
  3. What are the main LoRA hyperparameters, and where do adapters live?
    LoRA adapters attach to linear projection layers, most commonly:
    • Attention projections: Wq,Wk,Wv,Wo
    • MLP projections: up_proj, down_proj, and sometimes gate_proj
      A practical default is to start with Wq and Wv, then expand to other attention projections and MLP layers if more capacity is needed.
      The most important LoRA hyperparameters are:
      1. Rank r: controls the capacity of the low-rank update. Higher r can model more complex adaptations but increases memory and compute.
      2. Scaling α: controls the strength of the update, applied as ΔW←αrBA. You can think of α as a volume knob: small α keeps the model close to base behavior; large α lets the adapter change behavior more aggressively.
      3. Target modules: which weight matrices receive LoRA adapters, for example attention projections, MLP projections, or both.
      4. Layer coverage: whether LoRA is applied to all layers or only a subset. Budget-constrained setups often start with later layers first.
      5. LoRA dropout: optional dropout on the adapter path to reduce overfitting, especially for small datasets.
      Many teams tune target modules and rank r first, then adjust α and dropout for stability.
  4. How should I choose the LoRA rank r and layer coverage?
    The right rank depends on model size, task complexity, and how far the target behavior is from the base model:
    • Small rank (r∈[4,16)]: sufficient for small domain or style shifts such as tone control, writing in a company voice, or lightweight vocabulary adaptation. The base model already knows how to do the task; it just needs a behavioral nudge.
    • Higher rank (r∈[16,64)]: needed when the task requires new computation patterns, such as reliably producing structured JSON, following multi-step templates, or generating function-call arguments. These tasks demand more adapter capacity.
    • Still not enough: if improvements plateau, try broadening target modules, add MLP projections, or increase layer coverage before jumping to full fine-tuning.
      Layer coverage is a related capacity knob. Applying adapters to all layers gives higher capacity and is the common default for most tasks. A subset of layers is cheaper and sometimes sufficient; if budget-constrained, start with adapters in later layers, closer to output behavior, and expand coverage if quality is insufficient.
  5. What does it mean that LoRA can be merged, and why does it matter?
    LoRA learns ΔW=BA. At deployment, you fold the update into the original matrix:
    W←W+BA
    The merged model runs like a normal model with no separate adapter computation, no extra latency, and a simpler inference graph. Merging matters for low-latency serving and for deploying to environments that do not support adapter frameworks.
    The downside is that once merged, you lose the ability to swap adapters dynamically. If you serve multiple tasks from the same base model, keeping adapters separate is more flexible.
  6. What is QLoRA and when should you use it?
    QLoRA, or Quantized LoRA, loads the frozen base model in low precision, commonly 4-bit NormalFloat, while training LoRA adapters in higher precision. The base weights stay frozen and quantized; the adapter matrices A and B are the only trainable parameters.
    The LoRA math is unchanged: W’=W+BA. The difference is that W is stored and accessed in a quantized format, dramatically reducing GPU memory during training. The QLoRA paper (Dettmers et al., 2023) showed this makes it feasible to fine-tune a 65B-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit fine-tuning quality, bringing large-model fine-tuning within reach of a single high-end card.
    Use QLoRA when GPU memory is the bottleneck. Use standard LoRA when the frozen model fits comfortably in memory, since avoiding quantization eliminates one source of approximation error.
  7. What newer LoRA variants should you know about?
    Several variants refine the original LoRA idea:
    • DoRA (Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation) decomposes each pretrained weight matrix into a magnitude component and a directional component, then applies LoRA only to the direction. Separating these two aspects lets the optimizer update them independently, often improving quality with negligible parameter overhead, roughly 0.01% more parameters than LoRA.
    • LoRA+ sets different learning rates for the A and B matrices, typically a higher rate for B. Standard LoRA uses the same rate for both, but the two matrices play asymmetric roles; tuning their rates separately can improve convergence.
    • VeRA (Vector-based Random Matrix Adaptation) shares a single pair of random low-rank matrices across all layers and trains only small per-layer scaling vectors, reducing trainable parameters even further than LoRA.
      Standard LoRA remains the most widely used and best-supported method. These variants are worth knowing for interviews but are not yet default choices in most production pipelines.

Thread: SFT Training and Data

  1. What is the SFT training objective?
    SFT uses the standard autoregressive cross-entropy loss:
    LSFT=−∑t∈response tokenslogpθ(yt∣x,y<t)
    where x is the prompt, consisting of the instruction plus optional context, and y is the target response. The model learns to produce the desired output by maximizing the probability of each response token given all preceding tokens.
  2. Why do we compute loss only on the response tokens, not the prompt?
    The model should learn to generate good responses, not to “predict” the user’s instruction. Computing loss on prompt tokens would waste gradient signal on reproducing input text that the model will always receive at inference time. By masking prompt tokens, setting their labels to −100 in PyTorch, the training signal focuses entirely on the response behavior we want to improve. This technique is called loss masking.
  3. Where does SFT training data come from?
    There are three main sources:
    • Human-written demonstrations: annotators write the response you would want the model to produce. This is expensive but high-signal, and it establishes the target behavior clearly.
    • Existing logs: application logs match real user distributions and contain realistic edge cases. The catch is noise: raw logs include incorrect answers, inconsistent styles, and sometimes sensitive information. Using logs for SFT requires a filtering and relabeling pipeline.
    • Synthetic data: a stronger model generates candidate responses that serve as training targets. Synthetic data provides scale quickly and works best when you have clear specifications for correctness. It works poorly when correctness is ambiguous or the teacher model hallucinates confidently.
  4. How much data do you need for effective SFT?
    Less than most people assume, provided quality is high. The LIMA paper (Zhou et al., 2023) demonstrated that fine-tuning LLaMA-65B on just 1,000 carefully curated examples produced a model judged equal or better than GPT-4 in 43% of human comparisons. Their core finding is that almost all knowledge lives in pretraining; SFT mainly teaches the model how to present what it already knows.
    For practical purposes, 1,000—​10,000 high-quality examples are often sufficient for style, format, and instruction-following SFT. Domain-specific tasks that require learning new patterns, such as structured extraction or tool calling, may need 10,000—​100,000 examples. More data helps only when quality is controlled; adding noisy examples can degrade performance.
  5. What makes a good SFT dataset?
    Quality hinges on four properties:
    • Correctness: Every response should be factually and logically correct. Plausible-sounding but wrong examples teach the model to hallucinate confidently.
    • Completeness: Responses should fully address the user request. Partial answers train partial behavior.
    • Diversity: The dataset should cover the range of inputs the model will see in production, including adversarial and messy inputs such as contradictory instructions, ambiguous requests, and varying lengths. Without diversity, the model learns the happy path and fails on edge cases.
    • Balance: Datasets often skew toward whatever is easiest to collect. Match the training distribution to the serving distribution, then deliberately overweight high-cost failure cases that you most want to eliminate.
  6. How do you format examples for instruction tuning?
    Most frameworks expect a structured template that separates the system prompt, user message, and assistant response with consistent delimiters. A typical format uses role tags like <|system|>, <|user|>, and <|assistant|> to mark each turn, followed by the corresponding content. The model sees the full conversation as a single token sequence.
    Consistency matters more than the specific tokens chosen. If your training data uses one template and inference uses a different one, performance degrades. Key decisions include whether to include a system prompt, useful for behavior control, how to handle multi-turn conversations by concatenating turns with alternating role tags, and where to place loss masks, typically only on assistant tokens.
  7. What is example packing, and when can it hurt SFT quality?
    Example packing concatenates multiple short training examples into one long sequence to reduce padding and improve GPU utilization.
    It can hurt when:
    • separators are weak or inconsistent, causing boundary leakage, where the model blends adjacent examples,
    • your task requires strict output formats, such as JSON, where cross-example context increases format errors,
    • loss masking is incorrect, accidentally training the model to predict tokens that belong to the next example.
      A safe default is packing with clear delimiters and correct per-example masking. Disable packing for fragile structured-output tasks if it increases violations.

Thread: Failure Modes, Evaluation, and Production

  1. What is catastrophic forgetting in SFT, and how do you mitigate it?
    Catastrophic forgetting occurs when SFT improves the target behavior but degrades general capabilities: reasoning, instruction following, safety posture, or writing quality. The model drifts toward the narrow fine-tuning distribution and loses breadth.
    Common mitigations include:
    • prefer PEFT, such as LoRA or adapters, or freeze embeddings and early layers to limit drift,
    • use a smaller learning rate for full fine-tuning, and avoid over-training through early stopping,
    • mix in a small rehearsal set of general or instruction data alongside your task-specific examples,
    • maintain regression guardrails, meaning a “do-not-break” eval set that must stay above a threshold.
  2. What are common failure modes when using PEFT methods?
    The most common ones are:
    • Under-capacity: the adapter rank is too small or too few modules are adapted. Performance plateaus and improvements stall regardless of how long you train.
    • Overfitting: especially with small or homogeneous datasets. The model becomes brittle, performing well on training-like inputs but failing on slight variations.
    • Metric mismatch: teams rely on training loss as a success signal without task-level evaluation. LoRA can appear to improve by loss while real reliability, correctness, or safety remains unchanged.
  3. PEFT has hit a quality ceiling. How do you decide what to try next?
    Follow a capacity ladder:
    1. Increase PEFT capacity: raise LoRA rank r, broaden target modules, add MLP projections, include Wk and Wo, and increase layer coverage.
    2. Try a hybrid: keep most of the backbone frozen but unfreeze a small subset, such as the last few layers, the output head, or layer norms, to give the optimizer more room.
    3. Full fine-tuning last: switch only when the task requires large behavior changes and you have enough high-quality data plus a strong regression suite. Drift risk and cost are highest here.
  4. What is the minimal evaluation gate before shipping an SFT model?
    Before shipping, verify three conditions:
    • Baseline comparison: the fine-tuned model must beat the base model on the same prompts and decoding settings.
    • One target metric: the primary success metric, such as task accuracy, schema validity, or format compliance rate.
    • One regression guardrail: a small “do-not-break” set with an explicit acceptable-drop threshold.
      Select the final checkpoint by the target metric, with guardrails satisfied, not by loss alone. Loss is a health signal; it does not measure whether the model actually behaves as required.
  5. If you had to serve 100 task-specific fine-tuned variants, how would you manage them?
    Use a shared-backbone architecture: load the base model once and maintain small LoRA adapters per variant. At serving time, route requests to the appropriate adapter. There are two main strategies:
    • Merged weights: pre-merge each adapter into the base model and load the variant you need. This is simple and has zero adapter overhead at inference, but each variant occupies full model memory.
    • Dynamic adapter loading: keep one base model in GPU memory and swap adapters on the fly. This uses less total memory, but requires adapter-aware serving infrastructure such as LoRAX or vLLM with adapter support.
      For 100 variants, dynamic loading is usually more practical. Merged weights work better when you serve only a few variants simultaneously.
  6. What are common multi-adapter patterns in production?
    Production deployments typically follow one of these patterns:
    • Per-domain adapters: legal vs. finance vs. support vs. internal docs. Each adapter specializes in the vocabulary and conventions of a single domain.
    • Per-customer adapters: one adapter per tenant in a multi-tenant system. Each customer’s data stays isolated, and you can add or remove customers without affecting others.
    • Per-skill adapters: separate adapters for JSON extraction, tool use, summarization, or style control. Requests are routed to the appropriate skill adapter.
      All three patterns keep the base model stable while allowing targeted specialization.
  7. What is NEFTune and when does it help?
    NEFTune (Jain et al., 2023) adds uniform random noise to the input embedding vectors during SFT training. The technique is simple to implement, essentially a few lines of code, and acts as a regularizer that prevents the model from overfitting to surface patterns in the fine-tuning data.
    Results can be substantial: on AlpacaEval, NEFTune more than doubled LLaMA-2-7B’s score when fine-tuning on the Alpaca dataset, from roughly 30% to 65%. Reported gains were often in the 8—​10% range on other instruction-tuning datasets, though they varied by dataset and evaluation. NEFTune is most useful when training data is small or homogeneous, since that is where overfitting risk is highest.
