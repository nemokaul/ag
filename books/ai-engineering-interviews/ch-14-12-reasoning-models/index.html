Chapter 12. Reasoning Models

In previous chapters, we explored how language models acquire knowledge through pretraining (Chapter 2), how prompting strategies elicit better answers (Chapter 3), how fine-tuning and reinforcement learning shape model behavior ( Chapters 10 and 11), and how RLHF aligns models with human preferences (Chapter 11). This chapter brings these threads together around a single question: can language models reason?

The answer is yes, but not in the same way humans do, and not always reliably.

In this chapter, we discuss how reasoning models work, how they are trained and evaluated, and where they still fail. We begin with the types of reasoning that matter, then examine the techniques that enable them.

Types of Reasoning

Not all reasoning is the same. Reasoning comes in many forms, and LLM capabilities vary across them. Figure 12-1 summarizes the main types and shows where current LLMs tend to be stronger or weaker.

Deductive reasoning applies general rules to specific cases to reach a logically certain conclusion. “All mammals are warm-blooded. A whale is a mammal. Therefore, a whale is warm-blooded.” LLMs handle simple one- or two-step deductions well, but their performance degrades substantially as the number of chained steps increases. Saparov and He (2023) showed that models frequently take “greedy” shortcuts rather than following correct multi-step proof paths, causing their accuracy to drop sharply beyond three to five reasoning steps.

Inductive reasoning identifies patterns from specific examples to derive general principles. This is arguably the core competency of language models. The entire paradigm of in-context learning is a form of induction. However, models struggle when the required abstraction is truly novel. The ARC (Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus) benchmark (Chollet, 2019), which requires inducing transformation rules from two or three grid examples, is still one of the hardest challenges for LLMs.

Abductive reasoning infers the most likely explanation from incomplete observations. “The grass is wet, and it rained last night, so the rain probably caused it.” LLMs perform well on abductive tasks in familiar domains, likely because their training data is rich with explanatory text. However, they struggle when multiple equally plausible explanations exist or when the correct explanation is unusual.

Commonsense reasoning uses broad world knowledge to make inferences about everyday situations. This includes physical intuition (unsupported objects fall), social understanding (someone who was just fired is probably upset), and temporal reasoning (breakfast comes before lunch). Although models have improved substantially on commonsense benchmarks, they remain brittle, i.e. even small surface-level changes in how a problem is phrased can lead to large drops in accuracy.

Mathematical reasoning involves multi-step arithmetic, algebra, and competition-level problem solving. This has been the primary proving ground for reasoning models, because math problems have unambiguous correct answers that can be verified automatically. Progress in this area has been extraordinary; scores on MATH rose from GPT-4’s 42% to DeepSeek-R1’s 97% within roughly two years.

Causal reasoning is about understanding cause and effect rather than mere correlation. This area remains a persistent weakness for LLMs. Kiciman et al. (2023) found that LLMs can recite known causal relationships from their training data but struggle to infer novel causal directions from statistical evidence alone.

In interviews, distinguishing between reasoning types shows depth. Rather than saying “LLMs can reason,” a stronger answer is: “LLMs handle deductive reasoning well for short chains but degrade beyond a few steps. Inductive reasoning is a core strength through in-context learning. Mathematical reasoning has seen the most dramatic improvements through reasoning models. Causal reasoning remains weak.”

Approaches to Reasoning

Three broad families of approaches have emerged to get LLMs to reason. Figure 12-2 illustrates them.

Prompted General-Purpose LLMs

This category includes standard LLMs that are not explicitly trained as reasoning models. An example is GPT-4. These are general-purpose language models that spend a fixed amount of compute (one forward pass) to generate each token, regardless of the “difficulty” of the token.

These models’ performance on reasoning tasks can often improve substantially when they are prompted appropriately. Techniques such as chain-of-thought prompting, self-consistency, tree-of-thought, and least-to-most prompting fall into this category. These methods encourage the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps, even though the underlying model itself has not been specifically trained to reason. We discussed these techniques in Chapter 3.

Chain-of-thought prompting is particularly important because it effectively increases the model’s computational depth; each additional reasoning token is like an additional processing step. Merrill and Sabharwal (2023) showed that chain-of-thought can increase the computational class of problems transformers can solve, moving from constant-depth threshold circuits to problems solvable by polynomial-time Turing machines. In plain terms, thinking in tokens lets models solve problems that are mathematically impossible for them to solve in a single pass.

Trained Reasoning Models

These models are explicitly trained for reasoning. Unlike standard language models, they can adapt their inference-time compute to the difficulty of the task. Rather than immediately producing the next token, they may spend additional steps deliberating, exploring intermediate possibilities, checking consistency, and refining a solution path. This makes them especially useful for tasks such as multi-step reasoning, planning, coding, and mathematics, where problem difficulty can vary widely.

Their central idea is the use of thinking tokens, that is, intermediate tokens generated before the final answer that allow the model to reason in token space. Well-known examples of reasoning models are OpenAI's o1, o3, and DeepSeek-R1.

The practical impact is dramatic. On the MATH benchmark of competition-level problems, reasoning models have substantially outperformed earlier general-purpose LLMs. For example, GPT-4 scored roughly 42% on the MATH benchmark at launch, while later reasoning models such as OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1 reported much higher scores on closely related evaluations. The broader lesson is that language models can become far more capable on difficult tasks when they are trained to deliberate longer before answering.

Neuro-symbolic Models

A third approach combines a neural model with explicit symbolic mechanisms. The neural part is the language model, which reads natural language and interprets the problem. The symbolic part is a formal reasoning engine, such as a logic solver or theorem prover, which works with explicit rules and variables rather than free-form text (Olausson et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2023; Bayless et al., 2025).

In this setup, the language model first translates the problem into a formal representation, and the symbolic system then solves it step by step. The advantage is that, when the translation is correct, the symbolic solver can produce logically valid answers. The downside is that this approach is fragile; if the language model makes a mistake while converting the original problem into symbols, the solver will faithfully reason over the wrong representation and produce the wrong result.

In practice, the trained-reasoning-model paradigm (approach 2) has become dominant for general-purpose reasoning, and neuro-symbolic methods (approach 3) are used where formal correctness guarantees are required.

In the rest of this chapter, we focus on trained reasoning models and, for ease of reference, refer to them simply as reasoning models. We do not cover neuro-symbolic methods in this book; they are important in specialized settings, but are less central to mainstream generative AI interviews.

Training Reasoning Models

In this section, we explain how modern reasoning models are trained, from the core idea to the full training recipe. Figure 12-3 illustrates the roadmap of this section.

We begin with thinking tokens, the defining mechanism that allows a model to deliberate before answering. We then introduce reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), which replaces subjective human preference signals with rewards that can be checked automatically. Next, we discuss process versus outcome rewards, which differ in whether the model is rewarded for each reasoning step or only for the final answer. With the reward signal in place, we turn to GRPO, the optimization method commonly used in practice. We then examine the emergence of self-verification, one of the most important behaviors learned by reasoning models, and conclude with the multi-stage training pipeline that brings these pieces together.

Thinking Tokens

Reasoning models generate their response in two phases:

  1. A thinking phase, where the model produces an internal chain of reasoning. These tokens are used for computation but are typically hidden from the user in the final response.

  2. An answering phase, where the model produces the visible response, informed by the reasoning it just performed.

Note that this approach is different from standard LLMs, which typically generate output tokens in a single forward decoding process. Figure 12-4 illustrates this contrast.

In trained reasoning models, thinking behavior is not hand-coded or prompted; it emerges from reinforcement learning with a verifier. During training, the model generates candidate solutions, and a verifier (e.g., a math checker or code test suite) scores whether the final answer is correct. Solutions that include careful intermediate reasoning tend to score higher, so the model gradually learns to produce reasoning traces before answering. Over many training iterations, the model also learns to allocate more thinking tokens to harder problems and fewer to easier ones, because RL reward naturally penalizes insufficient reasoning on hard problems and provides no extra benefit for excessive reasoning on easy ones.

The resulting reasoning traces are often surprisingly structured; they include self-verification, backtracking, and error correction patterns that were never explicitly taught. The example below shows this behavior. Notice is that the highlighted line shows self-verification; the model pauses, re-derives the answer from a different angle, and confirms consistency. This pattern was never present in the training data. It emerged because checking work leads to more correct answers and therefore higher reward during RLVR training.

Example: Reasoning trace with self-verification

Prompt: What is the remainder when 2100 is divided by 7?

<think>

I need to find the remainder when 2100 is divided by 7.
Let me look for a pattern in powers of 2 mod 7:

21=2       22=4       23=8≡1 ← cycle restarts

24=16≡2       25=32≡4       26=64≡1

The pattern repeats every 3: {2,4,1,2,4,1,…}

So I need 100mod3=1, which means 2100mod7=2.

Wait, let me verify: 23=8, and 8mod7=1. ✓

So (23)33=299, and 299mod7=1.

Then 2100=299×2=1×2=2mod7. ✓

The answer is 2.

</think>

Output: The remainder when 2100 is divided by 7 is 2.

Mechanically, the thinking and answering phases are both standard autoregressive generation; there is no architectural switch between them. What differs is the role of the tokens. The thinking tokens are generated as the first part of the model’s output, between special delimiter tokens—the user’s prompt remains the input and is not wrapped inside them. The flow looks like this:

[User prompt] -> model generates -> <think> ... reasoning ... </think> -> final answer

In DeepSeek-R1, the thinking phase is delimited by <think> and </think> tags; other providers use different mechanisms. For example, OpenAI calls them “reasoning tokens,” and Anthropic exposes a separate thinking block in the API.

Whether the thinking content is visible to the user varies: DeepSeek-R1 is fully transparent, OpenAI hides the raw reasoning tokens, and Claude exposes them through the API but may summarize them in the consumer interface.

The training of these models involves several techniques that extend beyond standard supervised fine-tuning. The core insight is that for tasks with verifiable answers, such as math, code, or logic, you can use the correctness of the answer as a direct reward signal, without needing human preference judgments.

Next, we will discuss how reasoning models are trained.

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)

In Chapter 11, we discussed RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) as a method for aligning models with human preferences. RLHF trains a reward model on human preference data and then optimizes the policy against that learned reward. For reasoning, this approach has a fundamental limitation: humans are not reliable judges of complex mathematical or logical reasoning. A plausible-sounding but incorrect proof can easily fool a human annotator.

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) addresses this limitation by replacing the learned reward model with a verifiable reward function. For a math problem, the reward is: did the model produce the correct answer? For code, the reward is: do the tests pass? This signal is noiseless, cheap to compute, and scales to millions of problems without human involvement.

The typical reward function combines an accuracy reward with a format reward:

R=Raccuracy+Rformat

where R_accuracy = +1 if the final answer is correct and -1 otherwise, and R_format = +1 if the output follows the required structure (e.g., placing reasoning inside designated markers and the answer in a structured field), and -1 otherwise.

This seemingly simple setup can produce surprisingly strong reasoning behavior. DeepSeek-R1-Zero, which was trained with RLVR directly from a base model and without supervised reasoning traces, exhibited behaviors such as chain-of-thought reasoning, self-verification, and error correction. In other words, the model was not explicitly taught to write step-by-step solutions. Instead, it learned that generating more careful reasoning often led to more correct answers and therefore higher reward. This result suggests that, under the right training setup, reasoning behavior can emerge from reinforcement learning alone.

The shift from RLHF to RLVR for reasoning is a key interview topic.

  • RLHF uses subjective human preferences and is suited for alignment, e.g. helpfulness and safety.

  • RLVR uses objective, verifiable signals and is suited for reasoning tasks where answers can be checked automatically.

When answers are not verifiable, such as in creative writing or open-ended analysis, the RLHF approach from Chapter 11 remains the appropriate training signal.

These two serve different purposes and are often used in sequence.

Even with verifiable rewards, there is a question of granularity: should we reward the model for getting the right final answer, or for getting each reasoning step right?

Process Reward Models vs. Outcome Reward Models

An outcome reward model (ORM) scores only the final answer. Given a problem and a complete solution, the ORM produces a single scalar indicating the probability that the answer is correct. ORMs are cheap to train; you just check whether the final answer matches the ground truth, but they suffer from a credit assignment problem. If a ten-step solution has an error in step 7, the ORM gives a single negative signal for the entire solution, providing no information about which step to fix.

A process reward model (PRM) scores each intermediate step. Given a problem and a partial solution up to step k, the PRM outputs a score indicating whether step k is correct and follows logically from the previous steps. PRMs provide fine-grained credit assignment; they can pinpoint exactly where reasoning goes wrong.

Figure 12-5 illustrates the difference between these two approaches.

The landmark result on this topic is Lightman et al. (2023), “Let’s Verify Step by Step,” from OpenAI. The paper showed that on the MATH benchmark, PRM-guided search achieved 78.2% accuracy compared to 72.4% for ORM-guided search using the same base generator. The improvement is particularly large on harder problems, where solutions are longer and the chance of a single step going wrong is higher.

The trade-off is annotation cost. ORMs need only answer-level labels, such as whether the final answer is correct or incorrect, and these labels can often be generated automatically. PRMs, in contrast, require labels for individual reasoning steps, which is much more expensive. To support this approach, Lightman et al. released the PRM800K dataset, which contains roughly 800,000 human annotations at the step level.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Standard reinforcement learning for LLMs typically uses PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), which requires a separate critic (value) model to estimate the expected return from each state. For a large language model, this critic is typically the same size as the policy model, effectively doubling memory and compute requirements.

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), introduced in the DeepSeek-Math paper (Shao et al., 2024) and used extensively in DeepSeek-R1, eliminates the critic entirely.

The key idea of GRPO is that instead of learning a value function to estimate the baseline, it estimates it empirically from a group of sampled outputs for the same prompt.

The algorithm illustrated in Figure 12-6 works as follows:

  1. For each prompt x, sample a group of G outputs y1,y2,...,yG from the current policy πθ. Recall from Chapter 11 that π denotes a policy, i.e. the model’s probability distribution over outputs given an input.

  2. Compute the reward for each output: r1,r2,...,rG.

  3. Normalize rewards within the group to compute advantages:

    A^j=rj−mean(r1,…,rG)std(r1,…,rG)

  4. Update the policy using a clipped surrogate objective (similar to PPO). The loss is the average (denoted 𝔼) over all prompts and sampled outputs:

    ℒ=𝔼[min(ρA^,clip(ρ,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)A^)]−β·KL(πθ‖πref)

    where:

    • ρ=πθ(y|x)/πθold(y|x) is the importance sampling ratio. It is the ratio of the probability the current policy assigns to output y versus the probability under the old policy.

    • ϵ is the clipping threshold, typically in the range 0.1—​0.2, and bounds how far the policy can move in a single update.

    • β is the KL penalty coefficient, and controls regularization strength.

    • πref is a frozen copy of the model from before RL training began, distinct from πθold, which is the policy from the previous optimization step. The reference policy prevents the model from drifting too far from its pre-RL behavior (global constraint), while the old policy in ρ keeps individual updates conservative (local constraint).

    • The reference policy prevents the model from drifting too far from its pre-RL behavior, while the old policy keeps individual updates conservative.

The normalization step, i.e. subtracting the group mean and dividing by the standard deviation, is the baseline that replaces the critic. If 3 out of 16 solutions are correct, those 3 receive positive advantages and the other 13 receive negative advantages. No separate value network is needed.

The practical benefit of GRPO is significant. For a 67B-parameter policy, eliminating the critic saves roughly 67B parameters worth of GPU memory, making RL training feasible at scales where PPO would be prohibitively expensive.

Advantage Function Design

To understand why GRPO’s normalization works, it helps to revisit the standard RL concept it replaces. In reinforcement learning, the advantage function A(s,a) measures how much better a particular action a is compared to what the agent would do on average from state s. Formally:

A(s,a)=Q(s,a)−V(s)

Read this as: the advantage of action a in state s equals the value of taking that specific action, Q, minus the average value of being in state s at all, V. A positive advantage means the action is better than the baseline; a negative one means it is worse.

PPO estimates this advantage using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which relies on a learned critic network to approximate V(s). The critic provides per-token advantages, meaning each token in the generated sequence gets its own advantage signal indicating whether that particular token improved or hurt the expected outcome. GAE is powerful but comes with two costs: the critic doubles memory requirements, since it is typically the same size as the policy, and any errors in the critic’s value estimates propagate into the advantage signal.

GRPO sidesteps both problems by computing the advantage empirically from the group. The group mean mean(r1,…,rG) serves as the baseline, replacing V(s). Dividing by the standard deviation ensures that advantages are comparable across prompts with different reward distributions. Consider a prompt where rewards are all clustered near +1 versus another where they spread from −1 to +1; without this normalization, the raw deviations from the mean would have very different magnitudes, and the optimizer would effectively weight some prompts much more heavily than others.

A useful edge case makes the design intuition clear: when all G outputs for a prompt receive the same reward, all correct or all wrong, the standard deviation is zero and every advantage is set to zero. No gradient update occurs for that prompt. This is the correct behavior. If the model already solves a problem consistently, there is nothing to learn. If it fails on every attempt, the group provides no signal about which direction to move; the prompt is simply too hard for the current policy, and wasting a gradient step on it would add noise.

Practical Considerations and Limitations

There are a few practical considerations worth noting:

  • Group size G matters. Too small a group, for example G=4, produces noisy advantage estimates because the mean and standard deviation are computed from very few samples. Too large a group, for example G=64 or more, is expensive, since every output must be generated and scored. DeepSeek used G=64 for math tasks, which provided stable estimates while remaining tractable.

  • Advantages are per-output, not per-token. All tokens in a given output share the same advantage signal, because the reward is assigned to the complete solution. The model must learn which tokens in the reasoning trace actually contributed to success; a correct final answer rewards every token in the chain equally, including any irrelevant detours. This is a form of sparse reward, and it is coarser than PPO’s per-token signal. The coarseness is offset by the elimination of critic approximation errors, which in practice often introduce more noise than the per-token granularity removes.

  • Comparison to PPO’s advantage. PPO with GAE provides dense, per-token advantages through the critic, which in principle offers finer credit assignment. However, training a reliable critic for a 67B+ language model is itself a difficult optimization problem. If the critic is inaccurate, the per-token advantages it produces can mislead training. GRPO trades granularity for simplicity and robustness: the advantage signal is coarser, but it is computed directly from observed rewards rather than estimated by a separate model.

Emergence of Self-Verification

One of the most striking findings from the DeepSeek-R1 paper was the “aha moment” during training. As RLVR training progressed, the model spontaneously began producing self-verification patterns in its reasoning traces:

“Wait, let me reconsider this step.”
“Hmm, that doesn’t seem right. Let me try a different approach.”
“Let me verify: if x=3, then…​”

No training data contained such self-correction patterns. The behavior emerged because self-verification leads to more correct answers, which produces higher rewards. A model that catches its own mistakes and corrects them gets more problems right. This demonstrates that complex reasoning behaviors can emerge from simple reward signals without explicit instruction.

The “aha moment” is DeepSeek’s own framing, and it is worth treating with some care. Later work has questioned how much of this behavior truly emerges from RL: Liu et al. (2025) found that DeepSeek-V3-Base already exhibits self-verification and reflective phrasing before any RL, suggesting that RLVR may be amplifying and reinforcing a behavior latent in the pretrained model rather than creating it from scratch. The practical takeaway is unchanged, that RLVR reliably elicits self-verification, but the stronger claim that it appears purely from reward signals is contested.

Multi-Stage Training Pipeline

In practice, training a reasoning model is not a single RL run. DeepSeek-R1 used a four-stage pipeline, shown in Figure 12-7.

Stage 1) Cold-Start SFT: In stage 1, we collect a small number of high-quality chain-of-thought examples. We then fine-tune the base model on these to establish basic reasoning format. This gives the model the right “shape” of reasoning output before RL begins.

Stage 2) Reasoning-Focused RL: In stage 2, we apply GRPO with verifiable rewards on reasoning tasks (math, code, logic). This is where the model’s reasoning ability dramatically improves. The model learns to produce longer, more careful reasoning when problems are hard, and shorter reasoning when problems are easy.

Stage 3) Rejection Sampling + SFT: In stage 3, we use the RL-trained model to generate many solutions per problem. We then filter for correct, well-formatted, readable solutions, and combine these with general instruction-following data (covering tasks such as writing, summarization, and conversation, curated separately to preserve the model’s general capabilities). Finally, we fine-tune a fresh copy of the base model on this combined dataset. This “distills” the RL model’s reasoning capabilities into a cleaner model.

Stage 4) All-Scenario RL: In stage 4, we apply RL again, but now with a broader set of rewards including helpfulness and safety, not just reasoning correctness. This produces the final model that can both reason and be a good general assistant.

Together, these four stages transform a general-purpose base model into a capable reasoning system—one that can think carefully when needed, yet remain helpful and safe across everyday tasks. The next question is whether we can transfer these reasoning capabilities to smaller, more deployable models.

Reasoning Distillation

Training a full reasoning model is extremely compute-intensive. DeepSeek-R1, for instance, uses a base model with 671 billion parameters. To reduce this cost, reasoning distillation transfers the reasoning capabilities of a large model into a smaller one. The smaller model is trained with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on reasoning traces produced by a larger RL-trained model. In this case, the distilled model also learns to produce thinking tokens, even though it was not trained with reinforcement learning itself. An example of a distilled reasoning model is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B.

The basic procedure is as follows:

  1. Use the large reasoning model to solve a large set of problems, generating detailed reasoning traces.

  2. Filter for correct solutions using rejection sampling. The goal of this step is to keep only high-quality outputs.

  3. Fine-tune a smaller model on (problem, reasoning trace, answer) triples via supervised fine-tuning.

What gets distilled is not just the final answer. The smaller model also learns the structure of the reasoning process: how to break a problem into steps, how to verify intermediate results, and how much reasoning to apply depending on the difficulty of the task.

The DeepSeek team reported an important practical result: for small models, distillation is more effective than applying reinforcement learning directly. A 7B model may not have enough capacity to discover complex reasoning strategies on its own through RL, but it can still learn to imitate those strategies from strong examples. For example, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B achieved 55.5% on AIME 2024 through distillation from the 671B R1 model.

Inference-Time Compute Scaling

A key idea behind modern reasoning models is that performance can be improved not only by increasing model size or training compute, but also by increasing computation at inference time. Instead of requiring a larger model for every problem, we can allow the model to spend more computation during generation, effectively letting it “think longer” on harder tasks.

Snell et al. (2024), in “Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute,” showed that a smaller model given more inference-time compute can match or even exceed a larger model with less compute. This result establishes test-time compute as an important new scaling axis alongside model parameters and training data.

There are several ways to use extra computation at inference time to improve reasoning accuracy. A useful way to understand these methods is to view reasoning as search. Under this perspective, a partial reasoning trace corresponds to a state, generating the next step corresponds to an action, and the language model serves as a policy over possible continuations. Auxiliary components such as process reward models, value models, or verifiers provide signals for evaluating candidate trajectories, while the overall inference procedure acts as a search algorithm over reasoning paths.

Viewed this way, inference-time scaling methods differ mainly in how they allocate extra computation. Some spend it across multiple independent reasoning trajectories, while others spend it within a single trajectory by expanding, scoring, or revising intermediate steps. Broadly, these methods fall into two categories: parallel scaling, which increases sampling width by generating multiple independent attempts, and sequential scaling, which increases sampling depth by investing more computation into each individual attempt.

Representative examples of parallel scaling include best-of-N sampling and majority voting, while sequential scaling includes beam search over reasoning steps and sequential revision.

  • Best-of-N sampling (_parallel):_ generates N complete solutions independently, scores each one with a reward model or verifier, and returns the highest-scoring solution. This approach is simple and effective, but it requires completing all N candidate solutions in full, which can be computationally expensive. In practice, performance often improves with larger N, though the gains are typically subject to diminishing returns. Figure 12-9 shows this approach.

  • Majority voting a.k.a. self-consistency (_parallel):_ generates N solutions, extracts the final answer from each, and returns the most common answer. This requires no reward model and works because different reasoning paths tend to make different errors, while correct paths converge on the same answer. We covered this technique in Chapter 3. Figure 12-10 shows this approach.

  • Beam search over reasoning steps (_sequential):_ generates K candidate next steps at each stage, scores the resulting partial reasoning paths, and keeps only the top-B candidates for further expansion. In the variant shown in Figure 12-11, these intermediate steps are scored by a Process Reward Model (PRM). This makes the approach more compute efficient than best-of-N sampling, because weak reasoning paths are pruned early rather than fully completed.

  • Sequential revision (_sequential):_ generates an initial solution, then iteratively critiques and refines it over multiple rounds. Figure 12-12 illustrates this approach. It is especially effective when combined with external feedback, such as executing generated code and feeding the resulting error messages back to the model.

    More generally, tool-augmented reasoning allows models to delegate computations they are unreliable at to external systems. For example, a model may offload arithmetic to a calculator, symbolic manipulation to a solver, or hypothesis testing to code execution. The model then focuses its own reasoning on decomposition, strategy, and interpretation. A closely related idea is program-of-thought, in which the model generates executable code or programs as intermediate reasoning steps. These hybrid approaches help sidestep many of the failure modes of purely internal reasoning.

The compute-optimal allocation depends on problem difficulty. For easy problems, a single attempt is often sufficient. For moderately hard problems, multiple samples with majority voting is effective. For very hard problems, beam search or sequential revision is necessary. Reasoning models like o1 internalize this tradeoff; they learn to allocate more thinking tokens to harder problems.

Evaluation method also matters. The top ones for reasoning models are the following:

  • pass@1 measures whether the model’s first sampled answer is correct. It is the simplest metric and often best reflects single-shot user experience.

  • Majority voting samples N reasoning chains and returns the most common final answer. This usually improves performance over pass@1 by reducing the effect of any single bad sample.

  • Best-of-N with a verifier generates N candidate solutions, scores them with a verifier or reward model, and returns the highest-scoring one. When the verifier is strong, this can outperform both pass@1 and simple majority voting.

When discussing reasoning benchmarks, note which are saturated (GSM8K, HumanEval) and which still differentiate models (AIME, ARC, GPQA, SWE-bench). Mention that evaluation method matters; the same model can show very different scores under pass@1 vs. majority@32.

Challenges and Limitations

Despite rapid progress, reasoning models face several persistent challenges.

Error Propagation in Long Chains

When a model solves a problem through multiple reasoning steps, the chance of getting the entire answer right drops quickly. If each step is correct with probability p, and there are N steps, then the whole chain is correct with probability roughly p^N. Even with 95% accuracy per step, the overall success rate falls to about 60% after 10 steps and 36% after 20.

In reality, things are usually worse because reasoning steps are not independent. If the model makes a mistake early, that mistake becomes part of the context for the next step, and the error can snowball. This is why reasoning often hits a complexity cliff. Up to a point, the model may do well, but once the required chain becomes too long, performance can collapse rather than decline gradually (Dziri et al. (2023)).

Common failure modes are forgotten constraints (where the model establishes a constraint early but violates it later), arithmetic drift (where small calculation errors cascade into wildly wrong answers), and premise abandonment (where the model silently switches approaches mid-reasoning when it gets confused).

Overthinking and Underthinking

Reasoning models sometimes use thousands of tokens on problems that are trivially simple. It is said that the model “talks itself out of” a correct initial intuition. This is known as the overthinking problem. On easy problems, extended thinking can actually reduce accuracy compared to standard models.

The converse problem, underthinking, occurs when the model allocates insufficient reasoning to a hard problem, and produces a superficial answer. Models trained with RL do not always learn to allocate reasoning effort well. They may spend too much computation on easy problems and too little on hard ones.

Unfaithful Chain-of-Thought

This happens when a model’s stated reasoning does not accurately reflect its actual computational process. Turpin et al. (2023) showed that biasing features, such as the order of multiple-choice options, influence the model’s final answer, but are never mentioned in the chain of thought. The model generates a plausible-sounding reasoning trace that justifies the biased answer on apparently substantive grounds.

Lanham et al. (2023) at Anthropic tested faithfulness by corrupting chains of thought and measuring whether the model’s answer changed. For many tasks, truncating or corrupting the reasoning did not change the final answer, which suggests the model had already “decided” on the answer before generating the reasoning.

This matters for safety and oversight. If we cannot trust that a model’s stated reasoning reflects its actual decision-making process, we cannot reliably audit model behavior by reading its chain of thought.

Logical Inconsistency

Another challenge is that LLMs can contradict themselves across related questions. For example, a model may answer “true” to both “Is an albatross an organism?” and “Is an albatross not an organism?” This is called negation inconsistency. A model can also fail at basic transitive reasoning: it may agree that “a magpie is a bird” and that “birds have wings,” yet still deny that “a magpie has wings” (Mitchell et al., 2022).

These failures happen because the model answers each query independently, without any built-in mechanism to enforce consistency across its outputs.

This problem becomes worse as reasoning grows more complex. Lin et al. (2025) showed that when more constraints interact, logical reasoning performance drops sharply. They describe this as the curse of complexity: as the search space grows and logical conflicts become denser, maintaining consistency becomes much harder.

Reasoning or Pattern Matching?

A central question is whether LLMs truly reason or simply perform very sophisticated pattern matching. There is evidence pointing in both directions.

Evidence against genuine reasoning: Some results suggest that LLMs rely heavily on learned patterns rather than abstract reasoning. Wu et al. (2024) found that model performance drops sharply on counterfactual tasks, such as asking the model to reason under an invented rule like “assume addition works like multiplication.” Similarly, Mirzadeh et al. (2024) showed that even when the logical structure of a GSM8K problem is unchanged, simply altering names and numbers can lead to large swings in accuracy. These findings suggest that models may be sensitive to surface form in ways that true reasoning systems should not be.

Evidence for genuine reasoning: At the same time, there is evidence that LLMs can do more than retrieve memorized patterns. Reasoning models are sometimes able to solve novel competition-style problems that appeared after their training cutoff. Merrill and Sabharwal (2023) showed that chain-of-thought can expand a model’s computational power beyond what is possible in a single forward pass. This suggests that intermediate reasoning steps are not just stylistic outputs, but can provide a real structural advantage.

The underlying bottleneck may be compositional generalization, i.e. the ability to recombine known concepts in novel configurations. LLMs can learn individual reasoning primitives (arithmetic, logical deduction, pattern recognition) but struggle when a problem requires combining them in ways not seen during training. This explains why ARC (Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus) is so hard (each puzzle demands a unique composition of basic transformations) and why counterfactual task variants cause disproportionate accuracy drops.

The emerging consensus is nuanced: LLMs perform something functionally similar to reasoning that is practically useful across many domains, but this “reasoning” is more pattern-based and less robust to distribution shifts than human reasoning.

Summary

In this chapter, we examined how language models have evolved from single-pass generators to deliberate reasoning systems. We began by distinguishing the types of reasoning that matter and noted that models have very different capability levels across these types.

We then traced the shift from prompting-based reasoning (chain-of-thought, self-consistency, tree-of-thought) to training-based reasoning, where models learn to think through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). We covered the key training components: process vs. outcome reward models for scoring reasoning quality, GRPO for efficient policy optimization without a critic, and the multi-stage pipeline that combines supervised learning and RL.

We discussed inference-time compute scaling, the insight that spending more computation at generation time can substitute for model scale, and reasoning distillation, which compresses reasoning capabilities from large models into smaller ones. We reviewed the benchmarks used to measure reasoning progress and noted which are saturated and which still differentiate models.

Finally, we examined persistent challenges. Together, these topics form the core knowledge needed to discuss LLM reasoning in both technical interviews and system design conversations.

Interview Questions and Answers

Thread: Understanding Reasoning Models

  1. What is the difference between a standard LLM and a reasoning model?

    A standard LLM generates a response directly from the prompt using a fixed amount of computation per token, i.e. one forward pass through the transformer. A reasoning model introduces a thinking phase before the final response, where it generates internal reasoning tokens that work through the problem step by step. These thinking tokens serve as a computational scratchpad that extends the model’s effective processing depth.

    The distinction lies in how reasoning behavior arises. In a standard model, chain-of-thought reasoning is elicited through prompting, e.g. “let’s think step by step.” In a reasoning model, the thinking behavior is trained into the model through reinforcement learning; the model has learned that producing careful reasoning before answering leads to more correct outputs.

    Practically, this means reasoning models can dynamically allocate more computation to harder problems (generating more thinking tokens) and less to easier ones, without being explicitly told to do so.

  2. Is there any relationship between chain-of-thought length and reasoning quality?

    The relationship is non-monotonic. Longer chains of thought generally help on harder problems by enabling decomposition, working memory (intermediate results can be “read back”), error correction, and multi-approach exploration. DeepSeek-R1 training showed clear correlation between average response length and accuracy as training progressed.

    However, longer is not always better. On easy problems, extended thinking can reduce accuracy; the model “overthinks” and talks itself out of correct initial answers. RL-trained models can also learn to be verbose without being more correct if verbosity is even slightly correlated with reward.

    The optimal pattern is difficulty-dependent: short reasoning for easy problems, moderate reasoning with verification for medium problems, and long reasoning with exploration and backtracking for hard problems. Well-trained reasoning models learn to calibrate length to difficulty, which is one of the capabilities transferred during distillation.

  3. How does reasoning capability in LLMs relate to model scale?

    Chain-of-thought reasoning exhibits what has been called an emergent capability pattern: it only produces benefits above a certain model size. Some studies (Wei et al. (2022)) showed that for models below approximately 100 billion parameters, chain-of-thought prompting did not help and could even hurt performance; the models produced incoherent reasoning chains.

    However, reasoning distillation has partially changed this picture. DeepSeek showed that a 7B model trained on reasoning traces from a 671B model can exhibit strong reasoning capabilities that a 7B model trained from scratch cannot develop. The capability is not absent in smaller models; it is just too hard for them to discover independently through RL.

    The result is a practical two-tier landscape: large models (70B+) can develop reasoning through RL training, while smaller models (7B—​32B) gain reasoning primarily through distillation. The smallest models (1.5B—​3B) can benefit from distillation but with significant capability constraints, particularly on problems requiring many reasoning steps.

Thread: Training Reasoning Models

  1. How does reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) differ from RLHF?

    Both RLHF and RLVR use reinforcement learning to optimize a policy, but they differ in the source and nature of the reward signal.

    RLHF uses a reward model trained on human preference data. Humans compare pairs of model outputs and the reward model learns to predict which output humans would prefer. RLHF is suited for alignment, i.e. teaching models to be helpful, harmless, and honest, but the reward signal is subjective and noisy. Humans are not reliable judges of complex mathematical proofs or long logical arguments.

    RLVR uses verifiable, objective reward signals. For math problems, the reward is whether the final answer is correct. For code, it is whether the tests pass. The signal is noiseless, requires no human annotation, and scales to millions of training problems.

    The practical consequence is that RLVR enables training reasoning capabilities at a scale that would be impractical with human annotators. The two approaches are complementary: RLVR trains reasoning ability, and RLHF (or DPO) aligns the model’s broader behavior.

  2. What is a process reward model (PRM) and how does it differ from an outcome reward model (ORM)?

    An ORM scores a complete solution by checking whether the final answer is correct. It provides a single binary signal for the entire reasoning chain.

    A PRM scores each individual reasoning step. Given a problem and a partial solution up to step k, the PRM estimates whether step k is correct and logically follows from the previous steps.

    PRMs excel at credit assignment. If a ten-step solution has an error at step 7, the ORM gives one negative signal for the whole solution. The PRM identifies exactly which step is wrong. PRMs are therefore much more effective at guiding search during inference. Lightman et al. (2023) showed that PRM-guided search outperforms ORM-guided search by roughly 6 percentage points on the MATH benchmark.

    The trade-off is training cost. ORMs can be trained from automatic labels (just checking final answers), while PRMs require step-level annotations that are expensive to collect. The PRM800K dataset required approximately 800,000 step-level human labels.

  3. How does GRPO work? Why is it preferred over PPO for training reasoning models?

    PPO requires a critic (value) model that estimates the expected return from each state. The critic is typically as large as the policy model, doubling memory requirements.

    GRPO eliminates the critic by using a group-relative baseline. For each prompt, it samples a group of G outputs (typically 16—​64) from the current policy. It computes the reward for each output, then normalizes within the group:

    A^j=rj−mean(rewards)std(rewards)

    Outputs with above-average rewards receive positive advantages; those below average receive negative advantages. The policy is then updated with a clipped surrogate objective, similar to PPO.

    The group mean serves as an empirical baseline, replacing the learned value function entirely. For a 67B-parameter model, eliminating the critic saves roughly 67B parameters of GPU memory. GRPO is also more stable because it avoids the approximation errors inherent in learned value functions.

  4. How does the advantage function in GRPO differ from PPO’s advantage estimation? What are the practical implications?

    In standard RL, the advantage A(s,a)=Q(s,a)−V(s) measures how much better an action is relative to the average outcome from that state. PPO estimates this using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which requires a learned critic network to approximate V(s). The critic provides \emph{per-token} advantages: each generated token gets its own signal indicating whether it helped or hurt the expected reward.

    GRPO replaces the critic with a group-relative computation. For each prompt, the group mean reward acts as the baseline (replacing V(s)), and dividing by the group standard deviation normalizes advantages so they are comparable across prompts. No separate model is needed.

    There are three key practical differences:

    • Granularity. PPO’s per-token advantages offer finer credit assignment in principle. GRPO assigns the same advantage to every token in an output, because the reward applies to the complete solution. All tokens in a correct reasoning trace receive the same positive advantage, even tokens that contributed nothing useful. The model must learn over many updates which patterns actually drive success.

    • Robustness. PPO’s advantages are only as good as the critic’s estimates. For a 67B+ model, training an accurate critic is itself a challenging optimization problem; inaccurate value estimates inject noise into the policy gradient. GRPO avoids this entirely because its advantages are computed directly from observed rewards.

    • Cost. The critic in PPO is typically the same size as the policy, doubling GPU memory. GRPO trades that memory cost for the cost of generating G outputs per prompt (typically 16—​64). For large models, the memory savings usually dominate.

    A good interview summary: PPO gives finer-grained signal at higher memory and stability cost; GRPO gives coarser but more reliable signal at the cost of sampling multiple outputs per prompt. For large-scale reasoning model training, GRPO’s simplicity and stability have proven more practical.

  5. What happens in GRPO when all sampled outputs for a prompt receive the same reward?

    When every output in the group gets the same reward (all correct or all incorrect), the standard deviation of the rewards is zero. Since the advantage is computed as (rj−mean)/std, and the numerator is also zero for every output, the advantage for each output is zero. No gradient update occurs for that prompt.

    Far from being a bug, this is exactly the right behavior for two reasons.

    If all outputs are correct, the model already solves that problem reliably. Updating the policy on a problem it has mastered would risk perturbing weights in ways that hurt performance on other problems, with no upside.

    If all outputs are wrong, the group provides no contrastive signal. The whole point of group-relative normalization is to compare outputs against each other and reinforce the better ones. When none stands out, there is no basis for a meaningful update. The prompt is simply beyond the current policy’s capability, and the model will revisit it in later training iterations as its abilities improve.

    This property also means that the effective training curriculum is adaptive. Problems that are too easy or too hard for the current policy contribute zero gradient, and training signal comes primarily from problems at the model’s frontier, where some but not all attempts succeed. The group size $G$ controls the resolution of this filtering: larger groups make it less likely that all outputs agree by chance, so the model gets useful signal from a wider range of difficulty levels.

  6. What is the role of format rewards in reasoning model training?

    Format rewards are simple rule-based rewards that enforce structural requirements on the model’s output. In DeepSeek-R1, the format reward checks that the output places reasoning inside designated markers (e.g., <think>...</think>) and the final answer in a structured field.

    Format rewards serve three purposes: they keep the reasoning trace separate from the final answer (enabling automatic answer extraction for the accuracy reward), they prevent the model from skipping the thinking phase (which it might otherwise learn to do for easy problems), and they establish consistent output structure that downstream systems can parse.

    Without format rewards, RL training sometimes produces models with degenerate output patterns: mixing reasoning and answers, omitting the reasoning phase, or producing unstructured text that cannot be automatically evaluated. Format rewards are small in magnitude relative to accuracy rewards but essential for stable training.

  7. You have described RLVR, reward models, GRPO, and format rewards as individual components. How do they fit together in a complete training pipeline?

    The DeepSeek-R1 pipeline illustrates how these components combine across four stages:

    1. Stage 1 (Cold-Start SFT): Fine-tune the base model on a small set of high-quality chain-of-thought examples to establish basic reasoning format. The model learns the shape of a good reasoning trace before RL begins.

    2. Stage 2 (Reasoning-Focused RL): Apply GRPO with verifiable rewards on math, code, and logic tasks. Reasoning ability improves dramatically at this stage. The model learns to produce longer, more careful reasoning when problems are hard and shorter reasoning when problems are easy.

    3. Stage 3 (Rejection Sampling + SFT): Generate many solutions per problem using the RL-trained model, filter for correct and well-formatted solutions, combine these with general instruction-following data, and fine-tune a fresh copy of the base model on this combined dataset. The combined fine-tuning “distills” the RL model’s capabilities into a cleaner model.

    4. Stage 4 (All-Scenario RL): Apply RL again with broader rewards covering helpfulness, safety, and reasoning correctness to produce the final model.

    The rationale for multiple stages is that pure RL (as in R1-Zero) produces models that reason well but have poor formatting, readability, and general-purpose capabilities. The multi-stage approach combines RL’s reasoning improvements with SFT’s formatting and the base model’s general knowledge.

Thread: Inference-Time Reasoning

  1. What do we mean by inference-time compute scaling in reasoning models? And why is it important?

    Inference-time compute scaling is the practice of spending more computational resources at generation time to improve output quality. Rather than training a bigger model, you allow the model to “think longer” on harder problems.

    Snell et al. (2024) showed that a smaller model with more inference-time compute can match or exceed a larger model with less compute. Their finding establishes test-time compute as a new scaling axis alongside model parameters and training data.

    Techniques include best-of-N sampling (generate N solutions, return the best), majority voting (return the most common answer), beam search over reasoning steps (guided by a PRM), and sequential revision (iterative self-correction). Reasoning models like o1 internalize this by learning to generate more thinking tokens for harder problems.

    Inference-time compute is more flexible than model scale; you can allocate more compute to hard problems and less to easy ones, and you can adjust the balance based on latency and cost constraints.

  2. What role does Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) play in LLM reasoning?

    MCTS adapts the game-playing search algorithm to explore reasoning paths. The analogy maps game states to partial reasoning chains, actions to generating the next reasoning step, and rewards to answer correctness.

    The four phases are: selection (traverse the search tree using UCB to balance exploration and exploitation), expansion (generate candidate next reasoning steps with the LLM), evaluation (score the new node using a PRM, value model, or by completing the solution and checking the answer), and backpropagation (update scores along the path back to the root).

    MCTS is particularly useful when combined with process reward models; the PRM serves as the evaluation function for partial solutions, avoiding the expensive rollouts that would otherwise be needed.

    The main challenges are the enormous action space (each “action” is a natural-language sentence, not one of a few discrete moves), expensive rollouts, and the need for a good step-level evaluation function. Despite these challenges, approaches like rStar-Math have achieved strong results by using MCTS with mutual consistency verification across multiple reasoning trees.

  3. What is the compute-optimal strategy for inference-time scaling?

    The optimal strategy depends on problem difficulty and available compute budget.

    For easy problems (high base accuracy), a single model call is usually sufficient. Extra compute via majority voting or best-of-N provides minimal improvement because the model is already likely to be correct.

    For medium-difficulty problems, majority voting over 8—​32 samples is often the best value. It requires no reward model and reliably improves accuracy by aggregating diverse reasoning paths.

    For hard problems, PRM-guided beam search is more effective than majority voting because it can prune bad reasoning paths early and allocate compute to promising directions. Best-of-N with PRM scoring is a simpler alternative that works nearly as well.

    For very hard problems, sequential revision with external feedback (e.g., code execution, tool use) is necessary because the model needs to react to information it could not have predicted in advance.

    Snell et al. (2024) showed that the optimal compute allocation shifts as you increase budget: at low budgets, spending on more diverse samples is best; at high budgets, spending on deeper search per sample becomes more effective.

  4. How do reasoning models handle the tension between reasoning effort and latency?

    Reasoning models face a fundamental tension: more thinking generally produces better answers, but takes longer and costs more. A model that always generates 10,000 thinking tokens will be slow on simple questions and wasteful on easy problems.

    Well-trained reasoning models learn difficulty calibration; they produce shorter reasoning chains for easy problems and longer ones for hard problems. Difficulty calibration emerges naturally from RL training: on easy problems, short correct solutions receive high reward, so the model learns not to overthink.

    At the system level, several strategies manage this: compute budgets that cap the number of thinking tokens per request, adaptive routing that sends easy queries to a fast standard model and hard queries to a reasoning model, early stopping that terminates thinking when the model expresses high confidence, and tiered service levels where users choose between speed and quality.

Thread: Making Reasoning Accessible

  1. How does reasoning distillation work, and when is it preferable to RL?

    Reasoning distillation trains a smaller model on the reasoning traces generated by a larger reasoning model. The large model solves many problems, producing detailed chain-of-thought traces. Correct solutions are filtered via rejection sampling, and the small model is fine-tuned on (problem, reasoning_trace, answer) triples.

    DeepSeek found that for small models (7B—​14B parameters), distillation alone is more effective than applying RL directly. Small models lack the capacity to discover complex reasoning strategies through RL; the search space is too large relative to the model’s ability to explore it. But they can learn to imitate reasoning patterns when shown examples.

    Distillation transfers the format of structured reasoning, problem-solving strategies, self-verification habits, and length calibration (how much to think for different difficulty levels). It is the practical method of choice for deploying reasoning capabilities on resource-constrained hardware.

  2. How do you self-teach reasoning to a model using rejection sampling?

    Rejection sampling is a simple but effective approach for improving model reasoning. The process works as follows:

    1. Given a dataset of problems with known answers, use the model to generate N solutions for each problem (e.g., N=32 or 64).

    2. Automatically check which solutions produce the correct final answer.

    3. Filter to keep only correct solutions.

    4. Fine-tune the model on the filtered correct solutions.

    5. Optionally repeat from step 1 with the improved model (iterative self-improvement).

    The method works because even a weak model occasionally produces correct reasoning traces. By training on only the correct ones, the model learns to reproduce the patterns that lead to right answers more frequently. As the model improves, it generates correct solutions more often, creating a virtuous cycle.

    Compared to full RL training, rejection sampling + SFT is simpler to implement, more stable, and often serves as a strong baseline. However, it is less flexible: it uses binary filtering (keep/discard) rather than continuous reward shaping, and it cannot leverage partial credit or reward intermediate steps.

  3. What is STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)? How does it relate to modern reasoning training?

    STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) is an iterative self-improvement method for reasoning. The algorithm works as follows:

    1. For each problem, generate a chain-of-thought rationale and answer.

    2. Keep (problem, rationale, answer) triples where the answer is correct.

    3. For problems where the answer was wrong, provide the correct answer as a hint and ask the model to generate a rationale that leads to it (this is called “rationalization”).

    4. Fine-tune the model on the collected rationales.

    5. Repeat from step 1 with the improved model.

    The rationalization step is critical; without it, the model only learns from problems it can already solve. With it, the model also learns from harder problems by reverse-engineering reasoning paths from known answers.

    STaR is a conceptual precursor to modern RLVR approaches. Both use the model’s own outputs as training data, filtered by correctness. They diverge in optimization: STaR uses supervised fine-tuning on filtered outputs, while RLVR uses reinforcement learning with reward signals. RL is more flexible because it can learn from partial credit and optimize the full output distribution, not just mimic filtered examples.

Thread: The Reasoning Landscape

  1. What is the significance of the DeepSeek-R1 paper for the field?

    DeepSeek-R1, released in January 2025, was significant for several reasons:

    First, it demonstrated that reasoning can emerge from pure RL. DeepSeek-R1-Zero, trained only with GRPO and verifiable rewards on a base model (no supervised reasoning data), spontaneously developed chain-of-thought, self-verification, and reflection.

    Second, it introduced GRPO as a practical alternative to PPO for large-scale RL, eliminating the critic model and halving memory requirements.

    Third, it showed that reasoning distillation is more effective than RL for small models, a key practical finding for deployment.

    Fourth, it was open-weight, providing the most transparent look at reasoning model internals. Previous reasoning models (o1, o3) were closed, making them impossible to study.

    Fifth, it achieved performance competitive with o1 on major benchmarks (97.3% on MATH, approximately 70% on AIME 2024), demonstrating that the reasoning model paradigm was reproducible outside of OpenAI.

  2. What benchmarks are commonly used to evaluate reasoning models, and which are considered saturated?

    Saturated benchmarks (frontier models score above 95%):

    • GSM8K: grade-school math, 8,792 problems. Solved.

    • MATH: competition math, 12,500 problems. Nearly solved by reasoning models.

    • HumanEval: basic Python programming, 164 problems. Solved.

      Differentiating benchmarks:

    • AIME: competition math with creative problem solving. Top models score approximately 73—​83%.

    • GPQA Diamond: graduate-level science questions, designed to be Google-proof. Best models approximately 80%, exceeding human expert performance (approximately 65%).

    • ARC: abstract visual reasoning requiring novel rule induction. Best models approximately 76% with massive compute; humans approximately 85%.

    • SWE-bench Verified: real-world software engineering. Best agentic systems approximately 55—​60%.

    • LiveCodeBench: competitive programming, continuously updated to avoid contamination.

    • FrontierMath (Glazer et al., 2024) and Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE): deliberately built as the next ceiling once MATH and GPQA saturated. FrontierMath uses research-level math problems on which frontier models scored under 2% at release, making it one of the hardest public math benchmarks.

    The saturation of GSM8K and MATH has driven the field toward harder benchmarks. A notable concern is data contamination, where benchmark problems appear in training data, which motivates continuously updated benchmarks like LiveCodeBench and time-bounded evaluation sets.

  3. Compare the major reasoning models: OpenAI o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1, and others.

    OpenAI o1, released in September 2024, was the first widely available reasoning model. It generates internal chains of thought before answering and is particularly strong on math, coding, and science. The training details are not public, but it is believed to use process reward models and extensive RL. o3 and o3-mini followed with improved performance and efficiency.

    DeepSeek-R1, released in January 2025 is the most transparent reasoning model. It uses GRPO with verifiable rewards, and the paper details the full training pipeline. It matches o1 on many benchmarks and is open-weight (671B MoE architecture). The R1-Distill variants bring reasoning capabilities to smaller models (1.5B to 70B).

    Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking, released in Google, 2024, is a reasoning variant of Google’s Gemini models, targeting efficiency. QwQ (Alibaba, 2024) is another open reasoning model in the Qwen family.

    The models share the same core approach; they all use extended thinking through trained chain-of-thought, but they differ in training methodology, scale, and openness. The field is converging on similar training pipelines (RL with verifiable rewards, process supervision, distillation). Note that for commercial models like Claude and Gemini, the exact training methodology behind their thinking modes is not publicly disclosed, so categorizing them as purely RL-trained or SFT-distilled is not possible from available information.

Thread: Challenges, Safety, and Open Questions

  1. What is the error propagation problem in long reasoning chains?

    In a reasoning chain with N steps, errors compound. If each step has independent probability p of being correct, the chain’s overall probability of correctness is pN. For 95% per-step accuracy over 10 steps, overall correctness is about 60%. Over 20 steps, about 36%.

    In practice, errors are worse than this independent model suggests because they are correlated. An error at step k changes the context for steps k+1 through N, often inducing further errors. A wrong intermediate calculation produces wrong inputs for subsequent steps.

    Common manifestations include: forgotten constraints (the model establishes a rule in step 3 but violates it in step 8), arithmetic drift (small errors cascade into large ones), and premise abandonment (the model silently switches approaches when confused).

    Mitigations include process reward models that verify each step, tree-of-thought search that explores alternative paths, external tool use (calculators, code execution) for reliable sub-computations, and chunked reasoning where the model periodically summarizes its progress.

  2. What is the “unfaithful chain-of-thought” problem?

    Unfaithful chain-of-thought occurs when a model’s stated reasoning does not accurately reflect the computational process that led to its answer. The model arrives at an answer through one process (possibly involving biases or shortcuts) and then constructs a plausible-sounding justification.

    Turpin et al. (2023) demonstrated this by showing that biasing features (such as the ordering of multiple-choice options) influence the model’s answer but are never mentioned in the reasoning trace. The model produces a chain of reasoning that appears to justify the answer on substantive grounds, hiding the actual basis for its choice.

    Lanham et al. (2023) tested faithfulness by corrupting chains of thought. When truncating or inserting errors into the reasoning, the model’s final answer often did not change, suggesting the reasoning was not causally responsible for the answer.

    The implications for safety are serious: if chains of thought are post-hoc rationalizations rather than genuine explanations, then reviewing model reasoning for errors or biases will not reliably catch problems in the model’s actual decision-making process.

  3. What are the implications of reasoning model training for AI safety?

    Reasoning models raise several safety considerations:

    • Unfaithful reasoning means we cannot fully trust chain-of-thought as an explanation of model behavior. A model might use a problematic decision process while presenting clean reasoning.

    • Reward hacking is possible when RL-trained models learn superficial patterns that correlate with correct answers (e.g., always writing “let me verify…​” without actually verifying) rather than genuinely improving reasoning quality.

    • Emergent behaviors like self-verification in R1-Zero are encouraging but also raise questions: if models can develop unexpected behaviors through RL, some of those behaviors might not be beneficial.

    • Oversight difficulty increases because reasoning traces can be very long (thousands of tokens), making human review impractical. If process reward models are used for oversight, their own accuracy becomes a safety-critical concern.

    On the positive side, reasoning models offer better transparency than standard models; at least there is a reasoning trace to inspect, even if its faithfulness is imperfect. Process reward models provide a mechanism for step-level verification. And the RLVR paradigm enables training on objective correctness rather than subjective preferences, reducing certain alignment failure modes.

  4. Do LLMs truly reason, or just pattern match? What is the evidence on both sides?

    Evidence points in both directions. The strongest case against genuine reasoning comes from counterfactual evaluations. Wu et al. (2024) showed that when mathematical rules are changed (e.g., “assume multiplication works as addition”), LLM performance drops dramatically, suggesting reliance on memorized solution patterns. Mirzadeh et al. (2024) showed that changing only the numbers in GSM8K problems causes significant accuracy variance.

    Evidence for genuine reasoning includes: (1) reasoning models solve novel competition problems published after their training cutoffs; (2) Merrill and Sabharwal (2023) proved that chain-of-thought provably extends the computational class of problems transformers can solve, a structural capability gain rather than retrieval; (3) behaviors like self-verification emerge through RL without ever being demonstrated, suggesting the model learned a genuine strategy, not a pattern.

    The nuanced view is that LLMs perform something functionally similar to reasoning that works well in practice, but it is grounded in pattern completion rather than formal logic. The boundary between “sophisticated pattern matching” and “genuine reasoning” may not be sharp, and human reasoning itself may be more pattern-based than we typically assume.

  5. What are the key open research questions in LLM reasoning?

    Several questions remain central to the field:

    • Faithfulness: How can we ensure that a model’s stated reasoning faithfully represents its actual computation? Current models can produce plausible but unfaithful reasoning traces.

    • Generalization: Do reasoning models learn general problem-solving strategies, or do they memorize solution patterns for specific problem types? Can reasoning trained on math transfer to legal analysis or scientific discovery?

    • Efficiency: Can we achieve strong reasoning with fewer tokens? Current models often produce verbose reasoning where more concise approaches would suffice.

    • Verification: How good do process reward models need to be for safe deployment? If the PRM has systematic blind spots, it may approve flawed reasoning.

    • Integration with tools: Reasoning models that can use calculators, code execution, and search during their thinking process may overcome many current limitations, but this introduces new challenges in action planning and error recovery.

    • Scaling behavior: Is there a ceiling to test-time compute scaling, or does performance continue to improve as you allow models to think for longer? Early evidence suggests diminishing returns, but the shape of the curve is not yet well understood.

    Executive Takeaways:
    • Core Architecture: Explores OpenVINO Model Optimizer and Inference Engine (IE) integration with OpenCV.
    • Hardware Acceleration: Maximizes throughput on Intel CPUs, integrated GPUs, VPUs, and VPUs through heterogeneous plugin dispatching.
    • Model Zoo Pipelines: Streamlines pre-trained weights ingestion for face detection, image classification, and multi-modal computer vision inference.
    Q1: What is the primary role of OpenVINO Model Optimizer?

    Answer: It converts trained models from frameworks (TensorFlow, PyTorch, ONNX) into Intermediate Representation (.xml network topology and .bin weights), performing layer fusion, precision quantization (FP32 to FP16/INT8), and dead-node elimination.

    Q2: How does OpenCV DNN module leverage OpenVINO Inference Engine?

    Answer: By configuring net.setPreferableBackend(cv2.dnn.DNN_BACKEND_INFERENCE_ENGINE) and net.setPreferableTarget(cv2.dnn.DNN_TARGET_CPU), OpenCV routes layer computations directly through OpenVINO's optimized C++ kernels.

    API Pipeline Workflow:
    1. cv2.dnn.readNet(xml_path, bin_path): Ingests model topology and parameters into memory.
    2. cv2.dnn.blobFromImage(): Resizes input frames, scales pixel values, and converts from HWC to NCHW channel layout.
    3. net.setInput(blob) & net.forward(): Dispatches asynchronous inference to target hardware.
