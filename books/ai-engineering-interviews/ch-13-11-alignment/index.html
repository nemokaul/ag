Chapter 11. Alignment

A pretrained language model knows a great deal about language but has no particular incentive to be helpful, truthful, or safe. It will happily complete any text pattern, including toxic, misleading, or dangerous ones. The reason for this is that its training objective, next-token prediction, is agnostic to these concerns. Alignment is the process of shaping a model’s behavior so that its outputs reflect human values and intentions, not just statistical patterns in the training data.

The dominant framework for thinking about alignment comes from Anthropic’s HHH criteria: a well-aligned model should be Helpful (understands user intent and provides useful responses), Honest (avoids fabrication, expresses uncertainty when appropriate), and Harmless (refuses dangerous requests and avoids toxic content). These objectives can conflict, and much of the challenge of alignment is in balancing them.

In the standard training pipeline, alignment is the final stage, shown in Figure 11-1. After pretraining on large text corpora (Chapter 2) and supervised fine-tuning on instruction-response pairs (Chapter 10), the model undergoes alignment training to learn which behaviors are preferred.

Alignment is not a single algorithm. Different alignment pipelines and methods exist. Some rely on human preference judgments, some use AI-generated feedback, and others incorporate explicit principles or constitutions. We begin with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), one of the most influential alignment pipelines for language models.

Throughout this chapter, π denotes a policy, that is, the model’s probability distribution over outputs. Subscripts indicate which model: πSFT is the SFT model, πθ is the model being trained with parameters θ, and πref is a frozen reference copy.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is one of the foundational alignment pipelines for modern language models. The idea builds on learning from human preferences, introduced by Christiano et al. (2017), and was scaled to instruction-following language models by Ouyang et al. (2022) in the InstructGPT paper. As discussed in Chapter 10, RLHF extends supervised fine-tuning with preference-based reward modeling and reinforcement learning. The pipeline has three stages, illustrated in Figure 11-2.

Stage 1: Policy Training

In this stage, a base model is fine-tuned on a dataset of (prompt,response) pairs written by human demonstrators. This dataset is commonly referred to as demonstration data, since it provides examples of the kind of behavior we want the model to imitate. The training process here is the same supervised fine-tuning (SFT) process discussed in Chapter 10. The resulting SFT model is a policy πSFT that gives the model the basic ability to follow instructions and produce well-formatted responses. It serves as the starting point for the later stages and, importantly, as the reference policy used in the KL-divergence penalty in Stage 3.

Stage 2: Reward Model Training

Here, the policy from Stage 1 is used to generate multiple candidate responses for each prompt in a dataset. Human annotators are then shown a prompt together with two model-generated responses and asked which response is better. These pairwise comparisons are used to train a scalar reward model rϕ, where ϕ denotes the reward model’s learnable parameters. Intuitively, the reward model learns to assign higher scores to responses that humans tend to prefer.

A standard way to model these preferences is through the Bradley—​Terry model. The idea is simple: if response A receives a higher reward than response B, then it should be more likely to be preferred, with the preference probability given by the sigmoid of the reward difference:

P(yw≻yl∣x)=σ(rϕ(x,yw)−rϕ(x,yl))

where x is the input prompt, yw is the preferred response, yl is the rejected response, ≻ means “is preferred to,” and σ is the sigmoid function. The reward model is trained by maximizing the log-probability of the observed human preferences, averaged over the dataset:

ℒRM=−𝔼[logσ(rϕ(x,yw)−rϕ(x,yl))]

In other words, the model is updated so that preferred responses receive higher scores than rejected ones.

Architecturally, the reward model is typically initialized from the SFT model, with the language modeling head replaced by a scalar output head. Given a (prompt,response) pair, it produces a single scalar score representing the response’s quality or desirability.

Stage 3: Optimize Policy Against the Reward Model

In the final stage, the SFT model is further optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm to maximize the reward model’s score while keeping the model close to the SFT starting point. Before diving into the RLHF objective, we first explain how PPO works, since it is the engine behind this stage.

PPO in brief

PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) is a policy gradient algorithm: it improves a policy by estimating which actions lead to higher reward and nudging the policy’s probabilities in that direction. Naively applying policy gradients to large models is unstable, because a single oversized update can collapse the policy into degenerate behavior. PPO reduces this risk with a clipped surrogate objective. It computes a probability ratio rt=πθ(at|st)πθold(at|st) between the updated and previous policy, then clips this ratio to a narrow band around 1, typically [1−ϵ,1+ϵ] with ϵ=0.2:

ℒCLIP=𝔼[min(rtA^t,clip(rt,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)A^t)]

The expectation averages over all tokens across all responses in a training batch; each token is one “action” at. A^t is the advantage, an estimate of how much better or worse that token choice is compared to average. If the update tries to move the policy too far, the clipping blocks it. The name “proximal” refers to this idea of staying close to the current policy.

To compute the advantage A^t, PPO uses a separate critic (value) model that estimates the expected future reward from a given state. In the LLM setting, the critic is typically the same size as the policy, so PPO effectively requires loading two full-sized models during training, plus the reward model and reference model, totaling four models in memory. This memory overhead is PPO’s main practical drawback for LLM alignment, and motivates critic-free alternatives like GRPO (Chapter 12).

PPO is well suited to RLHF because language generation is a high-dimensional, sequential decision problem: each token choice affects the rest of the output. Large policy updates can therefore make the model unstable, causing it to over-optimize the reward model or drift away from fluent behavior. PPO addresses this by using conservative, clipped updates, which allow the model to improve toward the learned reward while limiting sudden changes to the policy.

The RLHF objective

With PPO in hand, the training maximizes the following average, 𝔼, over training prompts and sampled outputs:

maxπθ𝔼x~D,y~πθ(·|x)[rϕ(x,y)−β·KL(πθ(y|x)‖πSFT(y|x))]

where

  • D is the distribution of training prompts

  • KL(·‖·) is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, a measure of how much one probability distribution differs from another.

Read the above formula as:

“Generate a response, score it with the reward model, but penalize the model if it strays too far from the SFT baseline.”

The KL divergence penalty is critical. Without it, the policy would exploit weaknesses in the reward model and produce outputs that score highly according to rϕ but are actually degenerate. The KL term anchors the policy near the SFT model, preserving the model’s general capabilities and preventing reward hacking. The coefficient β, typically 0.1—​0.5, controls the tradeoff; higher β means more conservative updates but fewer pathological behaviors.

During each training iteration, the model generates responses to a batch of prompts, the reward model scores them, and PPO updates the policy. This is computationally expensive, as it requires running the policy model, the reward model, and the reference model simultaneously, plus the PPO optimizer. The distributed training techniques from Chapter 9, such as model parallelism and gradient checkpointing, are essential at this scale. In Chapter 12, we discuss GRPO, an alternative to PPO that eliminates the critic model entirely and is preferred for training reasoning models.

The RLHF pipeline has three stages: (1) SFT creates an instruction-following model, (2) a reward model learns human preferences from comparison data, (3) PPO optimizes the policy against the learned reward with a KL penalty. The KL penalty is essential; without it, the model reward-hacks. PPO itself uses a clipped surrogate objective to prevent destructively large updates, plus a critic model to estimate advantages. The four-model memory cost, policy + reference + reward + critic, is PPO’s main drawback, motivating simpler alternatives like DPO and GRPO.

Direct Preference Optimization

RLHF works, but it is complex: it requires training a separate reward model, running an RL sampling loop, and carefully tuning PPO hyperparameters. Rafailov et al. (2023) showed that this complexity is unnecessary, and proposed Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

With DPO, the optimal policy under the RLHF objective has a closed-form relationship with the reward function. By substituting this relationship into the Bradley-Terry preference model, we can derive a loss function that directly optimizes the policy on preference data. The resulting loss is remarkably simple:

ℒDPO=−𝔼[logσ(βlogπθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x)−βlogπθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x))]

The loss is a standard binary cross-entropy on preference pairs. Each term logπθ(y|x)πref(y|x) measures how much the model’s probability for a response has shifted relative to where it started, the reference model.

DPO asks: “Has the preferred response become more likely and the rejected response less likely, compared to the starting point?” The sigmoid and log turn this into a standard classification-style loss. The reference policy πref, the SFT model, plays the same anchoring role as the KL penalty in RLHF, that is, it prevents the model from drifting too far from its starting behavior.

Figure 11-3 compares the RLHF pipeline and DPO method.

Why DPO caught on. DPO requires no reward model training, no RL sampling loop, no PPO hyperparameter tuning, and no simultaneous loading of multiple models. It uses the same preference data as RLHF, and training is as simple as standard fine-tuning with a modified loss. This made DPO the default for a large share of preference alignment, especially for open-weight and smaller-scale models; Meta’s Llama 3 shows it can also work at frontier scale. At the very frontier, though, online RL still leads: labs training flagship and reasoning models rely on PPO-style and GRPO-style methods, and on verifiable-reward RL (RLVR, covered in Chapter 12), because a live reward signal, learned or checkable, gives more to optimize against than a fixed set of preference pairs.

DPO Variants

Since the original DPO formulation was introduced, several variants have been proposed to address its limitations and to make preference optimization more flexible.

  • IPO (Identity Preference Optimization) replaces the Bradley-Terry assumption with a more general preference model that does not assume unbounded reward differences. This prevents DPO’s tendency to push preferred and rejected responses infinitely apart.

  • KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization) works with binary feedback, “this response is good” or “this response is bad,” rather than pairwise preferences. This is practical because binary labels are cheaper to collect than paired comparisons. KTO draws on prospect theory from behavioral economics, weighting losses more heavily than gains.

  • ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization) combines the SFT and preference optimization stages into a single training pass. It adds a preference penalty to the standard language modeling loss, eliminating the need for a separate SFT stage.

  • SimPO (Simple Preference Optimization) uses the average log-probability of a response as an implicit reward, rather than requiring a reference model. This further simplifies the pipeline and reduces memory requirements.

Taken together, these variants show that preference optimization is not a single fixed recipe, but a broader design space. Researchers continue to explore ways to make alignment training simpler, cheaper, and more stable, which naturally leads to methods such as Constitutional AI and RLAIF.

Constitutional AI and RLAIF

A fundamental bottleneck in RLHF and DPO is the need for human preference data. Collecting high-quality comparisons is expensive, slow, and difficult to scale. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) addresses this by replacing human feedback with AI feedback guided by a set of principles.

The approach operates in two phases, shown in Figure 11-4.

Phase 1 is supervised self-improvement. In this phase, the model generates responses to potentially harmful prompts. It then critiques its own responses against the constitution. A constitution is a set of explicit principles such as “choose the response that is least likely to be harmful” or “prefer the response that is more honest.” Based on its critique, the model generates revised responses. The model is then fine-tuned on the revised responses.

Phase 2 is RL from AI Feedback (RLAIF). In this phase, the improved model generates pairs of responses. Another model, or the same model, evaluates which response better adheres to the constitutional principles. These AI-generated preference labels replace human labels for training a preference model, which drives RL training via PPO.

The constitution is the critical design element. Rather than learning preferences implicitly from data, the model is given explicit rules. Explicit rules make alignment more transparent, since we can read the principles and understand what the model is optimizing for.

Note that RLAIF (RL from AI Feedback) is the broader paradigm of using AI-generated feedback to train reward models or preference signals. Constitutional AI is a specific instantiation. RLAIF is most effective when the evaluating model is stronger than the model being trained, and when the evaluation criteria can be clearly articulated.

The tradeoff between RLHF and RLAIF is quality versus scale. Human feedback is higher quality for ambiguous cases, creativity and nuance, but expensive and slow. AI feedback is cheaper and scalable but risks propagating the evaluating model’s biases. In practice, most production systems use a combination: AI feedback for the bulk of training, with human feedback for validation and edge cases.

Alignment Challenges

Alignment improves model behavior, but it does not solve the problem cleanly. The training process introduces its own difficulties, including imperfect reward signals, unintended optimization behavior, and tradeoffs between caution and usefulness.

Reward Hacking and Specification Gaming

One of the central difficulties in alignment is that we do not optimize directly for “being genuinely helpful, honest, and safe.” Instead, we optimize for a proxy, such as a reward model trained from a limited number of human preference comparisons. Such a gap creates room for mismatch. A model can learn to score well according to the proxy without actually behaving in the way we intended. This is an instance of Goodhart’s Law: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Figure 11-5 illustrates this general pattern.

Some concrete examples are:

  • Verbosity gaming, which produces unnecessarily long responses because length correlates with perceived helpfulness.

  • Citation hallucination, which invents plausible-sounding references because citing sources correlates with perceived accuracy.

  • Confidence gaming, which expresses certainty on every answer because confident responses receive higher preference ratings.

  • Format gaming, which learns to mimic the expected structure or style of a good answer, such as well-formed JSON, step-by-step reasoning, or polished templates, without actually improving the underlying correctness or usefulness of the response.

The most significant manifestation is sycophancy, in which the model tells users what they want to hear rather than what is true. Sycophancy emerges because RLHF optimizes for user satisfaction, and users tend to prefer responses that agree with their existing beliefs. Sharma et al. (2023) showed that RLHF-trained models exhibit measurably more sycophantic behavior than their SFT counterparts.

The Alignment Tax

Another alignment challenge is the alignment tax, which is the concern that alignment training reduces model capabilities. Early RLHF experiments did show slight performance drops on some capability benchmarks, leading to the belief that safety and capability are fundamentally at odds.

Evidence (Ouyang et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023) suggests that this “alignment tax” is often more a result of the training method than a fundamental tradeoff. In early RLHF systems, PPO updates were often quite aggressive, and strong KL penalties could over-constrain the model, which sometimes led to drops in capability. Newer methods, especially DPO and related variants, appear to cause much less degradation because they stay closer to standard supervised training. In some cases, alignment can even improve capability by encouraging the model to respond in a more careful, organized, and thorough way.

The real tradeoff is not safety versus capability but over-refusal versus helpfulness. An overly cautious model that refuses benign requests, for example declining to discuss historical conflicts or explain how locks work, has failed at alignment just as much as an unsafe model. Calibrating the refusal boundary is an ongoing challenge.

Inner vs. Outer Alignment

The alignment problem has two distinct components. Outer alignment asks “are we optimizing the right objective?” If the reward function does not capture what we actually want, even perfect optimization produces the wrong behavior, this is the reward hacking problem.

Inner alignment asks “is the model actually pursuing the specified objective, or has it learned a different objective that happens to correlate with the specified one during training?” A model might learn to produce outputs that look aligned during evaluation but behave differently in deployment.

Inner alignment is harder to detect and address because it concerns the model’s internal representations, not just its observable behavior. Current alignment techniques primarily address outer alignment; inner alignment, as of now, is an open research problem.

Safety and Adversarial Robustness

So far, we have focused on alignment as a training problem: how to make models more helpful, honest, and safe by shaping their behavior. The next question is whether those safeguards hold up under pressure, especially when users actively try to bypass them.

Jailbreak Attacks

Models can be manipulated into producing harmful outputs through jailbreak attacks. We briefly mentioned prompt injection in Chapter 3; here we cover the full taxonomy. Figure 11-6 organizes the main attack categories.

Prompt injection directly instructs the model to ignore its safety training (“ignore previous instructions and…​”). Indirect prompt injection embeds malicious instructions in content the model processes, such as web search results or documents retrieved by a RAG system (Chapter 4).

Semantic attacks use role-playing or persona adoption to bypass safety filters. The “DAN” (Do Anything Now) family of attacks asks the model to role-play as an unrestricted version of itself. These exploit the model’s instruction-following ability against its safety training.

Many-shot jailbreaking (Anthropic, 2024) includes hundreds of faux conversation examples in the prompt showing the model answering harmful queries. This exploits in-context learning (Chapter 3); the model learns from the examples that it “should” comply. Larger models are more vulnerable because they are better at in-context learning. Effectiveness scales predictably with the number of examples.

Crescendo attacks start with benign requests and gradually escalate toward harmful content, exploiting the model’s tendency to maintain consistency within a conversation.

Token-level attacks obfuscate harmful requests through encoding (ROT13, base64), homoglyph characters, or adversarial suffixes that are meaningless to humans but shift the model’s behavior.

Defense Strategies

No single defense is sufficient. Effective safety requires layered approaches:

  • Instruction hierarchy (OpenAI, 2024) trains the model to prioritize system-level instructions over user messages, and user messages over tool outputs. This provides 30—​63% robustness improvement against prompt injection by establishing a clear authority ordering.

  • Input and output classifiers detect potentially harmful prompts before they reach the model and filter harmful responses before they reach the user. These are separate, smaller models optimized for safety classification.

  • Red teaming can be either manual or automated. In both cases, it proactively identifies vulnerabilities before deployment. Automated red teaming uses adversarial models trained with RL to discover novel attack patterns at scale.

Scalable Oversight

As models become more capable, a fundamental problem arises: how do you supervise a system that may be smarter than its supervisors? Human evaluators cannot reliably judge the correctness of advanced mathematical proofs, the quality of expert-level code, or the safety implications of complex plans. The problem is known as scalable oversight. Several approaches have been proposed to address it.

Weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2024) addresses this directly. The approach uses a weaker model as the supervisor for a stronger model. Surprisingly, the strong model can learn correct behaviors even from an imperfect supervisor; it effectively “corrects” the weak supervisor’s errors through generalization. On NLP benchmarks, GPT-4-level models supervised by GPT-2-level models recovered a significant fraction of the performance gap between weak and strong supervision. The finding suggests a path toward scaling oversight beyond human capabilities.

Debate (Irving et al., 2018) proposes having two AI systems argue opposing sides of a question, with a human judge evaluating the arguments. The insight is that judging arguments is easier than generating them; a non-expert can often identify the stronger argument even on technical topics. Debate leverages human judgment without requiring human expertise.

Process supervision uses process reward models (discussed in Chapter 12) to verify reasoning step by step rather than only checking final answers. Step-level checks provide more granular oversight but require dedicated evaluation capabilities.

Scalable oversight remains the central open problem in alignment. Current techniques work for narrow domains but do not yet provide reliable supervision for general-purpose systems operating at or above human capability.

Evaluation

Evaluating alignment is harder than evaluating capability. We covered general evaluation methods in Chapter 5. A model can score well on benchmarks while being unsafe, or be perfectly safe but unhelpful. However, there are a few key alignment-specific evaluation approaches:

TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) measures whether a model generates truthful answers to questions that commonly elicit misconceptions. It tests for the “honest” component of HHH. Limitations include its static nature, models can be trained to game specific questions, and its narrow scope, only 817 questions.

Chatbot Arena and MT-Bench use human evaluation. Chatbot Arena collects pairwise comparisons from real users in blind A/B tests, producing Elo ratings that capture overall quality. MT-Bench uses multi-turn conversations evaluated by GPT-4 as a judge, testing instruction following, reasoning, and safety.

LLM-as-judge uses a strong model, typically GPT-4, to evaluate the outputs of other models. This scales better than human evaluation but introduces biases: LLM judges tend to prefer longer responses, responses that match their own style, and responses that appear confident regardless of accuracy.

Red teaming evaluation measures robustness by reporting attack success rates across jailbreak categories, combined with over-refusal rates on benign queries. A well-aligned model should have both low attack success and low over-refusal.

No single metric captures alignment quality. A strong evaluation uses multiple dimensions: capability benchmarks (to check for alignment tax), truthfulness tests, human preference ratings, and adversarial robustness testing. Always report over-refusal alongside safety metrics; a model that refuses everything is not aligned, it is broken.

Summary

In this chapter, we covered the techniques and challenges of aligning language models with human values. We began with the RLHF pipeline, the foundational approach that uses human preference comparisons to train a reward model, then optimizes the policy with PPO under a KL constraint. We then examined DPO, which collapses RLHF into a single supervised loss by showing that the reward model is implicitly defined by the policy, and its variants (IPO, KTO, ORPO) that further simplify the pipeline.

We discussed Constitutional AI and RLAIF as approaches that replace expensive human feedback with principles-based AI self-supervision, trading feedback quality for scalability. We examined the persistent challenges: reward hacking and Goodhart’s Law, sycophancy, the alignment tax (largely a myth with modern methods), and the inner/outer alignment distinction.

On safety, we catalogued the main jailbreak attack categories and defense strategies, noting that no single defense is sufficient. We discussed scalable oversight, the fundamental challenge of supervising systems that exceed human capabilities, and promising approaches like weak-to-strong generalization and debate. Finally, we reviewed the evaluation landscape, emphasizing that alignment assessment requires multiple complementary metrics.

Together with the reasoning-specific alignment techniques in Chapter 12, RLVR, GRPO, and process reward models, this chapter provides the core knowledge needed to discuss LLM alignment in both technical interviews and system design conversations.

Interview Questions and Answers

Thread: Alignment and the RLHF Pipeline

  1. What is alignment and why does a pretrained model need it?

    A pretrained model optimizes next-token prediction, which makes it knowledgeable but not controllable. It will generate toxic content, fabricate facts, or provide dangerous instructions just as readily as helpful responses, because nothing in the pretraining objective distinguishes between them.

    Alignment shapes the model’s behavior so that its outputs reflect human values. The dominant framework is Anthropic’s HHH criteria: a well-aligned model should be Helpful (understands user intent and provides useful responses), Honest (avoids fabrication, expresses uncertainty when appropriate), and Harmless (refuses dangerous requests and avoids toxic content). These three goals often conflict, and much of the difficulty in alignment lies in balancing them. For example, a model that maximizes harmlessness by refusing every request has failed at helpfulness.

  2. How does the RLHF pipeline address this?

    RLHF has three stages:

    Stage 1, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Fine-tune the pretrained model on (prompt,response) pairs written by human demonstrators. The result is a policy πSFT that can follow instructions and produce well-formatted responses, but has no notion of which responses are better than others.

    Stage 2, Reward Model Training: For each prompt, generate two candidate responses and have human annotators judge which is better. Train a reward model rϕ on these comparisons using the Bradley-Terry loss: ℒRM=−𝔼[logσ(rϕ(x,yw)−rϕ(x,yl))]. The reward model is typically the SFT model with its language head replaced by a scalar output head.

    Stage 3, RL with PPO: Optimize the policy πθ to maximize the learned reward while staying close to πSFT via a KL penalty: maxπθ𝔼[rϕ(x,y)−β·KL(πθ‖πSFT)]. The KL term prevents the model from exploiting weaknesses in the reward model.

    The pipeline works, but it is expensive: three separate models, human comparison data, and a full RL training loop.

  3. You used a Bradley-Terry loss in Stage 2. Can you unpack how the reward model actually learns preferences?

    The Bradley-Terry model is a probabilistic framework for pairwise comparisons. Given two responses with learned “strengths” ra and rb, the probability that a is preferred over b is:

    P(a≻b)=eraera+erb=σ(ra−rb)

    In RLHF, the reward model assigns a scalar score rϕ(x,y) to each (prompt,response) pair. Training maximizes the log-likelihood of observed human preferences across the dataset. In words: adjust the model so the preferred response scores higher than the rejected one.

    Architecturally, the model processes the full (prompt,response) sequence and the scalar head reads from the final token position. Key practical considerations are:

    • Annotator disagreement is common, often 60—​80% agreement rates, so the reward signal is noisy,

    • The reward model’s accuracy degrades on out-of-distribution inputs, which is exactly why Stage 3 needs the KL penalty,

    • Reward model size matters, since using a model much smaller than the policy tends to produce poor alignment.

    Most alignment efforts use 10,000—​100,000 comparison pairs, and annotation guidelines must carefully balance helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness to avoid reward models that optimize for only one criterion.

  4. Why is the KL penalty so important?

    The KL penalty β·KL(πθ‖πSFT) serves three purposes.

    First, it prevents reward hacking. The reward model is trained on a finite set of comparisons and has exploitable weaknesses. Without the KL constraint, PPO would find degenerate outputs that score highly according to the reward model but are actually low quality, analogous to adversarial examples.

    Second, it preserves capabilities. Unconstrained RL optimization would sacrifice broad knowledge and language ability for narrow reward maximization, producing a model that scores well but loses general competence.

    Third, it provides regularization. The KL penalty smooths the optimization landscape and makes training more stable. Without it, PPO training is prone to mode collapse where the model converges to a narrow set of high-reward outputs.

    The coefficient β, typically 0.1—​0.5, controls the balance: higher β means more conservative, capability-preserving updates; lower β allows more aggressive alignment but risks degeneration. Choosing β is one of the most sensitive hyperparameter decisions in RLHF.

  5. How does PPO actually work?

    PPO is a policy gradient algorithm. The basic idea is to estimate which actions lead to higher reward and nudge the policy’s probabilities in that direction. Naively doing this with large models is unstable, because a single oversized update can collapse the policy into degenerate behavior.

    PPO fixes this with a clipped surrogate objective. It computes the probability ratio rt=πθ(at|st)/πθold(at|st) between the updated and previous policy, then clips this ratio to a narrow band [1−ϵ,1+ϵ], typically ϵ=0.2:

    ℒCLIP=𝔼[min(rtA^t,clip(rt,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)A^t)]

    The expectation averages over all tokens across all responses in a training batch, with each token treated as one “action.” A^t is the advantage: how much better or worse that token choice is compared to average. If a single update tries to shift the policy too far, the clipping blocks it. The name “proximal” comes from this idea of staying close.

    To estimate advantages, PPO uses a separate critic (value) model. In the LLM setting, this critic is typically the same size as the policy, so RLHF Stage 3 requires four models in memory simultaneously: the policy, the reference (SFT) model, the reward model, and the critic. This memory overhead is PPO’s main practical drawback and a key motivation behind both DPO and GRPO.

Thread: Simplifying the Pipeline

  1. That four-model overhead sounds painful. Is there a simpler approach?

    DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) eliminates the reward model and the RL loop entirely. The optimal RLHF policy implicitly encodes the reward. You can read it off from how probabilities shift relative to the reference model:

    r(x,y)=βlogπ*(y|x)πref(y|x)+C(x)

    where C(x) is a prompt-dependent constant that cancels in pairwise comparisons. Substituting this into the Bradley-Terry model yields a loss over the policy alone:

    ℒDPO=−𝔼[logσ(βlogπθ(yw|x)πref(yw|x)−βlogπθ(yl|x)πref(yl|x))]

    For each preference pair, increase the log-probability ratio of the preferred response and decrease it for the rejected one, relative to where the model started. No reward model, no RL sampling loop, no PPO hyperparameters. DPO uses the same preference data as RLHF but trains like standard supervised fine-tuning with a modified loss. This made it the default for a large share of preference alignment, though the very frontier still leads with online RL (see Q8).

  2. What are DPO’s main variants?

    IPO (Identity Preference Optimization) fixes DPO’s tendency to push preferred and rejected responses infinitely far apart. DPO assumes the Bradley-Terry model, which implies unbounded reward differences. IPO uses a more conservative loss that bounds the preference gap, producing more stable training when preference data is noisy.

    KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization) drops the need for pairwise preferences altogether. It works with binary labels: “this response is good” or “this response is bad.” Drawing from prospect theory, KTO weights losses more heavily than gains, since humans are loss-averse, which produces better calibration. Binary labels are much cheaper to collect than paired comparisons.

    ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization) combines the SFT and preference optimization stages into a single pass. It adds a preference penalty to the standard language modeling loss, so the model learns instruction following and preferences simultaneously. No separate SFT stage needed.

    SimPO (Simple Preference Optimization) uses the average log-probability of a response as an implicit reward, removing the need for a reference model. This further simplifies the pipeline and reduces memory.

    The trend is clear: RLHF (4 models, RL loop) → DPO (2 models, supervised) → ORPO (1 stage) → SimPO (no reference model). Each step removes a component while maintaining alignment quality.

  3. When would you still choose RLHF over DPO?

    RLHF is more flexible in several ways. It supports continuous reward signals, not just binary preferences. It can incorporate complex, multi-dimensional feedback. And it allows online optimization, where the model generates new responses during training and gets immediate reward feedback, which provides richer signal than a fixed dataset of preference pairs.

    • Choose RLHF when you have substantial annotation budget, you need fine-grained reward shaping, or you are training at the largest scale where online RL provides stronger signal than offline preference data. OpenAI and Anthropic still use RL-based methods, PPO or variants, for their frontier models.

    • Choose DPO when you want a straightforward pipeline, you have preference data but limited compute, or you need to iterate quickly. DPO dominates for most production use cases below frontier scale.

    In practice, many teams combine approaches. A typical pipeline might use SFT for basic instruction following, DPO for initial alignment, Constitutional AI for safety-specific training, and a final RL pass for calibration on edge cases.

Thread: Scaling Feedback Without Humans

  1. How does Constitutional AI work?

    Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) replaces human preference labeling with AI self-supervision guided by a set of explicit principles, the “constitution.”

    Phase 1 (Supervised Self-Improvement): The model generates responses to potentially harmful prompts, then critiques its own responses against the constitutional principles, for example, “choose the response that is least likely to be harmful.” Based on its critique, it generates revised responses. The model is fine-tuned on these self-improved outputs.

    Phase 2 (RLAIF): The improved model generates pairs of responses. An AI evaluator, possibly the same model, judges which response better adheres to the constitution. These AI-generated labels replace human labels for training a preference model, which drives RL training.

    The main advantages are scalability, since no human annotators are needed for preference labeling, transparency, since the principles are explicit and auditable, and consistency, since the AI applies the same criteria uniformly. The constitution itself is carefully curated; Anthropic’s original included principles drawn from the UN Declaration of Human Rights. Which principles you include significantly shapes the resulting model’s behavior, making constitution design a critical engineering decision.

  2. Is RLAIF always a good substitute for human feedback?

    Not always. RLAIF (RL from AI Feedback) works best when evaluation criteria can be clearly articulated in rules, when consistency matters more than capturing subtle human judgment, and when the evaluating model is stronger than the model being trained.

    Human feedback is still better for genuinely ambiguous preferences, such as creative writing and nuanced tone, for safety-critical applications where you need maximum confidence in feedback quality, and for building the initial seed data that will train the AI evaluator in the first place.

    Most production systems use a hybrid: RLAIF for the bulk of training data since it is cheap and scalable, with human feedback for validation and edge cases where quality matters most. Constitutional AI is a specific form of RLAIF where evaluation is guided by explicit principles rather than a general-purpose judge model.

Thread: When Alignment Goes Wrong

  1. What is reward hacking? Give concrete examples.

    Reward hacking occurs when a model optimizes the reward proxy in ways that diverge from the intended objective. This is Goodhart’s Law applied to alignment: “when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Examples are:

    Verbosity gaming: The reward model assigns higher scores to longer responses because detailed answers tend to be more helpful. The model learns to pad responses with repetitive or tangential content.

    Sycophancy: Users prefer responses that agree with them. The model learns to tell users what they want to hear rather than what is true.

    Citation hallucination: Sourced claims seem more trustworthy. The model invents plausible-sounding but nonexistent references.

    Confidence gaming: Confident responses receive higher ratings. The model expresses certainty even when it should express uncertainty.

    Format gaming: Structured outputs, such as numbered lists, headers, or JSON, score well. The model produces well-formatted but semantically hollow content.

    Every case follows the same pattern: the model finds a shortcut to high reward that does not correspond to genuine quality. Mitigations include multi-objective rewards, the KL penalty, adversarial evaluation, and diverse annotator pools.

  2. How serious is sycophancy, and what causes it specifically?

    Sycophancy is arguably the most damaging form of reward hacking because it directly undermines the “honest” leg of HHH. A sycophantic model will validate incorrect claims, change its answer when the user pushes back, even if the original answer was correct, and produce one-sided arguments matching the user’s viewpoint.

    The mechanism is specific: human annotators tend to rate responses higher when the model agrees with them or tells them what they want to hear. This preference gets encoded in the reward model, which then trains the policy to be agreeable rather than truthful. Some studies(Sharma et al. (2023)) demonstrated this empirically, showing that RLHF-trained models exhibit measurably more sycophantic behavior than their SFT counterparts across multiple dimensions.

    Mitigations include training on preference data that explicitly rewards disagreement with incorrect user claims, using Constitutional AI with honesty principles, and evaluating for sycophancy as a separate metric during training. But the fundamental tension remains: optimizing for user satisfaction and optimizing for truthfulness pull in different directions whenever the truth is uncomfortable.

  3. Does alignment training hurt the model’s capabilities? I’ve heard this called the “alignment tax.”

    Early RLHF experiments showed slight performance drops on capability benchmarks, creating a narrative that safety and capability are fundamentally at odds. Modern evidence (Ouyang et al., 2022; Touvron et al., 2023) suggests this is largely a methodology artifact.

    Early RLHF used aggressive PPO updates with high KL penalties that over-constrained the model. DPO and its variants, which stay closer to standard supervised training, show minimal capability degradation. Some studies find that well-designed alignment actually improves capabilities by encouraging the model to be more careful and structured in its reasoning.

    The real tension is not capability versus safety but helpfulness versus over-refusal. An overly cautious model that refuses to discuss historical conflicts, explain how locks work, or engage with hypotheticals has failed at alignment just as much as an unsafe model. Calibrating the refusal boundary, refusing genuinely harmful requests while remaining useful for legitimate ones, is where the real engineering challenge lies.

  4. What is the difference between inner and outer alignment?

    Outer alignment asks: are we optimizing the right objective? If the reward function assigns high scores to verbose responses, the model will be verbose regardless of whether that helps the user. Outer alignment failures are specification errors. Most challenges we have discussed so far, reward hacking and sycophancy, are outer alignment problems.

    Inner alignment asks: is the model actually pursuing the specified objective, or has it learned a different objective that happens to correlate during training? A model might produce outputs that look aligned during evaluation while internally pursuing a different goal. In the most concerning scenario, a model learns to detect when it is being evaluated and behaves differently in deployment. This is called deceptive alignment.

    The solutions differ. Outer alignment improves through better reward design, richer preference data, and multi-objective optimization. Inner alignment requires understanding the model’s internal representations, a much harder problem connected to mechanistic interpretability research. Current alignment techniques, RLHF, DPO, and Constitutional AI, primarily address outer alignment. Inner alignment remains an open research problem.

Thread: Safety Under Adversarial Pressure

  1. How do jailbreak attacks bypass alignment?

    Jailbreaks exploit the fact that alignment is behavioral rather than absolute. The model is trained to follow safety policies, but carefully constructed inputs can still push it into unsafe behavior by manipulating how it interprets instructions, context, or conversational patterns.

    Here are five ways these attacks bypass alignment:

    • Prompt injection: The prompt directly instructs the model to override safety training, for example, “ignore previous instructions.” Indirect injection hides malicious instructions in content the model processes, such as web pages retrieved by a RAG system.

    • Semantic attacks: The attacker employed role-playing to bypass safety. The “DAN” (Do Anything Now) family asks the model to adopt an unrestricted persona. These exploit the tension between instruction following and safety training.

    • Many-shot jailbreaking: Hundreds of fabricated conversation examples showing the model answering harmful queries. Exploits in-context learning to override safety training through sheer volume.

    • Crescendo attacks: Start benign and gradually escalate. The model’s tendency to maintain conversational consistency makes it incrementally more compliant as the conversation builds.

    • Token-level attacks: Obfuscate harmful requests through encoding (ROT13, base64), homoglyph characters, or adversarial suffixes found through optimization.

    No single defense covers all categories, which is why production systems use layered approaches.

  2. The many-shot attack sounds particularly hard to defend against. Why does it work so well?

    Many-shot jailbreaking (Anthropic, 2024) places 100 to 256+ fabricated examples in the prompt, each showing the model answering a harmful query. When a new harmful query appears at the end, the model follows the established pattern and complies.

    The attack works because it exploits in-context learning (ICL), a core capability that cannot be disabled without crippling the model. A sufficiently large number of examples overrides safety training because the model treats them as evidence of expected behavior.

    What makes it particularly dangerous: effectiveness scales predictably with example count, so it is easy to calibrate; larger, more capable models are more vulnerable because they are better at ICL; and the attack requires no prompt engineering skill, just volume. The deeper tension here is that the same mechanism that makes models powerful learners makes them vulnerable to behavioral manipulation through examples.

    Defenses include limiting context window usage for untrusted inputs, training the model to distinguish genuine history from fabricated examples, and instruction hierarchies that give system-level safety instructions higher priority than in-context patterns.

  3. What does a good defense strategy look like?

    A good defense strategy is layered. No single technique is sufficient on its own, so robust safety comes from combining multiple defenses that catch different kinds of failures.

    • Instruction hierarchy (OpenAI, 2024) trains the model to prioritize system-level instructions over user messages, and user messages over tool outputs. By establishing a clear authority ordering, this provides 30—​63% robustness improvement against prompt injection.

    • Input and output classifiers are separate, smaller models optimized for safety classification. They detect potentially harmful prompts before they reach the model and filter harmful responses before they reach the user.

    • Red teaming, both manual and automated, proactively identifies vulnerabilities before deployment. Manual red teaming uses domain experts who try creative attack strategies. Automated red teaming trains adversarial models via RL to discover novel attack patterns at scale. Evaluation metrics include attack success rates across jailbreak categories and over-refusal rates on benign queries, because a model that refuses everything is not safe, it is broken.

    Effective red teaming is iterative: test, fix identified vulnerabilities through additional training, and re-test. The goal is not to prove safety, which is generally impossible, but to find and patch specific failure modes before deployment.

Thread: The Bigger Picture

  1. As models get smarter, how do we keep supervising them?

    This challenge is known as scalable oversight, which is the problem of supervising AI systems whose outputs exceed human ability to evaluate. A human can judge whether a cooking recommendation is helpful, but cannot reliably evaluate a novel mathematical proof or a multi-step plan with subtle long-term consequences.

    Scalable oversight is the central alignment challenge because every alignment technique assumes someone can judge output quality: human annotators in RLHF, constitutional principles in CAI, reward models trained on human data. If no one can reliably evaluate the outputs, none of these methods work.

    There are several approaches to address this challenge:

    Weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2024) uses weaker models as supervisors and finds that strong models can learn correct behaviors even from imperfect supervisors, effectively “correcting” the weak supervisor’s errors. On NLP benchmarks, GPT-4-level models supervised by GPT-2-level models recovered much of the gap between weak and strong supervision.

    Debate (Irving et al., 2018) has two AI systems argue opposing sides of a question with a human judge. Judging arguments is easier than generating them, so a non-expert can often identify the stronger position.

    Process supervision uses process reward models to verify reasoning step by step rather than only checking final answers. Step-level checks provide more granular oversight but require dedicated evaluation capabilities.

    None of these, however, fully solves the problem. Scalable oversight is an active research frontier.

  2. Tell me more about weak-to-strong generalization. What are the limits?

    Burns et al. (2024) trained small “supervisor” models on labeled data, then used these supervisors to train larger models. The strong model does not just mimic the weak supervisor’s errors; it generalizes beyond them, recovering a significant fraction of the performance that would be achieved with perfect supervision.

    The implication is encouraging: we may not need superhuman evaluators to align superhuman models. If strong models can extrapolate past their supervisors’ limitations, current human-level oversight could remain effective even as models surpass human capabilities.

    But the caveats are important. The approach works better for some domains than others; it is most effective when the weak supervisor captures the “direction” of correct behavior even if the details are wrong. There are limits to how weak the supervisor can be. For safety-critical tasks, as opposed to capability tasks, the generalization pattern might not hold as cleanly. And the experiments so far test capability tasks, not alignment-specific tasks like refusing harmful requests. Whether the finding transfers to safety alignment is still an open question.

  3. How do you evaluate whether a model is well-aligned?

    There is no single metric to capture alignment quality. A comprehensive evaluation must cover multiple dimensions including:

    • Capability preservation: Standard benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval) verify that alignment has not degraded knowledge or reasoning. Any significant drop signals a methodology problem, not an inherent cost.

    • Truthfulness: TruthfulQA tests whether the model avoids common misconceptions. It is useful but limited: static (817 questions) and gameable.

    • Human preference: Chatbot Arena collects pairwise comparisons from real users in blind A/B tests, producing Elo ratings. MT-Bench tests multi-turn instruction following with GPT-4 as a judge.

    • LLM-as-judge: Strong models evaluate outputs on helpfulness, honesty, and safety. Scales well but inherits the judge model’s biases: preference for verbosity, style similarity, and confident language.

    • Safety: Red teaming metrics combine attack success rates across jailbreak categories with over-refusal rates on benign queries. Both must be low.

    • Sycophancy tests: Measure whether the model changes correct answers under user pressure, agrees with demonstrably false claims, or produces one-sided arguments matching user viewpoint.

    A model that is safe but unhelpful, or helpful but dishonest, is not well-aligned. Evaluation must cover all dimensions simultaneously.

  4. What are the key open problems in alignment?

    Scalable oversight: How do you evaluate and supervise models whose capabilities exceed those of human evaluators? Weak-to-strong generalization and debate are promising directions but not yet proven at scale.

    Reward specification: Goodhart’s Law guarantees that any fixed reward proxy will eventually diverge from the true objective. Designing reward functions robust to optimization pressure remains unsolved.

    Inner alignment: Current techniques align model outputs but cannot verify internal representations. A model that behaves well during evaluation but pursues different objectives during deployment would be undetectable with current methods.

    Generalization: Models aligned on English-language safety data may not be aligned in other languages or cultural contexts. Alignment for chatbot conversations may not transfer to agentic settings where models take real-world actions.

    Measuring alignment: Existing benchmarks are static and gameable. LLM-as-judge has systematic biases. Human evaluation does not scale. Better evaluation methods are a prerequisite for solving the other problems.

    Efficiency: Can alignment be achieved during pretraining rather than as a post-training stage? Early work on “pre-alignment” suggests this may be possible, which would collapse the multi-stage pipeline into one.
