Chapter 1. Introduction to Vision and Language

We are social and visual animals. We evolved in groups, depending on one another for survival. Our brains are wired for communication, empathy, and connection. Even our emotional well-being is deeply tied to our relationships. To strengthen our bonds, we developed language, a tool to optimize communication, and writing systems that allowed us to reach beyond the limits of face-to-face interaction.

Yet, among all our senses, vision dominates our perception of the world. A huge part of our brain is dedicated to processing visual information. We rely on sight to understand our environment, navigate spaces, recognize others, and pick up subtle social cues like facial expressions and body language.

It’s no surprise, then, that when we set out to build intelligent systems, we were captivated by two grand challenges: teaching them language to communicate with us, and vision to perceive the world as we do. At first, our systems were limited to handling either language or vision but not both together. Over time, however, a set of breakthroughs allowed us to merge these two rich modalities into unified architectures, giving rise to what we now call vision language models (VLMs).

In this chapter, we will briefly explore the history of machine learning and computer vision that led to this new paradigm, and set the stage for the exciting advances ahead.

Today’s VLMs can do things that would have seemed like science fiction just a few years ago. Consider a few examples:

Describe and reason about images

You can hand a model a photograph and ask “What’s happening here?” and get a detailed, contextual answer. Models like OpenAI’s GPT series and Google’s Gemini can interpret charts, extract data from receipts, compare product designs, and answer nuanced visual questions.

Understand long videos

Today’s VLMs can process hour-long videos, summarizing them or answering questions about what’s happening in the video.

Read text in images across languages

Modern VLMs can understand documents natively and recognize text in almost all languages, including handwritten notes.

These capabilities are stunning, but they also raise a natural question: “How do these models actually work?” The truth is, with closed models, we are clueless. But with open models, we can reverse engineer! Since open models, too, are an accumulation of years of scientific work and ideas, we need to first learn the main building blocks and a small history on “how we got to here.” In this chapter, we will go through a bit of history to see how we process images and text so far, so no matter how the paradigm shifts, you will grasp new technologies quickly. On top of this, you will see how the image and text can be bound to each other.

Learning Objective

In this chapter you will learn about the fundamental ideas of processing image and text, and connecting them together. You will learn theory as well as the tools you need to put the theory into practice.

Brief Introduction to Computer Vision

Before the deep learning era, computer vision relied heavily on handcrafted feature extraction techniques. Modern methods inherit from earlier techniques, so understanding them is important.

Let’s briefly take a look at signal decomposition methods.

Signal Decomposition Techniques

While the name might sound intimidating, the idea behind signal decomposition is straightforward. Instead of working directly with an image’s pixel values, these techniques re-express the image using mathematical building blocks such as sines, cosines, and wavelets, making certain properties much easier to see and manipulate. Think of it like switching from prose to sheet music: the content is the same, but the new representation makes specific patterns (rhythm, harmony) immediately visible.

Three transforms matter most for understanding the history of computer vision.

Fourier transform decomposes an image into a sum of sine and cosine waves at different frequencies. Low-frequency waves capture broad, smooth variations (overall lighting, gradual shading), while high-frequency waves capture sharp transitions like edges and fine textures. By working in this frequency representation, engineers could selectively keep or remove certain frequencies—for example, suppressing high-frequency noise while preserving the important structure underneath.

Discrete cosine transform (DCT) is a close relative of the Fourier transform, which packs most of the image’s information into just a few low-frequency coefficients. Natural images are dominated by smooth regions and gradual gradients, so the high-frequency coefficients that encode fine detail carry very little energy. DCT exploits this property and is the cornerstone of JPEG compression.

Where Fourier and DCT tell you which frequencies are present in the entire image, wavelets tell you which frequencies are present and where. This spatial awareness makes wavelets effective for tasks where you need to preserve sharp edges while compressing smooth regions, as in JPEG2000, or for denoising without blurring larger structures.

Let’s see one of these transforms in action. Figure 1-1 shows a sample image and its DCT representation. The DCT works on small, fixed-size patches of the image (typically 8 × 8 pixels), and for each patch, it computes a grid of coefficients. The two axes, u and v, represent horizontal and vertical frequency. Low values of both correspond to smooth, slowly varying shading; high values represent fine checkerboard-like textures. The second panel in Figure 1-1 shows that most energy clusters in the upper-left corner—exactly the low-frequency coefficients. JPEG compression takes advantage of this by discarding the small, high-frequency values (third panel). The result is a much smaller file that looks nearly identical to the original because our eyes are far more sensitive to smooth, low-frequency structures than to fine-grained textures.

These transforms laid the groundwork for how we think about visual information: not as raw pixel grids but as structured signals with patterns we can isolate and manipulate. The next question engineers faced was: how do you detect specific spatial patterns such as edges, corners, and textures directly in the image itself?

Filters and Feature Extraction Kernels

The search for spatial patterns directly in images leads us to feature-extraction kernels and, eventually, to the two breakthroughs that define modern computer vision: convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs).

Before we get there, though, it is worth spending some time with kernels, because they are the conceptual bridge between classical signal processing and deep learning. The edge detectors and texture filters that engineers once designed by hand are exactly the kind of operations that CNNs learn to perform on their own during training. A CNN’s earliest layers typically converge on filters that look remarkably similar to classical edge detectors. So understanding how handcrafted kernels work gives you direct intuition for what a CNN’s first layers are actually doing and why architectures like ViTs later moved beyond them.

A feature extraction kernel is a small matrix (also called a filter) that is applied over an image to detect specific patterns. At each position, the kernel values are multiplied by the underlying pixel values, and the results are summed to produce a single new value. Slide the kernel one step at a time, repeat, and you build up an entirely new representation of the original signal: one that emphasizes whatever pattern the kernel was designed to find.

That sounds abstract, so let’s make it concrete with the simplest possible example. Instead of a full two-dimensional image, consider a one-dimensional signal: a sequence of values that rises, holds steady, and then drops, as shown in Figure 1-2.

Our goal in this example is to detect where this signal changes. To do that, we need a kernel that responds to differences between neighboring values. The simplest kernel that does this is [–1, 1] just two numbers.

Let’s see the kernel in action:

  • When this kernel sits over a flat region (say, two consecutive 5s), it computes (–1 × 5) + (1 × 5) = 0: no change detected.

  • When it faces a jump in the signal (say, from 3 to 7), it computes (–1 × 3) + (1 × 7) = 4: a positive spike that flags the transition.

This tiny kernel acts as a change detector. To apply it, we slide the kernel across the signal from left to right, computing one output value at each position, as illustrated in Figure 1-3. At each step, we align the kernel with two adjacent signal values, multiply them element-wise, and sum the results. The resulting sequence of outputs is our filtered signal.

One detail to notice in Figure 1-3: to produce an output at the very first position, we need a signal value to the left of our sequence where none exists. To handle this, we introduce a zero, a placeholder that lets us compute the output without losing the first position. This is called padding, a standard technique in convolution. Zero-padding (filling with zeros wherever the kernel extends beyond the signal boundary) is the most common strategy, though others exist.

This slide-multiply-sum operation is called a convolution, and its output is called a feature map. That map highlights the specific feature the kernel was designed to find. In our case, the feature map spikes wherever the original signal transitions between values, and sits at zero or near zero wherever the signal is flat (see Figure 1-4).

Figures 1-4 and 1-5 overlay the original signal and its feature map so you can see this relationship directly.

Now let’s extend this idea from one dimension to two. A grayscale image is essentially a 2D signal (a grid of pixel intensities), so instead of a 1D kernel like [–1, 1], we use a small 2D matrix, typically 3 × 3. The operation is the same: slide the kernel across the image, multiply element-wise at each position, and sum to produce one value in the output feature map.

The choice of values in the kernel determines which patterns are detected. Figure 1-6 shows two classic examples: the Prewitt and Sobel kernels. Both are designed to detect edges: the Prewitt kernel in its simplest form, and the Sobel kernel with slightly more weight on the center row or column, which makes it more robust to noise. Each comes in horizontal and vertical variants, so together they can capture edges in any orientation. These kernels were workhorses of image processing for decades and remain useful reference points.

The key takeaway is that convolution gives us a principled way to extract meaningful structure (edges, corners, textures) from raw pixel data. The pattern you detect depends entirely on what values you put in the kernel. For the approaches we have seen so far, those values were chosen by engineers. The question that drove the next era of computer vision was this: what if the kernel values could be learned automatically from data?

From Filters to Convolutional Neural Networks

The idea of applying learned filters to images coexisted with feature extraction: Yann LeCun and colleagues proposed CNNs as early as 1989, applying them to handwritten digit recognition. But for over two decades, CNNs remained a niche approach.

The datasets were too small, the hardware was too slow, and training deep networks was plagued by vanishing gradients, causing learning to stall after a few layers.

Everything changed around 2012, when three things converged:

  • Large-scale labeled datasets (ImageNet, with its 1.2 million images) were offered.

  • GPU-accelerated training, and architectural innovations like ReLU activations and dropout regularization, became available.

  • AlexNet, a CNN trained on ImageNet, dramatically outperformed every handcrafted-feature pipeline on the image classification benchmark.

The deep learning era in computer vision began.

So what does a CNN actually look like? If you have followed along with the kernel examples in Figure 1-6, you already understand the core mechanism. A CNN applies multiple kernels in parallel to an input image, producing one feature map per kernel. Figure 1-7 illustrates this: 32 different kernels yield 32 feature maps, each highlighting different patterns in the image. The difference from classical filters is that these kernels are not designed by hand. They start as random values and are optimized during training through backpropagation: the same gradient-based learning process used in standard neural networks.

If you are already familiar with feedforward neural networks (FFNNs), the important thing to note is that a CNN reuses most of the same machinery: layers of nodes, activation functions, a loss function, and backpropagation. What defines a CNN is specifically the feature extraction stage: instead of connecting every input pixel to every node (as a fully connected layer would), convolutional layers slide small kernels across the image, sharing weights spatially. This weight sharing is what gives CNNs their efficiency on image data and their ability to detect the same pattern regardless of where it appears in the image.

The result is a natural hierarchy. Early convolutional layers learn low-level features such as edges, textures, and color gradients. Those layers look strikingly similar to the handcrafted Sobel and Prewitt filters we saw earlier. Deeper layers combine these into increasingly abstract representations: corners become shapes, and shapes become object parts like ears or wheels.

The final layers pass these rich feature representations to a task-specific head. For image classification (Figure 1-8), the head is simply a fully connected layer with one output node per class (for example, “cat” or “dog”), each producing a probability score.

Turns out that a convolutional backbone for feature extraction followed by a task-specific head is highly versatile. The same backbone that learns to detect edges and textures to classify cats and dogs can be repurposed with a different head for entirely different tasks. Are you ready to explore some of those pipelines?

Other Basic Convolutional Neural Network–Based Pipelines

Let’s talk about a few other CNN pipelines. All those other applications have something in common (in addition to cats in the example): they rely on a common backbone for feature extraction. Think of the backbone as the main body of the neural network that handles the core image processing work. It’s the part that extracts important features from images before any task-specific parts take over. You briefly see classification in Figure 1-9.

Inspired by the classification pipeline, you can see the object detection pipeline in Figure 1-10.

Can you spot the differences? For object detection, there is a different layer on top of extracted features called object detection head. Wait, what is this? The object detection head identifies multiple objects by predicting both their categories and bounding box coordinates. The final layer outputs class probabilities along with the locations of detected objects of interest within the image, as shown in Figure 1-10.

Unlike a classification head that simply categorizes whole images, the object detection head works on the rich feature maps produced by the backbone network. This head is specifically designed to process these extracted features in a way that enables both localization and classification of multiple objects.

The next pipeline is image segmentation. Image segmentation is a more fine-grained task that occurs on pixel level, where each pixel is assigned a class probability. When class probabilities are high in adjacent pixels, those pixels form a segmentation mask. Commonly, semantic segmentation assigns a single segmentation mask to all instances of an object in a given image, as shown in Figure 1-11.

Instance segmentation detects different instances, as you can see in the output of Figure 1-12.

Doing a quick recap, each of these pipelines solved one specific task. But notice what they have in common: they all rely on the same backbone for feature extraction. The classification network, the object detector, and the segmentation model all share the same convolutional layers that learn to detect edges, textures, and shapes. The task-specific head on top is what makes each pipeline different. This realization led to one of deep learning’s most powerful ideas: transfer learning.

Transfer Learning

Transfer learning allows practitioners to use large pretrained models for their specific use cases. Instead of spending compute and time for a model to learn universal features such as edges and corners or general objects, the model training only focuses on the domain-specific task at hand. With a reduced amount of data and compute, you could repurpose an existing CNN to fulfill your specific application, and this way, transfer learning becomes a good hack.

To train the models, you initialize the CNN kernels randomly, and through the training, the optimization process tweaks the kernels to detect features. When trained on diverse data, the feature extraction kernels become more universal. This means you can repurpose them. This is called transfer learning, where you transfer the information learned in a neural network to another neural network for the task at hand. The repurposed models are also called pretrained, and the pretrained feature extraction models are often referred to as backbones. Modern computer vision tasks mostly utilize pretrained models with a sophisticated task-specific head with feature refiner networks on top of them. These refiners are referred to as neck. The neck helps create richer representations that help the network better understand objects at various scales and positions in the image (Figure 1-13).

But what makes a pretrained model good enough to be used for transfer?

Computer Vision Backbones

Many pretrained models stood the test of time and have been adopted widely by practitioners. However, there is no one-backbone-fits-all type of model. Some backbones are used widely across many tasks, and some of them are used the most for a given task. Let’s review some of the most influential backbones.

ResNet

Early convolutional deep neural networks were hard to train due to vanishing and exploding gradients. ResNet was the first deep CNN to solve this using residual connections. The idea is simple: pass the input directly to the layer after the next activation, skipping one or more layers. This prevents gradients from vanishing (or exploding), which helps when scaling the network for depth.

Directly adding the input is called a residual connection, just passing the input (“x” in Figure 1-14) to the activation on top of the layer output. Another variant is a projection shortcut, which uses a 1 × 1 convolution to match the input shape to the output shape.

ResNet combined residual connections with bottleneck layers (using 1 × 1 convolutions to reduce dimensions before more expensive 3 × 3 convolutions) and batch normalization (forcing layer inputs to have zero mean and unit variance to stabilize training). Together, these ideas allowed CNNs to scale to over 100 layers, and ResNet became one of the most widely used backbones in computer vision.

ResNet has built upon a few other preexisting ideas to scale to 100+ layers. The first one is bottleneck convolutions, which is simply applying convolutions with filters of size (1, 1) to reduce the dimension before more complex convolutions with filters of size (3, 3) or (5, 5). After, the dimensions are rescaled to the original shape by applying another convolution with a filter of size (1, 1). ResNet combines this with residual blocks like those shown in Figure 1-15.

The last important element in ResNet’s success is batch normalization, forcing layer input batches to have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1. This helps stabilize the training of deep neural networks. ResNetv1 normalizes the processed signal after the convolutions and before ReLU activations.

MobileNet

MobileNet introduced a fundamental shift in architecture design in 2017. Where ResNet focused on depth, MobileNet focused on efficiency, making neural networks viable for real-time applications on resource-limited devices. The main difference is depthwise separable convolutions, which divide standard convolutions into two smaller steps: a depthwise operation (one filter per input channel) and a pointwise operation (a 1 × 1 convolution to combine channels). This reduces computation by 8–9x while maintaining comparable accuracy, making models like MobileNet practical for phones and embedded systems.

U-Net

U-Net had a radically different origin: it came from the biomedical world with a focus on image segmentation. Its symmetric “U-shaped” architecture features a contracting path that captures context (seen in Figure 1-16) and an expanding path that enables precise localization, connected by skip connections that preserve spatial information.

U-Net’s ability to perform pixel-precise segmentation with limited training data made it a mainstay in medical imaging. It later became a key component in diffusion-based image generation and is widely used in semantic segmentation across healthcare, robotics, and satellite imaging.

U-Net is widely used today in tasks like medical image segmentation, diffusion-based image generation, and semantic segmentation in fields ranging from healthcare and robotics to satellite imaging.

CNNs transformed computer vision, but they had an architectural limitation: convolutional kernels only see a small local neighborhood at each layer. Capturing long-range relationships, like understanding that a leash in one corner of an image is connected to a dog in another, required stacking many layers deep. A fundamentally different approach was brewing in another field, one that could relate any part of an input to any other part in a single operation: attention.

Going from image to text and back

So far, we’ve focused on how machines learn to see, extracting features from images through kernels, CNNs, and increasingly powerful backbones. But vision is only half of the story. To build models that can look at an image and describe what they see, answer questions about it, or follow visual instructions, we also need models that understand and generate language. That side of the story was unfolding in parallel, and it produced the single most important architectural idea in modern AI: the transformer. Transformers not only revolutionized the language but later were adapted to vision, too, so let’s take a look at how the revolution began.

Transformers and Their Origins in Language

Text is handled differently than images. In images there are pixels spread in space and across different channels. In text, the data is spread sequentially, and we want to learn semantic relationships between words and how they exist or relate to the real world.

Early natural language processing (NLP) applications tried to use word-to-word co-occurrence to estimate the context of a given sentence or a paragraph with very basic models. These applications got us only so far, though. We not only wanted the application to understand language, but we wanted it to generate it as well.

The first successful deep learning applications in NLP were based on an architecture called recurrent neural network (RNN). Practitioners divided the text into smaller pieces, building a mapping from these text fragments to numbers called tokens, which were then fed into the RNN. Initially, tokens were often created at the word level, but this approach proved inefficient over time, as more advanced tokenization methods at the subword level allowed for better handling of vocabulary and out-of-vocabulary words. The processor that splits tokens is called a tokenizer.

Recurrent neural networks and long-short-term memory

An RNN is primarily composed of cells that output a vector, which is repeatedly fed back into the cell along with the next input. In the first step, the cell receives only the first input—for example, a word. In the second step, the cell takes the next word and the output from the previous step as its inputs. These outputs, called hidden states, represent the information the network is learning and carrying forward through the sequence. As we progress through the paragraph, unrolling an RNN reveals how each word is processed step by step along with the evolving hidden states, as shown in Figure 1-17 (based on an example in the book Natural Language Processing with Transformers [O’Reilly]).

Naturally, the effect of the initial inputs to the hidden state shrinks as the sequences grow. Moreover, it’s hard for the gradients in the network to be in the small range between vanishing and exploding, making it harder for models to learn. Long-short-term memory (LSTM) networks (1997) and gated recurrent units (GRU) (2014) were introduced to address these problems. LSTMs simply have “gates”: functions that determine if a given hidden state will be kept, added, removed, or updated. GRUs are simplified versions of LSTMs with gates that update, remove, or add new hidden states.

What happens to the final hidden state is key. It acts as a compressed representation (an encoding) of the entire input sequence. RNNs that focus on producing these encodings are called encoders. You can feed an encoding to a classification layer (for tasks like sentiment analysis). You can also choose to feed it to another RNN that decompresses it word by word to generate text. These generating networks are called decoders. Many architectures combine both, as shown in Figure 1-18 (based on an example in the book Natural Language Processing with Transformers [O’Reilly]). Both encoder-decoder networks and decoder-only networks can predict the next word.

Encoder-decoder LSTMs are still very limited in the length they can process as the decoder receives a compression of the whole input to decode. You could feed all hidden states of the encoder to all cells of the decoder, but it would be very computationally intensive. To address the limitations of RNNs and pick which hidden states to particularly attend to, the Bahdanau attention mechanism was introduced in 2014. Neural networks with an attention mechanism are called transformers, and they are the architecture used by advanced systems such as ChatGPT. We will go through the attention mechanism in detail in upcoming chapters.

The boom of transformers in text

Transformers had their initial boom with the BERT model in 2018, as it enabled multiple successful applications up to the present day.

BERT was easy to train due to its self-supervised objective. Essentially, a small portion of words in the text were masked randomly and the model was trained to predict those words, which allowed it to learn from any corpus of text. After the initial training, BERT can be used in transfer learning for many tasks simply by replacing the task-specific head, as seen in Figure 1-19 (based on the image in the article by Jacob Devlin et al.). The machine learning community has trained many BERT models in different languages and domains thanks to the lack of dependency on labeled data.

BERT is the most successful encoder model: it revolutionized NLP by achieving state-of-the-art results across numerous language tasks. These days we still get many BERT variants with architectural tweaks to solve natural language understanding tasks.

GPT-2 was the most successful decoder-only mode. It demonstrated remarkable text generation capabilities across diverse domains with minimal task-specific training.

This model is trained through masking the next word and trying to predict it. This is called next token prediction or autoregressive training, as shown in Figure 1-20.

T5 is another model that became popular, given its possibilities to accomplish a wide range of tasks, training on instruction-answer pairs (see Figure 1-22). Its possibilities covered translation, summarization to detecting sentiments, or assessing entailment between two sentences (see Figure 1-21, which is based on an example from Colin Raffel et al., “Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning with a Unified Text-to-Text Transformer”). T5 was the most adopted encoder-decoder model.

Vision Transformers

Transformers had conquered language. The natural question was: could the same attention mechanism work for images?

CNNs built visual understanding from local patterns upward: from edges to textures to parts to objects. But self-attention offered something fundamentally different: the ability to relate any part of an input to any other part directly, regardless of distance. In a CNN, understanding that a leash in one corner of an image is connected to a dog in another requires information to travel through many stacked layers. With attention, that relationship can be captured in a single operation. This wasn’t just a technical upgrade; it represented a shift in how we think about visual representation.

ViTs brought this idea to images by splitting them into small patches and treating each patch like a token in a sentence. The attention mechanism then learns relationships across all patches simultaneously. The initial ViT architecture was similar to BERT, as shown in Figure 1-22 (example taken from Alexey Dosovitskiy et al., “An Image Is Worth 16×16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale”).

ViT took images of 224 × 224 resolution and split them into 16 × 16 patches, which yield (224/16)2 = 196 patches. These patches are then flattened into a one-dimensional vector of length 768 (162 × 3-channels) and fed to a linear layer, converting these patches into a 768-dimensional embedding vector. The system adds a special token at the beginning of the image patches to signal the classification goal and includes positional information with each patch. It then sends these enhanced patches through the transformer network. Finally, a simple multilayer perceptron takes the transformer’s output and determines the image classification.

ViT changed the paradigm in computer vision as evidenced by the large list of ViT variants, including dynamic-sized patches, modifying attention, adding convolutions on top of the attention layers or applying hierarchy through stages of ViT, learning patches (just like a tokenizer learns tokens), and different supervision techniques. We will go through some of the noteworthy architectures that advanced vision capabilities of multimodal models.

First, let’s try ViT with only a few lines of code! We will explain the code later, but to get started quickly, you need to install the Hugging Face transformers library:

pip install transformers

To initialize an image classification pipeline, think of it as a box that contains the model and everything else you need to do image classification. We will use the ViT model from the Hugging Face platform, trained on many classes:

from transformers import pipeline

Now that we initialized the pipeline, let’s use the model to run inference on the image:

Vision transformer variations
Let’s look at a few ViT variations that are used heavily in today’s VLMs. Masked autoencoder (MAE) is a standard ViT model with a twist: it divides images into patches, removes some of the image patches, and learns the image features through reconstructing images. This idea was later used in some of the initial image captioning models. You can see the main idea illustrated in Figure 1-23 (based on a paper by Kaiming He et al.).
The community has built many models with different image handling approaches to improve performance. BEiT is a similar approach to BERT but for images rather than text. BEiT learns to represent image patches as visual tokens, and the model is pretrained with masked tokens to do so.
Another important modification on top of vanilla ViT is hierarchical ViTs. These models take in images in high resolution and split them into smaller patches like 4 × 4, then repeatedly downsample them to extract features, making extracted features bigger and bigger. This is in contrast to vanilla ViT, where the model learns details first and looks at the bigger picture in later stages, introducing locality bias. This trick is used for models that predict segmentation masks or bounding boxes from text description of objects of interest.
The last major modification on top of ViT is self-supervision techniques. ViTs require a lot of data for pretraining, so scaling over data as well as the architecture is necessary. Enter data efficient image transformer (DeiT): it’s a model trained using a larger model to label data for the training of the small model (also called distillation). While the distillation technique used in DeiT is more complex, for now, you just need to know that this technique was used in early multimodal models. The MAE model also uses self-supervision: the training objective is to predict the masked image patches rather than class labels. MAE was used in early image captioning models as image backbone, and we still come across MAE or its variants in new models.
Now that we learned everything that led to the multimodality revolution (i.e., mixing text and images), let’s talk about the first successful open application of multimodality: Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP).
Connecting images and text: CLIP
How do we associate the objects we see with language? In the early stage of our lives, the objects are always pointed out and identified so we can learn their names. By asking a lot of questions, we learn concepts verbally, and we associate attributes with objects. Models also learn similarly: they are exposed to a lot of image and text pairs until they associate certain words with certain attributes and objects in images. Phrases are associated with images differentially, and the phrase and text similarity match is scored as it is shown in Figure 1-24.
Notice how the phrases with the word “cat” inside are assigned a higher score to cat images, while the dog is isolated. This is the first and most accepted way of teaching models how to connect modalities.
Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) is the first successful attempt that connected the two modalities, understanding both image and text and translating one to another. The idea behind CLIP is as follows: an image encoder and a text encoder are pretrained in contrastive manner. The training data consists of image and text pairs, and the training objective is to bring matching image-text pairs’ embeddings closer together while pushing dissimilar ones apart contrastively (see Figure 1-25, which uses an example from Alec Radford et al., “Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision”).
In detail, each image and text in the training set (think of each combination as one example) are passed through their encoders, and these embeddings are then normalized and multiplied with dot product. After this, image-to-text prediction loss and text-to-image prediction loss are calculated separately, and final loss is the average of two losses.
To understand Figure 1-25 better, consider a training batch of N image-text pairs. After encoding, you have N image embeddings (I₁ – IN) and N text embeddings (T₁ – TN), and computing the dot product between every combination produces an N × N similarity matrix. The diagonal holds the correct pairs: I₁·T₁ is the dog photo matched with “pepper the aussie pup,” and so on, and the contrastive loss maximizes these diagonal scores while suppressing everything off-diagonal, symmetrically from both the image side and the text side. The encoders themselves are transformers for text and either a ViT or ResNet for images, each producing a single vector per input before the dot product. What makes this deceptively simple objective so powerful is scale: CLIP was trained on 400 million image-text pairs scraped from the web, with no explicit class labels; supervision comes entirely from natural image-caption co-occurrence, which is why the resulting shared embedding space generalizes so broadly across visual and linguistic concepts. Once pretrained, the model can be used to match images and texts together, as shown in Figure 1-25 (example from Alec Radford et al., “Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision”).
As shown in Figure 1-25, during training, CLIP encodes images and text separately, maximizing similarity between matching pairs. At inference time (Figure 1-26), it encodes the image and all candidate labels and then selects the label with the highest dot product similarity.
During the inference, the model takes in an image and multiple candidate labels, and returns a high similarity score for text that describes the image the best.
CLIP was the first ever widely adopted VLM. Later on, the term evolved to models that can take image and text input, and output text, like GPT-4o. CLIP has found so many uses in the industry, from image-generation pipelines to image-text search. CLIP can do zero-shot image classification. If this is your first encounter with the term zero shot, it’s essentially used for tasks and models that can generalize for inputs and outputs beyond what it’s trained with, doing a task on “zero shot” without giving further training, out of the box. A model trained with yellow cats and black dogs can still recognize yellow dogs, so it is a zero-shot image classification model.
In short, when given an image and a bunch of open-ended labels to match the image, zero-shot classification models can assign similarity scores to match image to text (and vice versa). On a high level, it looks like the application seen in Figure 1-27. CLIP-like models are still used today as the vision backbone for most successful VLMs.
On the right side in Figure 1-27, there are candidate labels and their similarity scores against the image assigned by the model.
Let’s try using the CLIP model. To do so, we can initialize as follows, with the model based on an example from Hugging Face:
from transformers import pipeline 

model_id = "openai/clip-vit-large-patch14"
pipe = pipeline("zero-shot-image-classification", model=model_id)
We will use the same image as we did in the ViT model example but with three different candidate open-ended labels. The model will assess the most likely image description:
labels = ["bee on a flower", "bee in the hive", "bee in the sky"]
pipe(image="https://oreil.ly/ne2UQ",  candidate_labels=labels)
[{'score': 0.8133557438850403, 'label': 'bee on a flower'},
 {'score': 0.1714152991771698, 'label': 'bee in the hive'},
 {'score': 0.015228924341499805, 'label': 'bee in the sky'}]
As you can see, the model assigns the highest probability to the most relevant caption!
CLIP showed that vision and language could live in a shared embedding space. But it could only score how well an image matched a caption; it couldn’t generate new text describing what it saw. The next breakthrough would combine CLIP-style vision encoders with text-generating language models, creating systems that could truly see and speak.
Modern Vision Language Models
So far we have only talked about models that can understand images, but modern VLMs go way beyond this and not only understand but generate text based on what they understand.
Let’s have a short inference example to see what these models can do. These models are called image-text-to-text in transformers (because they take in image and text and output text):
from transformers import pipeline
pipe = pipeline("image-text-to-text", model="OpenGVLab/InternVL3-1B-hf")
When prompting these models, we need to provide conversation turns (user said this, model said that, and so on) and specify that the prompt includes an image:
messages = [
    {
        "role": "user",
        "content": [
            {"type": "image", "url": "https://oreil.ly/jlwF2"},
            {"type": "text", "text": "What animal is on the candy?"}
        ]
    },
]

result = pipe(text=messages, return_full_text=False)
result[0]["generated_text"]
The pipeline outputs “The candy on the hand has a turtle design,” which is the correct answer! But let’s go deeper. What do these models look like on the inside? We will see more about this in Chapter 6, but for now, we can say that they follow a common blueprint: they consist of an image encoder (often a ViT), a projection layer, and a text decoder (often a transformers-based language model). The encoder and decoder are trained on massive amounts of data, and to connect two pretrained models, the researchers put a projection layer in between them. How does the projection layer take inputs, though, and how does it connect, when image encoder outputs and text decoder inputs are completely different from each other? We initialize a linear layer with input dimensions of encoder output dimensions, and output dimensions of decoder input dimensions, then pass the image patches from this layer, and concatenate the output with text inputs. You can see this visually in Figure 1-28.
During training of the projection layer, the image encoder and text decoder are frozen (they aren’t learning) (see Figure 1-29). The projection learns how visual patterns relate to words, phrases, and sentences. Over time, the model learns not just what is in an image but how to talk about it in a useful way. After the training of the projection layer, the entire model can be trained, or the image encoder can still be frozen. The model is trained on more specific tasks like instruction following, where the training data consists of images, instructions, and responses. We will see this more in detail in Chapter 5.
We have traced the arc of ideas that led to today’s VLMs: from early signal processing and handcrafted features to the deep learning revolution with CNNs to transformers that conquered first language and then vision and finally to models like CLIP that bridged the two modalities. Modern VLMs combine all of these advances—a vision encoder, a language decoder, and a learned projection between them—into systems that can see and speak.
In Chapter 2, we’ll get our hands dirty: we’ll introduce the Hugging Face open source ecosystem and build our first multimodal application from scratch.
Brief Introduction to Hugging Face Open Source Ecosystem
A typical machine learning workflow requires either training a model from scratch or taking a pretrained model and fine-tuning it on a dataset with low-level PyTorch implementation of model architectures. For multimodal use cases, using a pretrained model is preferred as training from scratch is too costly. But where do you find these pretrained models, and how hard is it to fine-tune them?
The Hugging Face ecosystem makes it easy for the community to fine-tune and share models. The success of the transfer learning paradigm is mostly owed to the open source libraries of Hugging Face.
Hugging Face ecosystem consists of two main components: a hub and a set of libraries that work together to simplify access to models, datasets, and demos with an easy-to-use code layer.
Hugging Face Hub
Hugging Face Hub is the largest platform for sharing machine learning models, datasets, and demos. As of April 2025, it hosts over 1.6 million models and 360K datasets for many machine learning tasks, as shown in Figure 1-30 (based on an example from Hugging Face).
We will use Hugging Face model repositories heavily throughout this book, so let’s take a look.
Each model repository (Figure 1-31) consists of three tabs: model card, files, and versions and community.
The “Model card” tab in Figure 1-31 contains information related to the model, a widget to use the model, and a model tree. The model card contains metadata and documentation related to the model. Metadata contains information to filter for this model, such as task, the library, and license of the model. The documentation part of the model contains inputs and outputs, a snippet to get started with the model, training details, performance metrics, hardware benchmarks, and more. We highly recommend checking model cards to get started with models in case of API changes over time. Model tree shows all the fine-tuned or quantized versions of that model.
The “Files and versions” tab contains model weight files and configuration files required to load the model. The “Community” tab contains discussions and pull requests of the model repository. Discussions are very useful to ask questions about the model and get answers from the model owners and the community (shown in Figure 1-32).
Hugging Face Hub allows token-based access to modify and access repositories and many other operations. Throughout this book, you will read and write repositories. To do that, let’s create a token.
Go to token settings, and on top right, select “Create new token”. Tokens come in three access types: fine-grained, read, and write. Fine-grained allows creation of tokens with read or write access to specific repositories or repositories under a specific user and organizations. We really encourage using fine-grained tokens for security reasons. Read and write tokens allow read and write to all the repositories you have access to, including organizations where you have read and write access.
Libraries
Now that you have learned about the Hub, let’s take a look at the open source libraries of Hugging Face.
Transformers
Transformers library provides easy-to-use abstractions for pretrained models so you can easily fine-tune state-of-the-art models. It is now the central format in which all new pretrained models are implemented: transformers library is integrated to different libraries both within and out of the Hugging Face ecosystem. A model implemented in transformers can be easily exported to different formats for different hardware or inference solutions, so it is a must-learn for every machine learning practitioner working on cutting-edge solutions.
Transformers provide three main abstractions to load, infer with, and fine-tune models. We can summarize three of the abstractions that we will use:
AutoModel and AutoProcessor
In transformers, each model has its own class (e.g., CLIPModel is the class for CLIP). AutoModel classes automatically load model weights for a specific task at hand. These model weights can be from a local folder or a model repository on the Hugging Face Hub. For instance, we load VLMs with AutoModelForImageTextToText class, and zero-shot image classification models with AutoModelForZeroShotImageClassification class. Auto classes for multimodal models load the whole model, but we can also load parts of models. For example, we can use CLIPTextModel to load the text tower of CLIP.
We need to load the models along with the preprocessors to process inputs before passing the model. To load a processor locally or from the Hugging Face Hub model repository, we can use the AutoProcessor class. Processors contain both text processor (the tokenizer) and the image processor.
Here’s an example of loading CLIP and inferring with an image:
from PIL import Image
import requests
from transformers import AutoProcessor, 
AutoModelForZeroShotImageClassification

model = AutoModelForZeroShotImageClassification.from_pretrained
("openai/clip-vit-large-patch14").to(“cuda”)
processor = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-large-patch14")

url = "https://huggingface.co/datasets/vlmbook/images/resolve/main/bee.jpg"
image = Image.open(requests.get(url, stream=True).raw)

inputs = processor(text=["a bee on a flower", "a bee in a hive"],
images=image, return_tensors="pt", padding=True).to(“cuda”)

outputs = model(**inputs)
probs = outputs.logits_per_image.softmax(dim=1)
print(probs)
# tensor([[0.5359, 0.4641]], grad_fn=<SoftmaxBackward0>) 
# probabilities for each class respectively
Pipeline
We have already used the pipeline abstraction. Think of the pipeline as a box that contains the processor and the model. In pipelines, preprocessing of inputs, inference, and postprocessing the model outputs are abstracted:
from transformers import pipeline 
image_classifier = pipeline(model="openai/clip-vit-large-patch14", 
                            task="zero-shot-image-classification") 
image_classifier(image = "https://oreil.ly/ne2UQ", 
                 candidate_labels = ["a bee on a flower", 
                 "a bee in a hive"])
# [{'score': 0.9376115202903748, 'label': 'a bee on a flower'}, 
#  {'score': 0.06238847225904465, 'label': 'a bee in a hive'}]
In Figure 1-33 you can see how the pipeline looks for the zero-shot image classification task.
Trainer
Model fine-tuning and training loops follow a common workflow. Trainer is a plug-and-play abstraction to handle passing in datasets, handling loss calculation as well as advanced tricks to get the best out of small hardware such as gradient accumulation and gradient checkpointing. Trainer is also supported by Accelerate library for training on multiple hardware. We will see how to use Trainer in more detail in upcoming chapters.
Accelerate
Fine-tuning large models requires different tricks such as mixing different weight precisions, data parallel training, and multiple hardware. Accelerate is a library that enables the same PyTorch code to be run across any distributed configuration by adding just four lines of code. It handles all of these under the hood through Trainer, easily enabling the use of advanced tricks to train large models.
Datasets
Working with large datasets across different modalities is challenging. These datasets often require heavy memory larger than RAM and take a lot of time to process. Datasets library helps reduce the memory constraints and enables fast processing of datasets. It also comes with different features such as streaming datasets from Hugging Face Hub, which enables easy discovery of datasets. The library comes with support for all modalities, and is tailored for different needs.
Let’s take a look at how a dataset is loaded. First, make sure to have datasets installed:
pip install datasets
We will use load_dataset to load a small visual question-answering dataset:
from datasets import load_dataset
ds = load_dataset("merve/vqav2-small")
print(ds)
ds here is an object of type DatasetDict, and this dictionary contains the different splits the dataset has. Each split is a Dataset object with features and number rows:
DatasetDict({ 
validation: Dataset({ 
features: ['multiple_choice_answer', 'question', 'image'], 
num_rows: 21435 }) })
To actually bring the dataset itself, simply access the split by calling ds["validation"]. To get the first example, call ds["validation"][0]:
{'multiple_choice_answer': 'carnival ride', 
'question': 'Where are the kids riding?', 
'image': <PIL.JpegImagePlugin.JpegImageFile image mode=RGB size=640x424>}
We can use map to preprocess the dataset like the next example. For instance, this example lowercases all the questions:
def preprocess(example): 
example["question"] = example["question"].lower() 
return example 
ds["validation"] = ds["validation"].map(preprocess)
print(ds["validation"][0]["question"])
# where are the kids riding? 
You will see how to enable batch processing and using datasets with torch DataLoader for training later in the Chapter 5 post-training.
Coding Example: Searching for Images from Text
Now that you have learnt about the fundamentals in the transformers ecosystem, let’s build your first multimodal application. You will build an application that can search for artworks based on a text query. To do so, you will use the SigLIP2 model. SigLIP2 is the state-of-the-art CLIP-like model released by Google. Like CLIP, it consists of an image and text encoder that we call a tower.
Doing search with CLIP-like models is straightforward. In our case, we will search for images from text. It consists of two steps:
  1. Index all images using the image tower of SigLIP, get the embeddings, and store them side by side with images, as shown in Figure 1-34.
  2. During the inference, you will first get the embedding of the text query through (as shown in Figure 1-35), and then we would find the closest image embeddings and retrieve those.
You will use FAISS to store and search the embeddings. If you are on a GPU, make sure to check the CUDA version with the command nvidia-smi, and install the appropriate faiss-gpu version. For CUDA 12.x, it is faiss-gpu-cu12.
You also need the sentencepiece library, so let’s make sure you have everything you need:
pip install datasets faiss-gpu-cu12 transformers sentencepiece
You will use siglip-base-patch16-224. This is the SigLIP with patch size 16 and resolution 224, 224:
import torch 
from transformers import AutoProcessor, AutoModel, AutoTokenizer 

device = torch.device('cuda' if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu")
model_id = "google/siglip-base-patch16-224"
model = AutoModel.from_pretrained(model_id, device_map="auto").eval()
processor = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained(model_id)
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_id)
This is a toy example, so you will be using a small portion of the dataset. You will stream 100 examples from the dataset. To do so, you need to authenticate yourself first. You can use login of the huggingface_hub library, or in the command-line interface, use huggingface-cli login:
from huggingface_hub import login
login()

import os

Now let’s get to indexing the dataset. The next code example shows helper functions to index the dataset. add_vector adds a vector to FAISS, and embed_siglip processes the image and gets image embeddings from SigLIP model, so we will first call embed_siglip and store the embedding output with add_vector:
import numpy as np
import faiss
def add_vector(embedding, index):
    vector = embedding.detach().cpu().numpy()
    vector = np.float32(vector)
    faiss.normalize_L2(vector)
    index.add(vector)
    
def embed_siglip(image):
    with torch.no_grad():
        inputs = processor(images=image, return_tensors="pt").to(device)
        image_features = model.get_image_features(**inputs)
        return image_features.pooler_output
Now, let’s initialize the FAISS index, and start indexing the dataset. SigLIP model outputs 1152-dimensional vectors, and you need to pass it to IndexFlatL2 to create your index:
from torchvision import transforms

index = faiss.IndexFlatL2(768)
for elem in data["rows"]:
url = elem["row"]["image"]["src"]
image = Image.open(requests.get(url, stream=True).raw)
clip_features = embed_siglip(image)
add_vector(clip_features, index)
You can save this index so you can do the search with it later on. To save, you can call write_index, and to load, you can use read_index:
faiss.write_index(index,"./siglip_70k.index")
index = faiss.read_index("./siglip_70k.index")
You can now search with the text query. Let’s get the artworks with a woman in them. To do this, you need to process the text input and get the text embeddings, then you need to pass the text through the tokenizer, which can be accessed with processor.tokenizer:
prompt="a woman"
text_token = processor.tokenizer([prompt], return_tensors="pt").to(device)
text_features = model.get_text_features(**text_token)
text_features = text_features.detach().cpu().numpy()
text_features = np.float32(text_features)
faiss.normalize_L2(text_features)
You will get the two artworks that match the best. index.search returns the distance between your query and the closest images, along with the indices of them, which can be mapped to the dataset to get the actual images:
distances, indices = index.search(text_features, 2)
print(indices)
# array([[50, 18]])
Let’s open the retrieved images:
for elem in indices[0]:
  url = data["rows"][elem]["row"]["image"]["src"]
  image = Image.open(requests.get(url, stream=True).raw)

  # downscale
