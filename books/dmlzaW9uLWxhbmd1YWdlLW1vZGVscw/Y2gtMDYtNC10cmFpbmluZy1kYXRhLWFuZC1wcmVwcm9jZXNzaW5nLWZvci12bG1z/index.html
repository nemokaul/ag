Chapter 4. Training Data and Preprocessing for VLMs

In Chapter 3, you trained your first vision language model (VLM). You watched loss curves go down, debugged loops caused by padding tokens, and learned that batch size 1 is a good start but batch size 4 is where things get real. You got excited when the model finally started producing decent results. You also discovered something slightly terrifying: training even a baby VLM on 0.5% of FineVision (120,000 samples) required careful token packing, placeholder tokens for images, and frequent vibe checking to catch bugs early.

Now imagine scaling that up. Not 120,000 samples: try 3.2 million. Then 15.3 million. Not 0.5% of one dataset but mixtures of dozens of datasets, each with different visual domains, different annotation quality, different proportions, and different purposes. Welcome to the data challenges of real VLM training.

VLMs are not really trained from scratch, as you saw in Chapter 3 when we loaded pretrained SigLIP and SmolLM. Modern VLMs build on top of pretrained vision encoders and language backbones before any multimodal training begins. What follows is babysitting (yes, literally, day and night, my friend!) the model through multiple training stages, each demanding different data qualities and volumes.

Let’s look at the Jina-VLM training data details in Figure 4-1.

The numbers tell a story that is hard to ignore, especially if you (like me) are a dinosaur who trained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) some years ago. Whereas fine-tuning a CNN classifier might require thousands to tens of thousands of labeled images, VLM training operates at an entirely different magnitude. Jina-VLM’s pretraining stage alone consumes 3.2 million samples spanning 10 billion tokens, followed by post-training on 15.3 million samples and 37 billion tokens.

Each stage serves a distinct purpose and requires data tailored to that purpose:

  • Pretraining focuses on teaching the model’s components to communicate: natural scene captions, document descriptions, infographic explanations, and diagram annotations establish what the pixels mean in words.

  • Post-training shifts toward building specialized capabilities: scientific diagrams with reasoning chains, multipage documents with Q&A, mathematical problems with solutions, and OCR tasks across diverse layouts.

Data quality evolves alongside the training objectives. In this chapter, you will explore all the challenges that these multimodal datasets raise: how to source content at internet scale, filter and diversify millions of samples efficiently, annotate them without breaking the bank, mix them intelligently, and package them ready to be used in multinode environments. You will walk through real examples from datasets like FineVideo, FineVision, DocMatix, and the SmolVLA community-based data mixture to show you what works at scale.

Vision-Language-Action and Video Models

As you learn how to put together datasets at the scale to train VLMs, you will be learning how to do that for two specialized categories that are close to VLMs. Take this sidebar box as a quick cocktail intro to two VLM cousins that you will get to know better in Chapters 9 and 11. Reader, meet VLA and video model.

Vision-language-action (VLA) models combine visual perception, language understanding, and robotic action prediction. These models learn from demonstrations to map visual observations and language instructions directly to control actions, enabling robots to perform tasks in the physical world.

Video models extend vision-language capabilities to temporal sequences, processing multiple frames to understand motion, events, and dynamics over time rather than analyzing single static images.

Learning Objective

By the end of this chapter, you will know how to build, filter, and mix datasets that can actually train a VLM, not just in theory but in the messy, practical reality where compute costs money and bad data breaks models.

Looking at the Data

Your data requirements aren’t just different for each model type, as Table 4-1 illustrates, but they stack on top of each other. To train a VLM, you need image-text pairs. To train a video model, you need everything the VLM needs plus video-text pairs. To train a VLA, you need all of these things plus action data.

This stacking matters because it determines both your data strategy and your costs. A VLA model doesn’t start from scratch with robot demonstrations: it inherits visual understanding from VLM foundations, then learns only how to act given what it sees.

Image-Text Datasets

Image-text datasets are the foundation of VLM training. You used them in Chapter 3 when you loaded FineVision and trained your first model. But not all image-text data is created equal: the structure, scale, and collection approach vary depending on what training stage you are targeting.

In your walk through the market of image-text datasets, you mainly find two flavors: image-caption pairs (one image, one description like “A dog running through a park”) and conversational data (images paired with multiturn question-answer exchanges). The simple pairs teach basic vision-language correspondence: what objects and scenes look like in both pixels and words while conversations teach reasoning, instruction-following, and dialogue about visual content.

Static images are easier to collect than video or robotics data. The web contains billions of them with free annotations: alt-text, captions, HTML context, filenames, etc. You can scrape millions of image-URL pairs in a weekend, plus storage is manageable…This is why VLM training starts here.

This magnificent modality of information does not come for free, as always there is a trade-off: video datasets unlock temporal understanding impossible with static images but require serious infrastructure investment. There are four aspects to take into account:

Storage

A 10-second video at 30 frames per second (fps) contains 300 frames. Even compressed, video files are 10–100x larger than images. Where LAION-5B’s metadata might fit in hundreds of gigabytes, an equivalent video dataset requires petabytes.

Availability

You can’t easily scrape videos: most content lives behind platforms (YouTube) or paywalls (stock footage).

Annotation cost

Describing a static image takes seconds, and most big models can do a good job at it. Describing a 5-minute video with temporal grounding can take 10–30 minutes of human time, or you can risk state-of-the-art big models taking a stab at it with average results.

Input/output (I/O) bottlenecks during training

Many teams store compressed videos and extract frames on the fly, or precompute embeddings. If we zoom in on the training process, you need to feed your GPUs fast enough, or otherwise they will be expensively idle! How do you do that? You need workers to prepare video data within each training node.

We are not done yet. There’s one more modality that goes even further. If you liked Terminator, you will like this one: the action modality.

Vision-Language-Action Datasets

VLA datasets go beyond text and video: they capture the motor commands that drive actions. While we explore VLA architectures in Chapter 11, understanding VLA datasets shows you the full spectrum of multimodal data challenges. If image-text taught models to see and video-text taught them to understand time, VLA data teaches them to translate understanding into physical action.

VLA datasets are organized into episodes: complete task demonstrations from start to finish. An episode isn’t just video: it’s a synchronized recording of everything needed to reproduce robot behavior. Each episode contains visual observations (camera feeds, often multiple viewpoints), action sequences (control commands sent to the robot: joint angles, end-effector positions), and language instructions (“Pick up the red ball and place it in the bucket”). Figure 4-3 (which uses an example from Hugging Face) shows what this looks like in a training episode: dual camera feeds synchronized with action trajectories for each joint, grounded in natural language.

You can scrape billions of images and download millions of videos, but you cannot scrape robot demonstrations. Each VLA sample requires physical robots or high-fidelity simulations where someone actually performs the task and records it. In other words: human teleoperation or expert demonstrations. The synchronization is critical: camera feeds, joint encoders, and instruction timing must be perfectly aligned. In the spectrum of datasets, this is the opposite of web scrapping…This is really gourmet data! Let’s look at the menu in Table 4-5.

You can see the difference in data volume across datasets: pretraining image datasets can have billions of data samples, video datasets aim for hundreds of millions, but VLA datasets celebrate tens of thousands. RT-1’s 130K demonstrations represent 17 months of continuous robot operation, a massive investment for what would be tiny in image terms.

Luckily, the team that trained the VLA model SmolVLA proved that you don’t need millions of robot demonstrations if you build on strong VLM foundations. SmolVLA achieved competitive performance with just 11K episodes, thanks to its base VLM’s visual understanding. The robot data only taught “how to act given what you see,” not “what things are,” which was inherited from the VLM used as baseline.

Warning

There’s no universal standard to represent actions in VLA datasets. Some datasets discretize actions into tokens by binning joint angles, treating motor commands like words. Others provide continuous action trajectories: precise floating-point values for joint positions. The choice impacts how models learn, how well they generalize, and whether you can combine datasets from different sources.

This is where LeRobot comes in. LeRobot is an open source framework from Hugging Face that’s becoming the industry standard for VLA dataset formats and model training. It provides a unified interface for loading robot datasets, handling different action representations, and converting between formats. Instead of every lab creating its own incompatible data formats, LeRobot lets you work with datasets through a consistent API.

Now you understand the three data modalities: image-text forms the foundation, video-text adds temporal understanding, and VLA connects perception to physical control. These build on each other: video models leverage image understanding, and VLA models benefit from both. But understanding what these datasets are is only the beginning. The real challenge is building them: sourcing raw data at scale, filtering millions of samples efficiently, ensuring diversity, generating annotations, and packaging everything for consumption. “Building a Dataset” tackles all of those tasks, starting with where all this data actually comes from.

Building a Dataset

Now it’s your turn to build a dataset from scratch. Spoiler alert: you are not going to manually annotate three million samples. You are also not going to download the entire internet and hope for the best. What you need is a pipeline: a systematic funnel that takes raw data at one end and produces a clean, training-ready dataset at the other. You will be refining crude oil into gasoline…lots of filtering, processing, and quality checks along the way. While there is no single right way to do this, Figure 4-4 shows you a template to follow.

Let’s go through each step:

1. Data sourcing

Gather raw input, including videos, images, and, optionally, metadata. These can come from already existing datasets that you plan to augment, proprietary archives, or web crawls.

2. Data filtering

Remove problematic content (unlicensed material, duplicates, or corrupted files) or nonrelevant content for your training goal before more extensive processing.

3. Diversity validation

Check whether the dataset covers the intended range of scenarios and domains that you expect.

4. Data annotation

Augment the original samples with extra metadata, which might be Q&A about the media file if you are in later training stages or might be descriptions in early stages.

5. Quality validation

Conduct reviews that ensure annotations are correct and the data meets quality standards.

6. Preparing the dataset for consumption

Simplify the steps to plug your dataset into a training pipeline. This could mean, for example, extracting frames from video, precomputing embeddings, or packaging into shards.

In the following subsections, you will learn how to tackle the dataset building steps at the scale required by large language models (LLMs).

Data Sourcing at Scale

As a good detective facing an investigation in an immense city, any extra piece of information around the media files that you gathered for the dataset is invaluable: it will enable you to filter, diversify, and potentially simplify the annotation work.

How can you get media files (images, videos) for your training with some linked metadata?

The three most common ways are:

  • Build a custom dataset with your own domain-specific data. While it may be small, high-quality data will significantly improve your final training mixture.

  • Expand existing datasets by gathering additional samples, refining the selection, or augmenting the data. For example, DoclingMatix (a large-scale dataset designed for document Q&A and document structure understanding) builds on DocMatix by incorporating document-structure details. This helps models understand where images, formulas, or tables are positioned within the page inside the document.

  • Crawl the internet content by downloading images and videos with their associated descriptions and metadata. That might come from internet backups or platforms such as YouTube.

    Let’s look at how FineVideo did it in Figure 4-5: it started with YT-Commons: a list of YouTube video IDs with Creative Commons licenses and their closed captions. FineVideo used this text-focused public dataset to select video candidates, then downloaded and annotated them. Keep this figure in mind as we will reference it later in this chapter.

    Now, before you rush off to scrape the entire internet, there’s an important question: what about licensing and safety?

    Navigating the legal and ethical minefield

    Responsible data collection requires balancing automated filtering with human oversight to respect legal and ethical boundaries. Large-scale scraping must comply with licenses and ethical norms. That’s why many web-sourced datasets are restricted to noncommercial or research use only.

    Responsible data collection requires balancing automated filtering with human oversight to respect legal and ethical boundaries. Large-scale scraping must comply with licenses and ethical norms. That’s why many web-sourced datasets are restricted to noncommercial or research use only.

    FineVideo sourced videos from YouTube with a Creative Commons license. LAION-5B took a different approach, releasing only image URLs and embeddings rather than the images themselves to navigate copyright issues, while filtering out potential private or sensitive data.

    Ethical curation means more than just avoiding copyright violations. You need to exclude problematic content like extreme violence, hate speech, or personally identifiable information. These aren’t just nice-to-haves: they prevent your model from learning harmful patterns and protect you from legal liability. We will cover the practical filtering techniques for this in “Data Filtering at Scale”.

    Where pretraining and post-training data diverge

    For pretraining datasets, sourcing plus basic filtering is usually where the work stops. Teams download Common Crawl dumps, extract image URLs and surrounding text, apply safety filters, maybe check image-text alignment is coherent in a fast way (for example, using CLIP introduced in Chapter 1), and call it done.

    The LAION team’s entire pipeline was essentially this: scrape URLs, compute CLIP embeddings, filter obviously bad pairs, and release. No complex annotation, no human review of individual samples, no taxonomy development.

    Post-training is where every subsequent section of this chapter becomes critical. The filtering gets more sophisticated (“Data Filtering at Scale”), you need diversity validation (“Sample Diversity at Scale”), synthetic annotation pipelines (“Data Annotation and Quality Validation at Scale”), and quality control (also “Data Annotation and Quality Validation at Scale”). This is why pretraining datasets measure in billions while post-training datasets celebrate millions: the effort to get each new sample differs by orders of magnitude.

    With your raw data sourced and the legal/ethical boxes checked, it’s time to separate the wheat from the chaff. Let’s talk filtering.

    Data Filtering at Scale

    So if you have 1.8 million videos (from Figure 4-5) and associated metadata, how do you filter videos of little interest for our training goals or not ethically compatible with FineVideo? If it takes one minute per video to run all the filtering jobs to decide whether to keep a video, it would take you more than 3.5 years to go through all the video candidates on a single machine.

    What other options do you have?

    • Use your preferred cloud provider and get a few dozen servers to spread the filtering task among them at a considerable cost.

    • Use a funnel-like filtering approach. Start with lightweight filters that can quickly process all video candidates, then apply more resource-intensive analysis only to the videos that pass the initial screening. This progressive filtering minimizes computational costs by reducing the number of videos that require expensive processing at each stage.

      Most likely, you will use both approaches: once you figure out a funnel that can filter content in the way you want, you will scale it across servers. This parallelization can be as simple as putting in queue (num videos) / ( num servers) jobs to filter content on each server that you have. As a result, you will gain speed, and most probably you will bring down the cost.

      Here’s what this looks like in practice. Say you have 1.8 million videos and 100 cloud instances.

      Simple parallelization:

      # Divide work across 100 workers
videos_per_worker = 1800000/100  # 18000 videos each

      # Each worker processes its chunk independently
for worker_id in range(100):
start_idx = worker_id * videos_per_worker
end_idx = start_idx + videos_per_worker
process_videos(videos[start_idx:end_idx], worker_id)

      Funnel approach with parallelization:

      # Stage 1: Cheap filter on metadata (single machine, minutes)
candidates = filter_by_metadata(all_videos)  # 1.8M reduced to 800K

      # Stage 2: Medium-cost filter (10 machines, hours)
decent_quality = filter_by_clip_score(candidates)  # 800K reduced to 400K

      # Stage 3: Expensive filter (100 machines, hours)
final_dataset = filter_by_visual_analysis(decent_quality)  # 400K reduced to 100K

      The funnel approach saved you from running expensive visual analysis on 1.4 million videos that would have been filtered anyway. Each stage reduces the data volume, so later stages run faster even with the same compute budget. This is how FineVideo processed 1.8 million candidates down to 44K without going bankrupt.

      Finding good filtering proxies

      Ideally, you would manually inspect every sample to decide if it contributes positively to your dataset. Since this isn’t feasible, we use proxies: measurable characteristics that correlate with quality.

      For example, FineVideo used audio and visual dynamism filtering to select videos where something happened. An easy proxy for that is videos where there is some degree of visual change and a good speaking cadence. Despite the fancy name, Table 4-6 shows what this meant.

      Table 4-6. FineVideo filter implementations
      FilterImplementationResources

      Audio dynamism

      • We took the density of words in the video as a proxy of audio dynamism. The definition of word density is:

        • Word density = Number of words in closed captions / Total video length in seconds

      • By sampling and visually evaluating the quality of the content at different word-density thresholds, we decided to remove all videos with a word density below 0.5 words/second.

      Audio dynamism filtering only requires duration and word count per video. All this is data already available in YT-Commons. This allowed the initial filtering to run on a single laptop.

      Visual dynamism

      • We repurposed FFMPEG’s freezedetect filter to assess the video’s dynamism. While this filter is designed to identify frozen sections of a video (multiple identical frames placed one after the other), we could also identify chunks with low motion by setting the noise parameter to a very high value.

      • Rather than running freezedetect across the full video, we analyzed the video by temporal segments, and we voted if the video was static based on the number of segments categorized as static. Through manual evaluation, we set a threshold to discard the video if 40% of the segments analyzed have low movement.

      For visual dynamism filtering, we spread the task across multiple machines in the cloud. The code was packaged in Docker containers that could be launched using batch processing services from cloud providers.

      Check for corrupt samples

      Checking for corrupt samples is essential. When you use LLMs to generate annotations (as you will see in more detail in “Data Annotation and Quality Validation at Scale”), corrupt files create a cascade of problems. During annotation, the LLM will hallucinate responses for files it can’t read, introducing bad samples into your dataset. During training, if your model can open the file, it learns from wrong annotations; if it can’t open the file, your training run crashes.

      You can prevent data corruption issues by creating a validation filter that checks each sample in your dataset.

      For video datasets:

      • Try to open each video file.

      • Test if you can seek to different timestamps.

      • Verify that video frames can be properly read.

      For VLA datasets:

      • Ensure the video sources, robot arm joint angles, and gripper state are all recorded at the same time.

      • Check that these data streams stay synchronized throughout the recording.

      • Verify that observation states match the corresponding timestamp.

      The filter should automatically remove any samples that fail these validation checks, leaving you with a clean, reliable dataset for training.

      Ideally, if you are the only consumer of the dataset, ensure that you use the same libraries you used during annotation and training to manipulate the samples during this filtering.

      Sample Diversity at Scale

      Whether you are building a dataset to train a model on identifying issues on Swiss-made watches given an image, or you are parsing Taco Bell coupons, you will want to ensure diversity.

      A structured way to ensure diversity is to annotate each sample candidate of your dataset with a controlled list of categories (also called a taxonomy). Yes! Knowledge of information science can be useful for ensuring diversity.

      But what if you just downloaded thousands or millions of data samples? How can you figure out a taxonomy to annotate all the samples that you downloaded? You don’t need to explicitly categorize all of them. Focus instead on creating a taxonomy that covers the cases that you are interested in and the cases that you definitely want to discard, and then put the rest in a common bucket called “other.”

      Let’s go back to our FineVideo example to craft the taxonomy based on the requirements in Table 4-7.

      Table 4-7. Key questions to craft your taxonomy
      RequirementAnswer

      Which content do we need?

      Storytelling can happen in many ways: from news to vlogs, sports, music, etc. We know that we want to exclude drug- and violence-related content.

      Who will consume our dataset?

      Generalists and specialists: people who want their model to learn about general story understanding and people interested in niche use cases—for example, understanding meeting presentations or sports news.

      As we are supporting generalists and specialists, a hierarchical taxonomy will be the solution.

      In “Taxonomy development”, you will learn how to develop your own taxonomy for categorizing multimodal datasets.

      Taxonomy development

      To develop our taxonomy, we followed three key phases:

      1. Initial synthetic taxonomy

      We provided GPT-4o with our content scope (YouTube videos) and specified a two-level hierarchy of terms to enable flexible dataset slicing: once the dataset is published, users can filter at the broad category level or drill down to specific subcategories based on their model’s needs.

      2. Granularity adjustments

      We made the taxonomy more granular in areas where we expected high content diversity, knowing we could later downsample oversaturated subcategories. For instance, under the broad “sports” category, we created distinct subcategories for “sports commentary,” “sports talk shows,” and “sports analysis shows” since these represent different content styles and purposes, allowing us to balance representation across sports content types.

      3. Expert refinement

      We collaborated with a librarian to merge overly similar categories while splitting others that proved important to distinguish. For example, in news, we split out “political commentary” as a distinct category since opinion-based political content differs significantly from factual news reporting.

      The final taxonomy includes 126 detailed categories organized across two hierarchical levels, enabling users to customize the dataset for their specific needs (shown in Figure 4-6).

      We tested this initial taxonomy through content annotation, then iteratively improved it based on the annotation results with ongoing input from the information scientist.

      Content categorization

      To categorize the raw videos using the taxonomy that we developed, we relied on an affordable model running in a cloud instance. That was Llama 3.1 70B served through vLLM. You will learn all the tips and tricks about inference at scale in Chapter 7.

      Deployment of the LLM aside, the prompt required multiple iterations to ensure the answer is strictly a category in our taxonomy. During our prompt evaluation, we learned that by removing the existing YouTube tags and categories from the prompt, the quality of our results increased drastically: YouTube metadata was biasing the text generated by Llama 3.1 toward one of the categories provided by YouTube:

      prompt_template = """
Given those categories: {leaves}
Classify a youtube video given its closed captioning and some metadata details. 
RETURN ONLY the selected category and nothing else!
Title: {title}
Description: {description}
Channel: {channel}
Closed Caption: {closed_caption}
"""
      Tip

      Would you do that if you were working on a FineVideo XXL size? Running a 70B model on everything could be prohibitive. This is where you could use the 70B model to label a subset of the data, then train a tiny, specialized classifier on those labels, with the goal of using it to classify the rest of the data.

      After Llama categorized our videos based on the metadata, we decided which of those 126 categories we definitely don’t want in the dataset. Firearms and weapons? Gone. Substance use and drugs? Nope. Violence-related content? See ya. These videos got filtered out immediately after categorization.

      With the dangerous stuff out, now comes the fun part: deciding what proportions to keep. FineVideo aimed for roughly uniform distribution across categories since it’s meant to serve everyone. But your strategy depends on your goals. Training a medical model? Oversample rare diseases. Building a cooking VLM? Maybe ease up on the 7,000 sourdough starter videos (or don’t—no judgment). The point is, all that categorization work pays off here; without it, you are just throwing darts in the dark and hoping your model doesn’t become obsessed with one weird niche of the internet.

      Data Annotation and Quality Validation at Scale

      After all the filtering and diversity work, you’re left with a curated subset of high-quality samples. Now comes the most expensive part: creating the actual training annotations. This is where you convert your raw videos, images, and file metadata into structured question-answer pairs, captions, or whatever format your model needs to learn from.

      Let’s put this in perspective: if you started with millions of samples and your filtering pipeline brought you down to, say, 100K videos, annotating each one could cost anywhere from $0.05 to $5+, depending on the complexity and quality requirements. That’s $5K to $500K just for annotation costs, not counting any additional human validation.

      This is why the filtering stages we discussed earlier are so critical: every sample that makes it to annotation had better be worth the cost.

      Choosing your annotation approach

      We have the samples and the budget…Where do we start? You have several options, each with different cost-quality trade-offs:

      Human annotation

      This is the gold standard for quality but also the most expensive. For complex video understanding tasks, 10–30 minutes per sample might be required. Do the math: this approach only works for very small, high-value datasets or as ground truth for evaluating automated approaches.

      Model-based annotation

      This is where most teams end up. You use existing large multimodal models to generate annotations. It’s much cheaper than human annotation per sample, depending on the model and prompt complexity.

      Hybrid approach

      This is the compromise nearly everybody ends up with. Use models for the bulk annotation and humans for quality control, edge cases, or final refinement. It gives you reasonable quality at manageable costs but requires careful orchestration of both automated and human workflows.

      Building your own synthesis pipeline

      Unless you rely on very niche use cases where an existing model would not know where to start annotating, you will most likely rely to some degree on a model to annotate.

      Real-world hybrid approaches

      Let’s look at some real-world examples of hybrid approaches:

      FineVideo approach

      A systematic approach to annotation quality control involves three steps. First, they test the annotation model’s (Gemini 1.5) capabilities to understand its limitations, identifying issues like quality degradation at specific video durations. After generating all annotations, they automatically filter out clearly problematic ones (like the model placing annotations at minute 15 when a video is only 5 minutes long). Finally, they manually review 1% of the annotations to identify additional error patterns and refine filtering criteria.

      DocMatix hallucination filtering

      In the document processing space, DocMatix tackled hallucinations by implementing consistency checks. They would parse the same document multiple times and flag cases where the model generated contradictory information about the same visual elements. They also used rule-based filters to catch common hallucination patterns, such as invented text that doesn’t appear in the source image (see Figure 4-7).

      Video-STAR approach

      They implemented a multistage refinement (“self-training”) process where video metadata is used to generate data. Questions are generated using a VLM that is given both video metadata and the video content itself. Then, the same VLM is prompted with the questions. The samples are filtered to those containing the ground-truth metadata, and then the VLM is trained on this dataset. Then, the self-training cycle continues, where the now improved VLM is used to generate answers and so forth.

      The pattern across all those datasets

      Start with automated annotation, implement specific filtering for your domain’s common failure modes, and use humans strategically where automated quality control isn’t sufficient. The exact balance depends on your budget and quality requirements.

      Preparing the Dataset for Consumption

      After sourcing, filtering, diversifying, and annotating your data, the final step is packaging it for end users. Packaging the dataset properly is very important: if you package it properly, more teams will adopt it, and your distributed training setup will be way more efficient (you don’t want to be that guy with GPUs on vacation waiting for the training data to be ready).

      For multinode training environments where you are splitting work across dozens or hundreds of machines, there is one format that you absolutely need to know about: WebDataset. It’s become the industry standard for distributed training. And the best part? It works perfectly with Hugging Face Datasets.

      WebDataset packages datasets as collections of tar archives where files belonging to the same training sample share identical basenames. Small files, like images, are grouped into manageable tar archives, making downloads much faster than handling thousands of individual files.

      A dataset could be a collection of tar files called dataset-000000.tar to dataset054134.tar, for example. If we open a tar file for a dataset with image-text pairs, we might find something like this:

      • dataset-000000.tar:

      • ├── sample001.jpg # Image data

      • ├── sample001.txt # Text annotation

      • ├── sample001.json # Metadata

      • ├── sample002.jpg # Next sample’s image

      • ├── sample002.txt # Next sample’s text

      • └── sample002.json # Next sample’s metadata

      The beauty of WebDataset for multinode training is simple: each node grabs its own shards and goes about its business. Node 1 processes dataset-000000.tar through dataset-000010.tar, while node 2 handles dataset-000011.tar through dataset-000020.tar. No coordination, no bottlenecks, no waiting. Each node downloads what it needs.

      Raw files

      Once you’ve packaged your dataset and made it accessible, you might think you’re done. Upload to Hugging Face, share the repo link, and call it a day, right?

      You still have some hard decisions ahead of you: how do you actually use this data? If you are training a general-purpose VLM, do you throw everything in equally? If you are building a specialized model, how much general data do you keep? Get this wrong, and your model either forgets how to do basic tasks or never learns your specialized skill. Get it right, and you have just unlocked performance gains comparable to a change in your architecture. Welcome to the art of dataset mixing.

      Dataset Mixtures: The Hidden Hyperparameter

      Getting your data combination right can make or break your model’s performance. This section will show you how to build, evaluate, and adapt the right mix of datasets that align with your specific model goals.

      You will discover that, in modern VLM training, how you combine different datasets has an impact comparable to architectural changes (which is why this section is titled this way). Unlike traditional computer vision, where you might train on a single dataset like ImageNet, you will need to carefully balance diverse data sources for VLMs.

      This becomes even more critical when you realize that for some tasks, like robotics, you don’t have enough task-specific data or you realize that you want a model expert in your field but still with the capacity to communicate with people and answer clearly. You will learn the principles that will help you create the right data combinations that actually work for your use case.

      Mixture Ingredients and Proportions

      Effective dataset mixtures start with two decisions: what ingredients to include and in what proportions.

      Tip

      An important caveat: all the mixture strategies discussed here assume you are starting from a model that has already completed pretraining on massive web-scale data. When teams talk about maintaining general capabilities, they refer to those learned during the pre- and initial post-training phases.

      This is why post-training mixtures can be relatively small (millions versus billions of samples) yet still effective: they refine and specialize an already capable foundation rather than learning visual-language alignment from scratch.

      The first step in creating effective dataset mixtures is to understand what ingredients you have available and determine the optimal proportions for combining them. Here’s the central tension: train exclusively on your specialized task data, and you risk catastrophic forgetting: your model might excel at your niche task but forget how to handle basic vision-language interactions. But keep too much general data in the mix, and your model never builds deep expertise in your domain.

      A key insight from recent research is that you need to maintain a portion of general-purpose data throughout your training process, even when specializing. The exact proportion depends on several factors: how much specialized data you have, how sensitive your base model is (smaller models are more prone to forgetting), and whether you actually care about preserving general capabilities (sometimes you don’t; if you’re building a pure medical imaging model, losing the ability to describe cat videos is acceptable).

      For VLMs, VLA, and video models, that general-purpose portion typically consists of high-quality image-text pairs and conversational data. For LLMs, techniques such as model merging and on-policy distillation have proven highly effective for preserving core capabilities, but they are still emerging for multimodal models.

      VLA models show this tension perfectly. Robotics datasets contain only hundreds of thousands of episodes: you literally can’t train on pure robot data at VLM scale. As you read in the first section of this chapter, SmolVLA has shown competitive performance by building on VLM foundations, using robot data only to teach “how to act” while inheriting visual understanding from the base model.

When we built SmolVLM, we had to be particularly strategic about the data combinations since we were building a family of models with a small number of parameters.       Task-Driven Mixture Design

      Let’s continue with the SmolVLM example. When creating SmolDocling—a version of SmolVLM specialized for document understanding—the team didn’t simply add more document data. They carefully redesigned the entire training mixture to emphasize OCR-relevant tasks while preserving enough general vision-language data. Their mixture included:

      • 40% document images (PDFs, forms, tables, handwriting)

      • 25% natural images with text (street signs, product labels, screenshots)

      • 20% general conversational vision-language data

      • 15% pure text data to maintain language modeling capabilities

      Why these specific proportions? The team found through ablation studies that 40% document data was the sweet spot (enough to build strong document understanding without overfitting to document-specific patterns). The breakthrough was the 25% “bridge” category of natural images with text: street signs, product labels, and screenshots helped the model generalize its OCR capabilities beyond formal documents to real-world text-recognition scenarios. The 20% general conversational data prevented catastrophic forgetting of basic vision-language interaction, while 15% pure text maintained language modeling capabilities.

      Ablations and Evaluation

      OK, you have heard how SmolVLA and SmolVLM used different data mixtures for their training, but how did they arrive at those mixtures? In this section, we will cover how they did it.

      Ablation methodology

      When Orr Zohar was working on Apollo, a project exploring video understanding in large multimodal models, his team discovered something they called scaling consistency: many design decisions (data mixtures, sampling strategies, model architectures) found on moderately sized models (2–4B parameters) correlate strongly with the performance of larger models. Once the model reaches a critical scale, the correlation between small and large models’ performance becomes high and approximately log-linear; smaller models below this threshold show poor correlation. This effect also holds across model families, and the benefit saturates once the proxy models are trained on roughly 500K training examples. The authors term this phenomenon scaling consistency; basically it means that design choices made on 2–4B parameter models trained on a moderate amount of data transfer reliably to 7B+ models (see Figure 4-8, which uses an example from Orr Zohar, et al., “Apollo: An Exploration of Video Understanding in Large Multimodal Models”).

      As shown in Figure 4-8, design decisions made with smaller models on smaller datasets transfer to larger models on larger datasets. (Top) R² correlation between 7B and other model sizes increases with model size, while 0.5B shows inconsistent patterns. (Bottom) Agreement between model pairs stabilizes around 500K dataset samples, with correlations plateauing at larger dataset sizes.

      Apollo provides a systematic approach to mixture evaluation that is worth sharing. The Apollo team trained models with different mixture proportions and evaluated the correlation between performance on smaller versus larger models:

      1. Define five to eight candidate mixtures with different proportions.

      2. Train each mixture on 4B parameter models for 10K steps.

      3. Evaluate on a diverse benchmark suite (not just the target task).

      4. Select top two to three mixtures and train longer (50K+ steps).

      5. Validate final mixture choice on larger model.

      This process cut their mixture-experimentation costs by ~10x compared to running all experiments on full-size models.

      Measuring mixture impact

      While the final impact of a mixture in model training is not fully known until the end of training, one can store intermediate checkpoints and evaluate them on a diverse set of benchmarks that cover different capabilities. For example, in a general VLM, you can explore if it is improving in visual Q&A tasks, document understanding, and multi-image Q&A cases.

      In those intermediate checkpoints, track which data sources contribute most to the performance of your model in the validation benchmarks, not just training loss. For VLMs (and LLMs), training loss isn’t predictive of downstream performance because changing the data distribution necessarily changes the loss landscape. What matters is whether your mixture improves performance on the tasks you actually care about.

      Tip

      The critical insight: when you are running ablation studies to test different approaches, you need to use the same proportions of data mixtures that you will use for your final training. You might have enough high-quality data to run a short ablation study, but not enough to fully train. If you test your approach on pure high-quality data during ablations, then switch to mixed-quality data for full training (because you don’t have enough high-quality data), your ablation results won’t predict your final model’s performance.

      Think of it like testing a recipe with premium ingredients, then cooking the actual meal with whatever’s in your pantry: the results will be completely different.

      Even the most successful teams don’t get their mixture right on the first try. They build systems for continuous improvement:

      Mixture logging

      Track which data sources contribute most to validation performance at different training stages. You might find that web data is crucial early in training but becomes less important later.

      Performance regression tracking

      Set up automated alerts when performance on any mixture component drops below a threshold. This catches catastrophic forgetting before it becomes severe.

      A/B mixture testing

      Once you have a baseline mixture, test small variations (i.e., 5% proportion changes) on smaller models and fewer steps before committing to expensive full retraining.

      You have now built a dataset from scratch, filtered millions of samples, ensured diversity, annotated at scale, packaged it for consumption, and learned how to mix it with other datasets. That’s the full pipeline. But here’s the thing: this isn’t a one-and-done process. The teams building the best VLMs treat dataset creation as an iterative loop. They train a model, identify its failure modes, go back and adjust their filtering criteria or mixture proportions, and retrain. FineVideo went through multiple rounds of taxonomy refinement. SmolVLM tested dozens of mixture variations. This is normal. Data work is never “done”: it’s a continuous process of improvement as you learn what your model needs.

      Summary

      You started this chapter knowing how to train a baby VLM on 0.5% of FineVision. Now you understand why that’s adorable but absolutely not how the pros do it.

      Modern VLM training doesn’t happen on curated toy datasets and the challenge isn’t finding data (the internet has plenty); it’s turning the noisy raw content into something your model can actually learn from.

      The solution? Build a funnel. Raw data flows in from web scrapes, existing datasets, or your archives. Lightweight filters clean the junk (corrupt files, static videos, that one person’s 47,000 sourdough starter clips). Taxonomy-based diversity validation ensures you are not accidentally training a model that only understands one weird niche of the internet. Model-based annotation converts raw samples into structured Q&A pairs because you definitely can’t afford to manually annotate three million samples. Quality validation catches the hallucinations and errors. Finally, you package everything (ideally as WebDataset shards on Hugging Face) so that multinode training can actually use it without your GPUs sitting idle waiting for data.

      The funnel is cheap to expensive by design. If filtering one video takes a minute and you have 1.8 million videos, that’s 3.5 years on a single machine. So you run metadata filters first (seconds per video), then medium-cost filters like CLIP scoring (still manageable), and only run expensive visual analysis on whatever survives. FineVideo used this approach to process 1.8 million candidates, down to 44K, without going bankrupt.

      Here is where things get spicy: building datasets isn’t enough. You need to mix them correctly. Train exclusively on your specialized task data, and your model forgets how to have basic conversations. Keep too much general data, and it never builds domain expertise. The art is maintaining enough general-purpose data to preserve capabilities while pushing hard on your target domain.

      One breakthrough to remember: scaling consistency research proved that mixture decisions on 2–4B models trained on ~500K samples transfer reliably to 7B+ models. This means you experiment on small, cheap models, find the winning mixture, then promote it to the big model. No more burning compute testing every idea at full scale.

      From now on, you will look at VLM capabilities differently. That model that excels at documents while still describing cat videos? That’s not magic architecture: it’s a careful mixture design. That robotics model is performing well with surprisingly little data? That’s smart reuse of VLM foundations. And those research papers casually mentioning “we trained on 15 million samples”? You now know the engineering pipeline that made that sentence possible.

      Next up: Chapter 5 covers post-training, in which you take a generally capable model and train it to solve your specific problem. But the data engineering principles from this chapter follow you everywhere, because even the cleverest training tricks can’t save you from garbage data.
