Chapter 10. Any-to-Any Models

Multimodal models are great because they ground language in the world we actually live in. Vision language models (VLMs) can see images and answer questions in natural language. Video models understand how actions unfold and how cause and effect play out over time. Audio models can hear speech and sound. All of them bring AI closer to human-like perception.

What if you want to build an assistant that can see, hear, and speak? Based on what we have seen so far, you would need a vision model, an audio model, a speech synthesis model, and careful orchestration among them.

The next frontier is any-to-any models: unified architectures that handle understanding and generation across text, images, audio, and video within a single system (Figure 10-1).

To be truly any-to-any, a model must handle at least two input modalities and two output modalities. If that sounds ambitious, it is…and the engineering choices involved are fascinating—as you will see in this chapter.

Particularly, you will learn about the three architectural trends for any-to-any models, along with their pros and cons. You will also run inference and train those models to gain experience and judge these different approaches.

Why three families? That is a fair question. The challenge of unifying modalities creates a fundamental puzzle: how do you represent continuous signals (images, audio, video) in a way that a transformer can both understand and generate?

As you noticed when we discussed vision and video aspects thus far, those modalities have different computational characteristics when we compare them to text:

  • Text is naturally discrete (words/tokens) and benefits from autoregressive prediction.

  • Images are high-dimensional with values that vary smoothly (pixel intensities, colors, spatial positions), making them better suited for iterative refinement approaches like diffusion rather than discrete token prediction.

  • Audio has a temporal structure and can work well as either discrete codes or continuous spectrograms.

  • Video combines spatial and temporal complexity with massive compute requirements.

These differences in text, image, audio, and video create competing pressures:

Efficiency versus fidelity

You want a single backbone that can reuse all the tricks that made large language models (LLMs) work (tokenization, autoregressive decoding, cross-entropy training), but images, audio, and video are high-bandwidth continuous signals that don’t naturally fit into a small, discrete vocabulary.

Simplicity versus modularity

A monolithic “one transformer does everything” design is conceptually clean, but in practice, it couples language, perception, and generation so tightly that improving one often hurts the others.

Shared representation versus modality-specific detail

A shared token space makes multimodal reasoning easy, but each modality has its own structure (spatial for images, temporal for audio/video) that benefits from tailored encoders/decoders and sometimes completely different generation algorithms (AR versus diffusion versus codecs).

Different trade-offs among these pressures yield three architectural families, briefly defined in Figure 10-2.

Learning Objective

Build practical any-to-any multimodal pipelines by understanding the three architectural families and knowing when to choose discrete tokenization, diffusion-based generation, or modular component stitching for your use case.

Introducing the Three Approaches

Before we dive in, let’s define these approaches so you have a sense of where the chapter is heading.

Unified Vocabulary Models

These models convert all modalities into discrete codes via vector quantization, then use a single transformer to predict the next token regardless of modality (hence the term discrete-token models). See Figure 10-2, left.

Vector quantization works like a compression dictionary: you take a continuous signal (like image patches) and map each piece to the nearest entry in a learned codebook. Here is a simple mental image to understand codebooks for now: imagine describing colors as “red,” “green,” or “blue” instead of exact RGB values like (127, 45, 203). We will cover codebooks in more detail later.

This approach is simple and enables fast generation with tight multimodal alignment, but quantization limits visual fidelity. We will see that nowadays this option is popular when you need low latency or when images serve as reasoning steps rather than final outputs.

Hybrid Models

These models use different strategies per modality—for example, autoregressive prediction for text but diffusion or flow-based generation for visuals within a shared transformer backbone. This produces the highest image quality among unified models, but inference is slower due to iterative diffusion, and training is more complex. This option is the current go-to when visual fidelity is the highest objective. See Figure 10-2, middle.

Modular Models

Understanding and generation are decoupled: a multimodal language model provides semantic conditioning to a separate, specialized generator (a diffusion model, text-to-speech engine, or other rendering module). See Figure 10-2, right. One important subtype of this family is query-based generation, in which the interface between the multimodal large language model (MLLM) and the generator is an explicit set of learnable query tokens (though the family is broader than that, as you will see):

  • This offers maximum modularity: you can swap generators without retraining the backbone, only retraining the interface between components.

  • This option is the go-to when you need flexibility to upgrade components independently or cannot afford lengthy training, preferring to stitch together multiple pretrained components instead.

  • While this option is a good bang for the buck (you can limit your training to retrain the interface between components and achieve good initial results), the limited interface between components can be a bottleneck to achieving fine-grained alignment between inputs and outputs.

Now that you have had an introduction to the three families, let’s get to know them in more detail. For this, there is no better way than getting your hands dirty. In the next sections, we will run inference on models from those families while learning more about those architectures. We will conclude the chapter by examining the details of training those models.

Unified Vocabulary Models

You will kick off with unified vocabulary models…If everything is unified and you want it to work, it means every modality needs to speak the same language, and for transformers, that language is tokens. The question is: how do you turn a photograph or a piano chord into something that looks like a word?

That is what discrete tokenizers do: translating images, video, and audio into sequences of discrete tokens that an autoregressive transformer can process just like text. A discrete tokenizer is just the same tokenizer we have seen so far until this chapter. As you can imagine, the choice of tokenizer directly affects reconstruction quality, semantic understanding, and computational efficiency.

If you zoom in on the unified vocabulary models, you can find two main architectures:

Monolithic

All modalities share a single expanded vocabulary made of discrete tokens; the LLM directly predicts discrete tokens for both text and nontext outputs.

Factorized heads

The LLM predicts special tokens (triggers such as "<image_start>" or "<audio_end>") that hand off to specialized auxiliary heads that produce image or audio tokens. These tokens are used to generate images and audio. These heads are lightweight output modules, separate from the main LLM’s prediction head, each dedicated to a specific modality; they take the LLM’s hidden state at the trigger token as input and produce the corresponding image or audio tokens from it.

It is also possible to find hybrid architectures in between those two, as you can see in Figure 10-3 (middle).

Monolithic Architecture

Let’s start with the monolithic approach. You will review how it works and explore DeepSeek’s Janus-Pro to contrast theory and reality. Let’s go!

How the monolithic approach works

Language models operate on fixed vocabularies. If you look at some open models, for example, Llama 3 uses 128K tokens, and Qwen3 uses 152K tokens. While this is a substantial amount of tokens, you can see that the foundational challenge is bridging the discrete world of language and the continuous world of images/audio. How do we represent images/audio with such a limited vocabulary? The monolithic approach relies on vector quantization (VQ) and vocabulary expansion.

Note

A codebook is a fixed-size table of reference vectors (like a palette of colors). During training, the model learns which reference vectors best represent the data. At inference time, each continuous vector gets snapped to its closest match in the table (the same way a paint-by-numbers kit replaces infinite color variation with a numbered set of paints). The index of that match becomes the discrete token. We will revisit codebooks in more detail when we discuss residual vector quantization (RVQ) later in this chapter (see “Connecting Continuous Latents to Language Models Through Diffusion”).

To take an image or audio sample and convert it into tokens:

  1. An encoder transforms image patches or audio chunks into continuous vectors.

  2. Each vector maps to the nearest entry in a learned codebook.

  3. The codebook index becomes the discrete token for that patch.

Basically, the codebook becomes the new vocabulary for that modality, and we then expand the existing LLM’s vocabulary with the codebook’s vocabulary.

For example, if we had used Qwen3 as our LLM and expanded it with some visual tokens, our new vocabulary would look like this:

  • Text vocabulary: 152,064 tokens

  • Image codebook: 8,192 entries

  • Unified vocabulary: 160,256 tokens

Those extra visual tokens now map to reserved blocks: <vis0>, <vis1>, ..., <vis8191>. During the training, the model learns from context whether to predict text or visual tokens next.

This simplicity comes with a bottleneck: to represent complex signals with high fidelity, we would need many tokens, and therefore the codebook would need to be enormous. This results in a bigger need for computational resources.

Ultimately, quantized visual encoders tend to underperform their continuous counterparts in both understanding and generating visuals. So how do real systems cope with this limitation? Let’s look at DeepSeek’s Janus-Pro, which found a pragmatic middle ground.

In practice: DeepSeek’s Janus-Pro (a transitional architecture)

Janus-Pro is an open-weight multimodal model from DeepSeek that handles both image understanding and image generation. We are placing it in “Monolithic Architecture” because it expands the LLM vocabulary with discrete visual tokens for generation. However, as you can see in Figure 10-4 (based on an example from Xiaokang Chen et al., “Janus-Pro: Unified Multimodal Understanding and Generation with Data and Model Scaling”), it actually splits its encoders by role (one for understanding, one for generation), which makes it a transitional design sitting between the monolithic and factorized-head approaches we will explore next.

The model decouples image encoders by role:

Input (perception)

High-capacity continuous image encoder processes images for understanding (e.g., SigLIP2).

Output (generation)

VQ-VAE tokenizer (a combination of vector quantization with an autoencoder) produces discrete codes that the LLM predicts. This means that the vocabulary is expanded with visual tokens but only used for generation and editing, not understanding.

This asymmetry relieves the one-encoder-fits-all tension seen in pure monolithic systems. The understanding encoder optimizes for semantic understanding without compression constraints, while the generation tokenizer focuses purely on reconstruction quality.

If we go and play with the transformers implementation, the model exposes an interface with an explicit generation_mode switch:

  • generation_mode="text" for image understanding and text output

  • generation_mode="image" for image generation

Since the generation mode is a switch, the model does not interleave text and images within a single stream; you must explicitly switch modes.

We prepared a unified script that lets you test pure-text examples, visual Q&A, and image generation. You can find it in the general Chapter 10 notebook. Pay attention to the generation_mode. Note that this model isn’t officially supported by transformers library but is custom code implementation to make it loadable into transformers API by authors.

Despite its elegance, the monolithic approach’s reliance on single-codebook tokenizers forces the main transformer to master both high-level semantics and low-level pixel details, often leading to compromises in generation quality. What if we could free the LLM from that burden entirely, letting it decide what to generate while handing off how to render it to a specialist? That is exactly what factorized heads do.

Factorized Heads

As you recall, we introduced Janus-Pro in the monolithic approach, but as a transitional architecture. In fact, it sits between a model that directly predicts visual tokens from its own vocabulary and factorized heads, which takes decoupling a step further by removing modality token prediction from the LLM entirely.

Instead of making the LLM predict pixels or waveforms, factorized heads augment the output pipeline with auxiliary models that handle modality-specific generation. This is the key architectural shift:

Monolithic models

LLM directly predicts discrete visual tokens from an expanded vocabulary (152K text + 8K visual = 160K total).

Factorized-heads model

LLM outputs special tokens (<image>, <audio>) that trigger auxiliary heads. These heads generate the actual modality outputs (image patches, audio chunks) using the LLM’s hidden states as semantic guidance.

Notice the pattern: the LLM never predicts pixel values or audio samples directly. It produces rich hidden states that encode semantic intent, and the specialized heads translate that intent into modality-specific codes.

What do we gain compared to the monolithic approach?

Separation of concerns

The LLM decides what to generate (semantic meaning); auxiliary heads handle how to render it (actual pixels or sound).

Breaking the codebook bottleneck

Instead of describing each image patch with a single codebook (like Janus-Pro’s 8,192 entries), rely on multiple small codebooks working in concert. You will learn more about this in “Multicodebook generation”.

Clean vocabulary

The LLM’s vocabulary stays purely text-based. No image or audio tokens bloat it, keeping the model efficient at language tasks.

Natural interleaving

Because the LLM controls the generation flow through trigger tokens, it can freely interleave text and generated content: write a sentence, generate an image, continue writing, and generate audio—all in one autoregressive stream. Recall that Janus-Pro required an explicit mode switch. Factorized heads remove that constraint.

Asymmetric pattern: Continuous in, discrete out

The dominant pattern in factorized architectures fully separates input and output handling:

Input side (understanding)

Images, video, and audio are processed by continuous encoders (like ViT or SigLIP for vision, streaming encoders for audio). These encoders produce dense embeddings that feed directly into the LLM via cross-attention, with block-wise scheduling to efficiently handle long contexts. Qwen2.5-Omni exemplifies this with block-wise encoders for streaming perception.

Output side (generation)

When the LLM needs to generate nontext content, it emits a special token that hands control to a specialized modality head. This head predicts discrete code stacks, which a decoder then renders into pixels or waveforms. Qwen2.5-Omni’s Thinker-Talker (Figure 10-6) design makes this split explicit: the Thinker (LLM) handles reasoning, while the Talker (head) generates speech.

This separation keeps the LLM good at language because its vocabulary stays purely text-based. Block-wise encoders let it handle long videos and audio efficiently. The generation heads can use detailed multicodebook representations without making the LLM’s vocabulary larger. And because understanding and generation are separate, you can improve one without affecting the other.

Multicodebook generation

We just said that factorized heads break the codebook bottleneck. But what does that bottleneck actually look like, and how exactly do multiple codebooks help? Let’s make this concrete.

Remember how Janus-Pro’s generation was limited? Each image patch could only be one of 8,192 discrete codes. This creates a problem: either you keep the codebook small (fast but blurry results) or make it huge (better quality but computationally expensive and harder to train).

Residual vector quantization (RVQ) breaks this trade-off by using multiple small codebooks in sequence. Let’s see an example:

Single codebook (Janus-Pro)

Pick 1 code from 8,192 options. These are limited options that end in limited generated details.

RVQ

Pick 8 codes, one from each of 8 codebooks with 1,024 options each. This makes a total of 1,024^8 combinations.

An easy analogy would be the following: imagine we have to describe a person, and instead of choosing from 8,192 preset descriptions, you get 8 separate attributes (height, eye color, hair color, etc.), each with 1,024 options. The combinations let you describe far more precisely.

You might be thinking…why can you use RVQ here and not in the monolithic models? Adding 8 separate codebooks to the monolithic models would explode their vocabulary size. In a factorized-heads approach, it is possible to use RVQ because the heads themselves use RVQ, not overloading the LLM’s vocabulary. In other words, the generation head handles all these codes independently.

How do heads generate RVQ codes?

Once the LLM hands off to a generation head, that head must predict the code stack. There are two strategies:

Sequential (coarse to fine)

The head predicts codes one codebook at a time: first the most important (coarse structure), then it is refined with each subsequent codebook. Each prediction conditions on the previous ones. That can be applied to audio generation (EnCodec), where each layer adds finer detail. It is slower but allows progressive refinement.

Parallel

The head predicts all codebook indices simultaneously, conditioned on the LLM. That fits with image generation, where spatial relationships across the image matter more than dependencies between codebook levels.

Hybrid Multiobjective Models

If discrete tokens are a lossy JPEG of reality, the hybrid multiobjective family asks: why compress at all? Keep text as tokens (what LLMs do best) but let images and audio live as continuous latents, refined through diffusion (what preserves the highest fidelity).

Since this is a common source of confusion, Table 10-1 compares the factorized-heads approach from autoregressive models with the hybrid multiobjective. Both can use diffusion for generation, but factorized heads delegate to a separate diffusion head (the LLM never sees continuous values), while hybrid multiobjective processes continuous latents directly within the main transformer.

Table 10-1. High-level summary that compares factorized-heads approach with hybrid multiobjective family

The LLM delegates discrete generation to specialists.

The transformer is bilingual, fluent in both discrete and continuous representations.

Modality representation

Inputs are typically continuous encoder embeddings; outputs are text tokens plus head-specific discrete codecs/tokens. The LLM vocabulary itself can remain text-only.

Text = discrete; images/audio = continuous latents

Who generates?

LLM triggers a special token, and the auxiliary head generates discrete codes.

Main transformer processes and denoises continuous latents directly.

Loss functions

Single objective: cross-entropy over unified vocabulary

Multiple objectives: cross-entropy (text) + diffusion MSE (images)

Generation steps

Autoregressive control in the LLM, followed by modality-specific decoding in the auxiliary head; no diffusion loop inside the main transformer.

Iterative: 20–50 denoising steps

Faster, but quantization limits quality

Highest quality, but slower

The table reveals architectural difference: where does continuous processing happen? In factorized heads, the LLM’s output and generation space stay discrete: it decides when to generate an image by emitting a special token, then hands off to a separate module to handle continuous generation (note that the input side still processes continuous embeddings from vision/audio encoders, as we saw in “Factorized Heads”). In hybrid multiobjective models, the main transformer itself processes continuous latent patches alongside discrete text tokens. During each of the 20–50 diffusion steps, those noisy image latents flow through the same attention layers as your text prompt, creating tight integration between modalities.

Now that you understand where hybrid models differ, let’s explore the three key pieces that make them work:

  • How variational autoencoders (VAEs) turn images, video, and audio into compact latent representations

  • How these latents connect to language models for generation

  • How diffusion-based generation works (you will run in your brain some pseudocode)

Let’s start with the foundation: continuous representations through VAEs.

Variational Autoencoders

At a high level, a VAE (Figure 10-7) learns what matters in a signal by compressing it and then reconstructing it.

Its training objective balances two competing goals: reconstruction and regularization. Formally, the VAE loss combines these two goals:

LVAE = Lreconstruction + β · LKL

Lreconstruction = ||x – x^||² measures how well the decoder reconstructs the original input x from the latent z, and LKL = KL(q(z|x) || p(z)) is the Kullback-Leibler divergence that penalizes the encoder’s latent distribution q(z|x) for deviating from a standard Gaussian prior p(z). The hyperparameter β controls the trade-off: higher β gives a smoother latent space (better for generation) at the cost of reconstruction sharpness.

Note

You may also encounter vector quantized VAE (VQ-VAE), which replaces the continuous latent with discrete codes from a learned codebook (the same codebook mechanism we discussed in “Monolithic Architecture”). VQ-VAEs drop the KL term entirely and instead use a commitment loss that encourages encoder outputs to stay close to codebook entries. This makes VQ-VAEs popular in discrete-token architectures (Janus-Pro uses one for its generation encoder), while standard VAEs with continuous latents are the foundation for diffusion-based hybrid models.

What does this formula mean? The reconstruction goal pushes the decoder to accurately recreate the original input from the compressed latent (an image should look like the original image when decoded, an audio clip should sound the same). The regularization goal pushes the latent space to be smooth and well structured so that nearby points represent similar content and smooth interpolation between points produces meaningful intermediates.

The compression achieved can be ~50x for image or 200x for audio in current models, as you will see in Table 10-2. Additionally, because the latent space is continuous and learned, it preserves fine details that matter.

With these compact, continuous representations in hand, the next question is: how do we actually generate new content from them? This is where diffusion enters the picture.

Connecting Continuous Latents to Language Models Through Diffusion

As you well know, standard LLMs are built to predict discrete tokens from a fixed vocabulary, and this does not match with continuous latent spaces where infinite values are possible.

This is the gap diffusion covers: instead of trying to predict continuous values directly, we learn to gradually transform random noise into structured data through iterative refinement. This two-phase process works as follows:

  1. During training (forward process), we take a real image latent and progressively add Gaussian noise over many timesteps until it becomes indistinguishable from pure noise.

  2. During generation (reverse process), we train a neural network to predict and remove the noise at each step, conditioned on the text prompt. Starting from pure random noise, we iteratively denoise to recover a clean latent.

Figure 10-8 illustrates this process (image from Calvin Luo’s website): the bottom row shows the forward noising chain that progressively corrupts data into noise, while the top row shows the learned reverse process that gradually denoises back to structured data.

The training objective is elegant and simple: just mean squared error between the actual noise added and the model’s prediction. The model learns to denoise at all noise levels simultaneously, from barely perceptible noise to pure static.

While we used images as an example for diffusion, this same principle works across all modalities: image latents, video latents, audio spectrograms, or even robotic action trajectories. Only the shape and structure of the latent space change.

The Transfusion Breakthrough: Unifying Discrete and Continuous

The team behind Transfusion pioneered the concept of training a single transformer to handle both discrete text tokens and continuous diffusion (image latent patches) simultaneously.

How do they do that? Transfusion combines two loss functions into a single training objective: cross-entropy for text token prediction and MSE for denoising image latents. At any given training step, the applicable loss depends on whether the current token position is text (cross-entropy) or image (MSE). The model never optimizes both simultaneously on the same token, but the unified objective allows gradients from both modalities to flow through shared parameters. All modalities attend to one another within the same attention layers, creating rich cross-modal understanding.

The innovation is in the attention mask structure, shown in Figure 10-9.

Text tokens follow standard causal attention; each token only sees what came before, enabling autoregressive generation. Image latent patches operate differently: all patches within an image block (marked by <BOI> and <EOI> tokens) can attend to one another bidirectionally, which is essential for effective denoising.

The boundary rule is simple: text written before an image can’t yet see that image’s patches, but text written after can attend to the complete image. This hybrid masking makes the model bilingual: it uses autoregressive decoding for text and bidirectional decoding for images in the same forward pass.

Putting It All Together: From Prompt to Generated Output

Let’s take a text-to-image example: when you prompt a hybrid model to generate an image, the process unfolds across 20–50 iterative steps. First, your text prompt gets encoded into embeddings that capture not just words but intent, style, and relationships. Then random Gaussian noise is sampled in the target latent shape (e.g., 64 × 64 × 4). This randomness is the seed of creativity, ensuring different outputs even with identical prompts.

The core of generation happens through iterative refinement. At each step, the model predicts the noise component in the current noisy latent, subtracts a portion of it, and moves toward a clean, structured latent. Crucially, these noisy latent patches flow through the transformer alongside text tokens at each iteration (this is the tight integration that we mentioned in “Hybrid Multiobjective Models”). Finally, the denoised latent is decoded by the VAE to produce actual pixels (or waveforms for audio, or frames for video).

A practical technique called classifier-free guidance (CFG) significantly improves the quality of outputs’ match to prompts. The formula is:

ε̂ = εuncond + s · (εcond – εuncond)

εcond is the noise prediction conditioned on your prompt, εuncond is the unconditional prediction (empty prompt), and s is the guidance scale.

Why does this work better than just using the conditional prediction alone? Think of (εcond – εuncond) as the “direction the prompt is pulling.” When s = 1, you get the standard conditional prediction. When s > 1 (typically 5–15), you amplify the prompt’s influence beyond what the model would naturally produce. You are saying “go further in the direction the prompt points.” This is analogous to increasing contrast in a photo: the prompt-relevant features become more pronounced. The trade-off is that very high guidance reduces output diversity and can introduce artifacts, as it pushes the model away from its natural distribution.

To enable CFG with a single model, training randomly drops the conditioning (replaces the text prompt with an empty string) for some fraction of training steps (typically 10–20%). This teaches the model to produce both εcond and εuncond, so at inference time, both predictions come from the same network. No separate “classifier” needed, hence classifier-free.

Late-Conditioning Models

What if you did not have to train a single giant model at all? The late-conditioning family decouples understanding entirely from generation. This architecture uses a multimodal language model to produce semantic representations (called queries) that tell a separate, specialized generator what to create. In other words, the MLLM handles the what, while purpose-built generators handle the how.

This is interesting, but what is the difference between this family and the factorized heads we saw in “Factorized Heads”? You could argue the distinction is just “what do you freeze?” and you would not be entirely wrong. Both use an LLM for semantics and a separate module for generation. The difference is one of degree and design philosophy.

In factorized heads, the generation head is architecturally coupled to the LLM: it reads hidden states directly, shares the same forward pass, and is typically trained jointly. You can freeze the LLM, but the head was designed to work with its specific representations.

In late conditioning, the MLLM and generator are designed as independent systems from the start. The MLLM can be any off-the-shelf VLM. The generator can be any off-the-shelf diffusion model or text-to-speech (TTS) engine. Only the lightweight connector between them needs training. This is not just a training choice; it is an architectural commitment to modularity that enables component swapping without retraining.

In practice, the boundary is blurry: a factorized-heads system where you freeze the LLM and only train the head starts to look a lot like late conditioning. The key question is whether the generator was designed to be independent and swappable, or tightly integrated with a specific LLM backbone.

This modularity is what makes late conditioning attractive. As you will see, if you have a limited budget, you can spend it training just a lightweight connector between existing powerful models: a strong MLLM on one side, a capable diffusion model or TTS engine on the other…and you have got an any-to-any system. New generator released? No problem: swap it in without touching the MLLM. Want to preserve your MLLM’s language capabilities? Keep it frozen. As always, there is a catch: the interface between components can become a bottleneck. Fine-grained alignment between inputs and outputs may suffer compared to tightly integrated approaches, such as hybrid multiobjective approaches, where text and image processing share the same attention layers. In other words, you trade maximum integration for maximum flexibility.

Figure 10-10 shows how the three roles—understand, connect, generate—map to concrete components in the pipeline. The MLLM processes this sequence in one pass, producing text outputs and query states 𝐐∈ℝS×D. The connector fθ transforms these query states into conditioning vectors 𝐜∈ℝC×D′. Finally, different generators (Image DiT, TTS Engine, Video DiT) can be swapped in without retraining the MLLM, each receiving the same conditioning vectors.

Let’s look under the hood (Figure 10-10). The query-based conditioning pipeline has four components working in sequence:

Input sequence

Your text prompt combined with learnable query tokens (S tokens that will capture semantic intent)

MLLM

Processes the entire sequence with causal self-attention in one pass, outputting both text responses and query states Q ∈ ℝS × D

Connector fθ

Transforms query states into conditioning vectors c in the format the generator expects (could be a simple MLP, cross-attention adapter, or small transformer)

Generator Gψ

A swappable module (Image DiT, TTS engine, Video DiT) that produces the final output conditioned on c

How does the system know when to generate? The MLLM signals intent through special trigger tokens. When it produces <image>, <speech>, or <video> in its output, the system recognizes this as a handoff signal. It then extracts the query states from that position, passes them through the connector, invokes the matching generator, and returns the final output. You will read more about it in “How Generators Use Conditioning”.

Now that you have the general idea, let’s zoom in on three key aspects: how conditioning interfaces represent intent, how connectors bridge the gap, and how generators use conditioning. Last but not least, we will get hands-on with Qwen-Image-Edit, a modular diffusion editor that puts these ideas into practice.

Conditioning Interfaces: How to Represent Intent

The design of the query space is very important: it shapes what the model can express. Let’s look at three dominant approaches and their trade-offs in Table 10-3.

Table 10-3. Query space design options

Each approach comes with trade-offs:

  • Hidden-state queries offer the most flexibility (the same queries can potentially drive different generator types) but require a learned connector to bridge the gap between the MLLM’s representation space and the generator’s expectations. This means more training data, especially when the two spaces are misaligned.

  • CLIP-aligned latents converge faster because both MLLM and the generator already speak the same “language,” but expressivity is limited to what CLIP can represent.

  • Hybrid approaches get the best of both worlds (sharp text rendering, better prompt adherence) at the cost of training complexity: you need multiple losses, careful balancing, and sometimes reinforcement learning to keep everything aligned.

With the query representation settled, the next piece is the connector that bridges MLLM and the generator. Let’s take a look!

How Generators Use Conditioning

Once queries pass through the connector, how does the generator actually use them? This depends on the modality and generator architecture.

Let’s look at Table 10-5 and Figure 10-11 to discover a pattern:

  • Diffusion-based generators (images, video) use cross-attention to iteratively incorporate conditioning. At each denoising step, the model “looks at” the query vectors to decide how to refine the output spatially and temporally. This is dynamic: the model can weight different parts of the conditioning differently at different steps.

  • Audio/speech generators work differently. The MLLM’s output (text plus control tokens for prosody, emotion, speaker identity) feeds directly into the TTS or audio model as a specification: just “here’s what to produce and how.”

Hands On: Qwen-Image-Edit, a Modular Diffusion Editor

Qwen-Image-Edit is not a pure query-token late-conditioning model (see Figure 10-12) in the strict MetaQueries sense. A more precise description is a modular dual-conditioning diffusion editor: Qwen2.5-VL provides high-level semantic control, a VAE encoder provides appearance-preserving reconstructive control, and a multimodal diffusion transformer (MMDiT) diffusion backbone renders the final edited image. We place it here because it illustrates the same core idea as late-conditioning—separating semantic understanding from rendering, even though the interface is tighter and less swappable than in canonical query-based systems.

Concretely, the architecture works as follows. Qwen2.5-VL is frozen and serves as the condition encoder: its last-layer hidden states constitute the semantic representation of the user’s edit instruction, together with the source image.

Meanwhile, the source image is independently encoded by a VAE into spatial latent representations that preserve low-level appearance details. Both conditioning streams feed into an MMDiT that denoises the output image. The semantic stream tells the model what to change; the VAE stream tells it what to preserve. This dual-conditioning design maps cleanly to the “Hidden-state queries” row in Table 10-3, even though the queries here are frozen VLM hidden states rather than explicit learnable tokens.

Let’s look at the code. The most instructive snippet is not the one-line pipe (image=..., prompt=...) call but the point at which we explicitly separate understanding from rendering. Note that in the following example we keep only the core parts; the full runnable notebook, setup steps, and helper utilities live in the notebook:

from diffusers import QwenImageEditPipeline
from PIL import Image
import torch
import requests

# In the book we only keep the core mechanism.

url = "https://huggingface.co/datasets/vlmbook/images/resolve/main/panda.png"
img = Image.open(BytesIO(requests.get(url).content)).convert("RGB")
img = img.resize((1024, 1024))

"Qwen/Qwen-Image-Edit",
torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
).to("cuda")

instruction = "add vegetation to the landscape"

# Step 1: make the conditioning interface explicit.
# Instead of hiding everything inside pipe(...), we first ask the model
# to convert (instruction + source image) into conditioning embeddings.
prompt_embeds, prompt_embeds_mask = pipe.encode_prompt(
prompt=instruction,
image=img,
)

# Small API detail: when we pass embeddings manually, we also make the
# attention mask explicit if the pipeline returned None.
if prompt_embeds_mask is None:
prompt_embeds_mask = torch.ones(
prompt_embeds.shape[:2],
dtype=torch.long,
device=prompt_embeds.device,
)

# Step 2: hand those embeddings to the renderer.

out = pipe(

prompt=None,
prompt_embeds=prompt_embeds,
prompt_embeds_mask=prompt_embeds_mask,
true_cfg_scale=4.0,
negative_prompt=" ",  # keeps the CFG path enabled
num_inference_steps=40,

out.images[0]

If you run the code, you will see the impact of adding some vegetation to this rally picture (Figure 10-13).

Notice how this architecture embodies the late-conditioning principle in practice: Qwen2.5-VL handles semantic understanding, while the VAE + MMDiT stack handles rendering. The MLLM never edits pixels directly; it provides conditioning that the generator uses to produce an image. Qwen-Image-Edit is not fully modular in the same way as canonical query-token systems (the conditioning interface and diffusion backbone are codesigned for editing), but it is still one of the clearest production examples of “decouple understanding from generation” available today.

This modularity is late conditioning’s superpower: upgrade or swap components independently. Need better image quality? Replace the diffusion model. Need lower latency speech? Switch TTS engines. The MLLM’s semantic understanding remains intact throughout.

Training

You have seen three ways to architect an any-to-any model. Now comes the hard part: actually training one without it collapsing. Training any-to-any models means balancing multiple tasks, modalities, and loss functions that actively compete for model capacity. Get the balance wrong, and your model excels at text while failing on images, or it generates great videos but forgets how to reason.

In this section, we will explore different training strategies and get our hands dirty with some SFT code examples.

Task Balance and Data Mixing

As you learned in Chapter 4 (“Dataset Mixtures: The Hidden Hyperparameter”), dataset mixtures can make or break model performance. This becomes even more critical for unified models, where you are mixing:

Perception-to-text tasks

Image/video captioning, visual QA, audio transcription

Text-to-generation tasks

Text-to-image, text-to-video, text-to-speech

Editing tasks

Image modification, style transfer, inpainting

Different tasks have different characteristics: a captioning sample might be 256 image tokens input and 20 text tokens output. A text conversation could be 200+ tokens. Video samples can dominate batches purely through token count. If 90% of your data is text-only, the model will favor text and underperform on multimodal tasks.

The same principles from Chapter 4 apply here: deliberately balance your mixture. Chameleon, for instance, explicitly controlled ratios of text, code, and visual tokens throughout training (2.9T text-only tokens, 1.5T from image-text pairs, plus 400B interleaved tokens). This deliberate balancing ensured the model learned to switch smoothly between modalities rather than specializing in whichever dominated the batch.

In practice, two balancing strategies work together:

Data-side balancing

This controls what the model sees. Oversample underrepresented tasks, cap very long sequences (for example, limit video to 1,024 tokens), and ensure your batch composition reflects target capabilities. This is the same principle from “Mixture Ingredients and Proportions”, which discusses avoiding catastrophic forgetting: maintain general-purpose data even when training on specialized tasks.

Loss-side balancing

This controls how gradients combine. Weight each task’s loss contribution (start equal, then adjust based on validation). Methods such as GradNorm (equalizing gradient magnitudes) or uncertainty weighting (learning-task-dependent noise) can automate this.

Staged Training: Divide and Conquer

Training everything from scratch simultaneously often fails. Tasks compete for capacity: one modality’s signal drowns out others, and you get catastrophic interference. Instead, practitioners train in blocks, but which blocks first? Three patterns dominate.

Connector-first (alignment before joint training)

Freeze all backbones (LLM, vision encoder, audio encoder) and train only the lightweight adapters connecting them. This ensures modalities “speak a common language” before subjecting everything to mixed gradients.

An example is when adding vision to a pretrained LLM, you freeze both the LLM and the vision encoder (e.g., SigLIP or CLIP), and train only a small MLP that projects visual features into the LLM’s input space.

This approach is interesting because the LLM doesn’t degrade as it learns to interpret the new modality.

First understanding and then generation (sequential training)

First, fine-tune the MLLM for understanding tasks (e.g., VQA, captioning). Once stable, freeze it and train a generator conditioned on its outputs.

BLIP3-o exemplifies this approach perfectly: they train understanding capabilities in Phase 1, then freeze the MLLM entirely and train a 1.4B diffusion transformer for image generation in Phase 2. Figure 10-14 illustrates this: the left shows joint optimization (problematic), while the right shows the two-stage curriculum that BLIP3-o uses successfully.

The idea behind this approach is that understanding (cross-entropy on text) and generation (diffusion MSE on latent variables) pull parameters in opposite directions. Sequential training isolates them, preserving understanding while enabling generation.

Gradual unmasking (for discrete models)

Start with nontext tokens masked or heavily downweighted. Over the first few thousand steps, gradually increase their loss weight. Let the model stabilize text capabilities before tackling full mixed-modal objectives.

Intuitively, we can see that early discrete visual tokens are random noise, and their large vocabularies can destabilize the embedding space. Warm-up gives time for the text structure to solidify.

Staged training example: Qwen2.5-Omni

Qwen2.5-Omni (7B) illustrates how these principles come together in practice. The team initialized from strong pretrained components: Qwen2.5 as the base LLM, Qwen2.5-VL for vision, and Whisper for audio. Rather than training everything jointly from the start, they adopted end-to-end Thinker-Talker training with careful staging.

The Thinker (understanding) uses block-wise audio/visual encoders for streaming inputs with TMRoPE (Temporal Multi-resolution Rotary Position Embedding) for audiovisual alignment. The Talker (speech generation head) went through three training phases: speech continuation (basic token prediction), RL-based stability tuning (reducing artifacts), and multispeaker style tuning (adding expressiveness).

The team optionally freezes the Thinker when specializing the Talker, embodying the “understanding first, then generation” pattern. This careful staging allows them to maintain language performance comparable to single-modality Qwen baselines while adding multimodal capabilities, demonstrating that staged training prevents capability regression.

Architecture Specific Loss Functions

This is a brief but very important section. Each of the three families we’ve explored requires different training objectives. Understanding these differences matters because choosing the wrong loss function or mixing incompatible losses through shared parameters is one of the fastest ways to break your training.

The core question here is: how do you supervise a model that must both predict discrete text tokens and generate continuous images or audio? Each family answers this differently, with distinct trade-offs in training complexity, gradient dynamics, and final performance. Let’s look at Table 10-6.

Table 10-6. Losses overview

Hybrid multiobjective

You will see how to freeze the MLLM backbone after understanding training and train only the diffusion generator and connector on image-text pairs.

Both scripts handle the tricky parts: proper loss weighting, staged freezing/unfreezing, multimodal data loading, and checkpoint management. They’re designed so you can adapt them to your own datasets and use cases, and we recommend following the comments in the code.

Cheat sheet for training

When it comes to training, keep the following advice in mind:

  1. Start from pretrained components: Don’t train from scratch. Use strong LLMs (Qwen, Llama), pretrained vision encoders (SigLIP, CLIP), and audio encoders (Whisper). Qwen2.5-Omni’s success came partly from initializing with already-capable components.

  2. Use staged training: Connector-first, then understanding, then generation. Each stage stabilizes before adding complexity.

  3. Balance your mixture: Refer to the mixture design principles in Chapter 4. Keep general-purpose data throughout (avoid catastrophic forgetting), balance task proportions, and use ablations on smaller models to validate choices (see the discussion of scaling consistency in “Ablations and Evaluation”).

  4. Monitor multiple metrics simultaneously: Track understanding (VQA accuracy), generation (FID, CLIP score), and language preservation (MMLU, perplexity). If any metric regresses while others improve, your mixture or staging needs adjustment.

  5. Use task-aware learning rates: Lower LR for frozen/stable components (10x lower for backbone), higher for new adapters/connectors.

    Summary

    You started this chapter asking a simple question: what if you want to build an assistant that can see, hear, and speak? With what you knew before, the answer was “stitch together four models and pray the glue holds.” Now you know there’s a better way. Three ways, actually, and the choice between them depends on what you are willing to sacrifice.

    The fundamental puzzle is always the same: transformers speak tokens, but the world speaks continuous signals. Images don’t naturally chop into a vocabulary of 8,192 options. Audio isn’t a sequence of discrete syllables. Video is just an obscene number of both. How you resolve that tension determines which architectural family you end up in.

    Fully autoregressive models say, “force everything into tokens.” Vector quantize your images, expand your vocabulary, and let the same next-token prediction machinery handle everything. Elegant. Fast. And limited by the fact that a codebook of 8,192 entries is a terrible way to describe a photograph. Factorized heads get smarter about this: the LLM stops predicting pixels entirely and instead triggers specialized generation heads that can use RVQ, stacking multiple small codebooks for combinations no single codebook could handle.

    Hybrid multiobjective models ask a different question: why tokenize at all? Keep text discrete (LLMs are great at that), but let images live as continuous latent representations and refine them via diffusion. You get 20 to 50 denoising steps instead of one forward pass, but the quality difference is real. The catch is that your transformer now needs to speak two languages simultaneously—cross-entropy for text, MSE for denoising—and you cannot let those gradients fight each other through shared output parameters.

    On the other extreme, you have late-conditioning models: don’t build one tightly integrated system. Build two great systems and train a lightweight connector between them. The MLLM handles semantics, the generator handles rendering, and if a better diffusion model drops next month, you swap it in without touching anything else. The trade-off is that the interface itself can become the bottleneck: fine-grained alignment between input and output suffers when the two sides communicate only through a bottleneck of query vectors.

    Training any of these without catastrophic failure requires the same staged discipline: connector-first (alignment before joint training), understanding before generation, and always keep general-purpose data in your mixture. The BLIP3-o result is worth remembering: joint training of understanding and generation on shared parameters degraded both. Contrastingly, sequential training with isolated parameters achieved state of the art on both. The architecture doesn’t save you from a bad training strategy.

    Next up: Chapter 11 covers agents and vision-language-action (VLA) models, where the outputs aren’t images or speech but actions. The any-to-any intuitions that you gained can now transfer directly.
